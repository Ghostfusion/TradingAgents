# Risk measures, tails and drawdowns: the tail measures a book budget actually wants are exactly the ones whose estimates are most fragile, so every published tail must carry its sample, its coverage and its quality

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads eight papers from a local arXiv harvest that the category census matched to risk measures, tails and drawdowns: 23 records spanning 2009-2026, two of them with a journal reference, all 23 screened and eight read in depth at the PDF. It is written for the research and analysis layer of TradingAgents - the offline calculators under `tradingagents/strategies/`, the agent tools that expose them, the risk debaters and the score engines - and it asks one question throughout: what should the engine publish as a tail number, and what should it refuse to publish.

## What this covers

The arc runs from what a capital-acceptance rule is allowed to be, through how a tail number behaves when the sample is short and the tail is heavy, to what drawdown and skewness do that variance does not. The single most consequential finding for this repo is that the measures a book-risk budget would like to enforce - expected shortfall, a conditional co-risk measure, a drawdown envelope - are precisely the ones whose finite-sample estimate is biased downward, whose one-outlier sensitivity is unbounded, and which cannot be backtested by violation counts, while the natural axioms that protect liability holders still select value-at-risk; the actionable response is not to abandon the tail measures but to publish them only with their observation count, a coverage test and a quality band, and to rank selection by drawdown and skew rather than by volatility.

Selection rule: the paper must state a page-anchored, quantitative claim about estimating, ranking with, or enforcing a risk measure - VaR, expected shortfall, a co-risk measure, a drawdown, a tail index, or a stress test - that the research layer can act on; credit-scoring work was kept only where the estimation lesson transfers to market risk.

## The short answer

1. Any surplus-invariant, law-invariant, conic (or numeraire-invariant), truncation-closed acceptance set must be a set induced by value-at-risk, and no non-trivial expected-shortfall rule satisfies those properties (`1707.05596v2`, p.7-8).
2. Tail sensitivity and robustness are a trade-off, not a setting: one observation beyond the quantile can make a historical expected-shortfall estimate arbitrarily large, while the VaR estimate would need more than (1-alpha)n points to move (`2206.02582v2`, p.10).
3. CoVaR and CoES reduce to a univariate risk measure at a quantile omega fixed by the copula, and the ratio delta-CoES/delta-CoVaR depends only on the tail index, which is what makes a tail-index estimator possible (`2206.02582v2`, p.5, p.8).
4. Both estimates are biased downward in finite samples and need at least 2000 observations; at n=500 the bias is -0.559 for delta-CoVaR and -2.099 for delta-CoES, and the variance 3.883 against 15.348 (`2206.02582v2`, p.16).
5. Co-risk and risk-contribution measures are not classically backtestable because they are not elicitable; they need joint or multi-objective elicitability tests, not plain violation counts (`2206.02582v2`, p.14).
6. Maximum drawdown is model-free and encodes path information that VaR and CVaR discard, but it is permutation-dependent, and a drawdown-ranked book can come out less risky in every reported measure (`1403.8125v4`, p.3, p.8-9).
7. Volatility is a weaker determinant of a risk premium than skewness: across six strategy families the Sharpe/skew correlation runs -0.36 to -0.89 while the Sharpe/volatility correlation is negative or zero, and the fitted line is S is approximately 1/3 minus zeta/4 (`1409.7720v3`, p.14-15).
8. Trend following is the outlier: positive skew (+0.43) with a positive excess return, so it behaves as a market anomaly rather than a risk premium and must not be evaluated as one (`1409.7720v3`, p.16).
9. A VaR number inherits the price-probability definition behind it: the frequency-based (constant-volume) probability is not the market-based one, and market price volatility depends on the volatilities of trade values and volumes and their correlation (`2101.08559v3`, p.3, p.7).
10. Rare-event probabilities need an asymmetric link with a tail parameter, and missing values in risk data are informative: weights-of-evidence coding raised the count of significant predictors from 11 to 20 in the UK GEV case (`1412.5351v1`, p.7, p.15, p.24-25).
11. Copula-based systemic measures are dependence-consistent only under the inequality conditioning, and the copula family fixes the number: Clayton is the most conservative of the four tested and the only family for which the vulnerability version is monotone in Kendall's tau (`2203.10777v1`, p.6, p.14).
12. The far-left implied-volatility skew is a market risk measure in disguise: a short-time expansion recovers a positive distortion parameter from S&P 500 out-of-the-money puts, with mu*eta approximately 35 (`1107.4632v3`, p.13-14).

## 1. What an acceptance set is allowed to be

`1707.05596v2` frames the problem as a regulator choosing an acceptance set A for a firm's capital position X, the assets net of liability, where the positive part is shareholder surplus and the negative part is the option to default (`1707.05596v2`, p.2-3). The properties under test are surplus-invariance (the test must not depend on the surplus of shareholders), law-invariance (it can be checked on historical data), conicity or numeraire-invariance, and truncation-closedness (`1707.05596v2`, p.4). Basel II accepts a position when its 99% VaR is non-positive, and Basel III moves the market-risk test to 97.5% expected shortfall from 1 January 2018 (`1707.05596v2`, p.2, p.5). The central result is Theorem 2.1: a surplus-invariant, law-invariant, conic and truncation-closed acceptance set must satisfy A^-alpha subset A subset A^0-alpha, or equal A^+alpha, and if conicity is strengthened to numeraire-invariance it must equal A^-alpha or A^+alpha (`1707.05596v2`, p.7). The sets A^-alpha, A^0-alpha and A^+alpha are exactly the VaR sets, differing only at the boundary event X equals zero (`1707.05596v2`, p.6-7). An acceptance set satisfying those properties cannot be induced by expected shortfall unless it is trivial, because the ES set is not surplus-invariant (`1707.05596v2`, p.8). With convergence-in-probability closedness the choice collapses further to A^+alpha alone (`1707.05596v2`, p.9). The operator consequence is blunt: the axioms that protect liability holders select VaR; choosing expected shortfall is choosing to relax one of them, and the relaxation has to be paid for with a stated reason.

## 2. Tail sensitivity versus robustness, and the price of a short sample

`2206.02582v2` makes the trade-off quantitative. It shows that CoVaR, CoES and MES can be written as a univariate risk measure evaluated at a copula-determined quantile omega: CoVaR is VaR-omega of Y, CoES is ES-omega of Y, and MES is the CoES case with alpha equal to zero (`2206.02582v2`, p.5). Conditioning on X equal to its VaR, the original Adrian-Brunnermeier definition, is not dependence-consistent and is unstable because it conditions on a set of measure zero; conditioning on X at or below its VaR is consistent (`2206.02582v2`, p.3). Under a generalised-Pareto tail beyond VaR-omega, the ratio delta-CoES/delta-CoVaR depends only on the tail index xi, so the ratio is itself an estimator of xi (`2206.02582v2`, p.8). The sensitivity functions make the robustness cost exact: the historical expected-shortfall estimator is linear in the size of a loss beyond the quantile, whereas the CoVaR sensitivity is piecewise constant, so a single data point beyond VaR can make the ES estimate arbitrarily large while the VaR estimate needs more than (1-alpha)n points (`2206.02582v2`, p.10). The finite-sample simulation is the cleanest statement of sample-size requirements in the slice: data are drawn from a Gumbel copula with dependence parameter 2.22 and t-distributed margins with 3 degrees of freedom, giving true delta-CoVaR 5.071827, true delta-CoES 7.383257, omega 0.9974727 and tail index 0.3130637 (`2206.02582v2`, p.13). Across 10,000 datasets of sizes 500 to 20,000, both estimates are biased downward: at n=500 the bias is -0.559 for delta-CoVaR and -2.099 for delta-CoES, falling to -0.011 and -0.045 at n=20,000, with variances 3.883 and 15.348 at n=500 falling to 0.131 and 0.989 (`2206.02582v2`, p.16). The paper's own threshold is at least 2000 observations, and it warns that any published estimate is likely to be too low (`2206.02582v2`, p.16). The outlier experiment confirms the shape: the delta-CoVaR response is piecewise constant while the delta-CoES response is piecewise linear with an intermediate region where the bias grows and a far region where it shrinks, so a sharply falling estimate can signal risk building up rather than risk falling (`2206.02582v2`, p.16-17).

## 3. Estimating co-risk measures and tails on short samples

The backtesting point in `2206.02582v2` is the one most likely to be missed in a research layer: because the co-risk measures have two or three sources of variation (the VaR model, the dependence model, and for ES the ES model), they cannot be backtested in the classical sense, and the only sound checks are the joint-elicitability or multi-objective-elicitability tests (`2206.02582v2`, p.14). A clean violation count is not a backtest of a conditional measure, only of the quantile it conditions on. `2203.10777v1` runs the validation that a plain VaR does admit: on 10,000 simulated observations repeated 100 times, average violation rates for CoVaR, MCoVaR and VCoVaR fall between 0.0489 and 0.0507 against the 5% level and between 0.0082 and 0.0117 against 1% (`2203.10777v1`, p.18). On five cryptocurrencies over 2,283 daily observations from 1 September 2015 to 30 November 2021, covering 64.54% of market capitalisation, realised VaR violation rates run from 4.78% to 6.00% against the 5% target where 114.1 violations were expected, with BTC at 6.00% (137 violations) (`2203.10777v1`, p.19, p.21). The empirical conclusion is a caution: every conditional measure sits below the corresponding univariate VaR, reflecting the positive dependence in the sample, and BTC and LTC are the pair that most affect each other (`2203.10777v1`, p.21-22). `1412.5351v1` supplies the rare-event estimation lesson from the credit side. Its generalised-extreme-value link function replaces the symmetric logistic link, with a tail parameter tau that recovers the Gumbel distribution as tau tends to zero (`1412.5351v1`, p.7). The data are 39,785 UK and 154,934 Italian small and medium enterprises observed in 2010 and resolved in 2011, split 70% training and 30% control (`1412.5351v1`, p.10, p.13). Out of sample with weights-of-evidence coding, the UK GEV model gives MAE+ 0.784, MSE+ 0.722 and AUC 0.741 against logistic 0.798, 0.705 and 0.731, and the non-parametric BGEVA gives 0.782, 0.702 and 0.722; for Italy BGEVA reaches MAE+ 0.781, MSE+ 0.651 and AUC 0.824 against GEV AUC 0.813 (`1412.5351v1`, p.24-25). The fitted tail parameters are -0.41 for Italy and -0.9 for the UK (`1412.5351v1`, p.25). Weights-of-evidence coding beat multiple imputation for most models, and for the UK GEV and BGEVA models it raised the count of significant predictors from 11 to 20, evidence that the missing values were not missing at random (`1412.5351v1`, p.15).

## 4. Drawdown as a measure and a ranking rule

`1403.8125v4` treats maximum drawdown as the worst peak-to-trough loss and argues it is more insightful than VaR or CVaR because it is directly computable from a return series, model-free, and sensitive to the order of returns in a way a distribution is not (`1403.8125v4`, p.3). That last property cuts both ways: a random permutation of a time series produces a different maximum drawdown, so it is path-dependent by construction (`1403.8125v4`, p.3). The empirical work uses KOSPI 200 from January 2003 to December 2012, SPDR US sector ETFs from January 1999 to December 2012, and the S&P 500 from January 1993 to December 2012, with six-month estimation windows for monthly momentum and six-week windows for weekly contrarian portfolios, ten ranking groups, overlapping portfolios, and a risk model of ARMA(1,1)-GARCH(1,1) with classical tempered stable innovations (`1403.8125v4`, p.6-7). The results show drawdown and recovery are usable ranking criteria, not only reporting statistics. In the KOSPI 200 weekly contrarian test the recovery-ranked long-short portfolio returns 0.1455% per week at 1.7567% volatility against the benchmark cumulative-return portfolio's 0.0731% at 2.8417%, and the recovery portfolio shows the smallest daily VaR and CVaR in the table, 1.149% and 1.391% (`1403.8125v4`, p.8-9). In the KOSPI 200 monthly momentum test the cumulative-return-minus-drawdown portfolio returns 1.4330% at 7.0357% against the benchmark's 1.3305% at 6.8258% (`1403.8125v4`, p.11). In the ETF monthly test the composite drawdown-recovery portfolio returns 0.1724% at 3.5652% against the benchmark's 0.1174% at 3.5522% (`1403.8125v4`, p.16). In the S&P 500 weekly test the recovery portfolio's maximum drawdown is 43.99% against the benchmark's 72.56%, roughly 40% lower (`1403.8125v4`, p.18). In the S&P 500 monthly test the cumulative-return-minus-drawdown portfolio returns 0.498% at 7.303% volatility against the benchmark's 0.480% at 6.835% (`1403.8125v4`, p.19). The warning is that the drawdown-ranked portfolios are not uniformly safer: in several weekly tests the maximum-drawdown ranking rules were worse in every risk measure, so drawdown is a usable selection axis only when it is compounded with recovery rather than used raw.

## 5. Skewness rather than volatility is the priced tail

`1409.7720v3` makes the case that risk premia are compensated for skewness, not volatility, across a wide universe. The equity risk premium averages about 5% with a cross-sectional dispersion of +/-3% and a global t-statistic of 4.2, and the Sharpe ratio is negatively correlated with index volatility at rho approximately -0.27 (`1409.7720v3`, p.4). In the fixed-income grade table the volatility of the indices is remarkably constant at around 4.8% annualised across grades, except the lowest grade at 7.3%, while the skewness drops sharply between BBB and BB (`1409.7720v3`, p.11). For the FX carry deciles the skewness/Sharpe correlation is -0.76 while the volatility/Sharpe correlation is +0.78, and the fully diversified carry strategy has S approximately 0.85 at skewness -0.94 (`1409.7720v3`, p.12). The short var-swap strategies are the paradigmatic case, with a total Sharpe ratio of 1.26 and skewness -4.61, and S&P 500 at 1.47 and -4.64 (`1409.7720v3`, p.13). Table 7 gives the correlation summary: skewness/Sharpe is -0.36 for bonds, -0.38 for international indices, -0.89 for size, -0.85 for momentum, -0.76 for FX carry, but +0.64 for value, +0.23 for low volatility and +0.58 for trend (`1409.7720v3`, p.14). Almost every strategy falls on the fitted line S is approximately 1/3 minus zeta/4, which the paper offers as an objective criterion for judging a risk-premium strategy (`1409.7720v3`, p.15). The exception is decisive: trend following has a positive skewness of +0.43 (monthly +1.72) with a positive excess return, and value and low volatility are milder exceptions with skew +0.27 and +0.18, so these are genuine anomalies or pure alpha rather than risk premia (`1409.7720v3`, p.16). The appendix shows why the skewness measure matters: the paper's rank-based skew is finite as long as the tail index exceeds one half, whereas the classical third-moment skewness requires the tail index to exceed three and is therefore undefined for the heaviest tails (`1409.7720v3`, p.20).

## 6. Where the VaR number comes from, and reading the risk measure from the smile

`2101.08559v3` attacks the input rather than the measure. VaR asks how much can be lost at a given cumulative probability and horizon, and the RiskMetrics normal approximation uses 1.65 standard deviations for a 5% one-sided event (`2101.08559v3`, p.3). The frequency-based price probability counts trades at each price and therefore describes the case of constant trade volumes (`2101.08559v3`, p.3). The market-based alternative defines the price of a trade as its value divided by its volume and shows that market-based price volatility depends on the volatility of trade values, the volatility of volumes, and their correlation (`2101.08559v3`, p.5, p.7). To forecast price volatility at a horizon T you must forecast the first and second moments of trade values and volumes at T, and the paper concludes that the projections of price probability will remain limited to Gaussian approximations and that volatility forecasts will stay inaccurate and uncertain (`2101.08559v3`, p.8-9). `1107.4632v3` closes the loop from the other side: the left tail of the implied-volatility skew, coming from out-of-the-money puts, reflects the market's assessment of a large drop, and indifference pricing by dynamic convex risk measures defined through backward stochastic differential equations links the two (`1107.4632v3`, p.1). The short-time expansion shows the zero-order term is the standard no-arbitrage term and the first-order correction carries the risk-measure driver (`1107.4632v3`, p.3, p.13). In the calibration to S&P 500 implied volatilities from options with nine days to maturity on 7 October 2010, fitting the liquid near-the-money puts gives kappa approximately 6.6 and y approximately 0.18, and fitting the far out-of-the-money puts below -0.06 log-moneyness gives mu*eta approximately 35, a positive distortion coefficient (`1107.4632v3`, p.13-14). The numerical study confirms the sign: a larger distortion steepens the smile, while a larger risk-aversion parameter lowers the level and flattens the smile (`1107.4632v3`, p.16).

## Caveats

Several papers here cannot support a trading conclusion on their own. `1707.05596v2` is a pure theorem with no data. `2101.08559v3` is a theoretical account of price probability with no estimator and no calibration. `1107.4632v3` is a theorem plus a single-day, nine-day-to-maturity calibration to one index, so its mu*eta approximately 35 is one point estimate, not a law. `2206.02582v2` reports one CRSP sample from 1970 to 2020, and its tail-index estimator can return negative values when the true index is near zero, as it does in its own network results. `1412.5351v1` is credit-default data for a single year (2010 to 2011), not market returns, so its rare-event and missingness results transfer as estimation lessons only. `1403.8125v4` covers three equity universes over 1993-2012 with no costs, slippage or capacity analysis, and its risk model is fitted on the same overlapping portfolios it evaluates. `2203.10777v1` uses five cryptocurrencies over six years of daily closes, so its dependence estimates are one crypto regime. `1409.7720v3` is an association study across many assets and periods, not a causal model, and its central line is a suggestive fit rather than a valuation theory. `0904.0870v1`, a 2009 review with no data, was screened but not read in depth, so nothing is claimed from it here.

## Papers read in depth

- `1107.4632v3` - From Smile Asymptotics to Market Risk Measures (2011; Math. Finance 25:2, 400-425, 2015). Links the implied-vol tail to a dynamic convex risk measure via a short-time expansion and recovers a positive distortion from S&P 500 puts; one day of data, one index.
- `1403.8125v4` - Maximum drawdown, recovery, and momentum (2014). Shows a drawdown- and recovery-ranked book can be less risky in every reported measure; three equity universes with no costs and same-sample evaluation.
- `1409.7720v3` - Risk Premia: Asymmetric Tail Risks and Excess Returns (2014). Elicits an approximately linear Sharpe-skewness relation and isolates trend following as a positive-skew anomaly; an association study, not a model.
- `1412.5351v1` - A comparative analysis of the UK and Italian small businesses using Generalised Extreme Value models (2014). Rare-event estimation with an asymmetric GEV link and informative missingness; credit data for one year.
- `1707.05596v2` - Surplus-Invariant, Law-Invariant, and Conic Acceptance Sets Must be the Sets Induced by Value-at-Risk (2017). Proves the axiomatic collapse to VaR and the exclusion of ES; a pure theorem with no thresholds.
- `2101.08559v3` - To VaR, or Not to VaR, That is the Question (2021). Argues that the price-probability definition, not the formula, limits VaR; theoretical with no calibration.
- `2203.10777v1` - Vulnerability-CoVaR: Investigating the Crypto-market (2022). Copula estimation of co-risk measures validated by simulation and applied to five coins; one crypto regime of daily closes.
- `2206.02582v2` - Making heads or tails of systemic risk measures (2022). The central paper: copula representation, tail sensitivity, robustness, finite-sample bias and a tail-index estimator; one US equity dataset.
