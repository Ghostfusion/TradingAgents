# Market data quality and integrity: a recorded outcome is the collector's classification, not the event it names, and the research layer must publish that provenance and its degradation next to the number

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads the market-data-quality slice of a local harvest of arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) and adjacent categories: 12 records spanning 2016-2026, of which only one carries a journal reference. It answers the operator's question directly - which data defects are real, how the source of an observation is audited, and what a defect does to a reported number - and it is written for a research and analysis layer that computes deterministic reads from vendor feeds and hands them to LLM analysts, not for an execution engine. The evidence is thin: several of the twelve records are only nominally about market data, one has no local PDF, and the deep-read set of six is small enough that the strongest statement in the slice rests on one measurement audit. Where the sibling library `Strategies/books/03-data-quality.md` already covers imputation, denoising, measurement error and vendor reliability in the wider corpus, this brief defers to it and adds only what this slice contributes: the provenance of a label, the temporal collapse of a model fit on one window, and the concrete TradingAgents surfaces that can carry those checks.

## What this covers

The arc of this slice is that "market data quality" here is a measurement problem, not a feed-plumbing problem: the papers do not study exchange outages or vendor gaps, they study whether a number a researcher holds means what the researcher assumes. The single most consequential finding is that an outcome label can be a property of the data collector rather than of the event it names: in `2607.02823v4` a model that discriminates pump.fun launches well inside its development window (AUROC 0.8594) collapses to chance on a pre-registered two-week holdout (AUROC 0.4642), because the recorded terminal label is a joint function of the platform-side event and the collector's polling design (p.1, p.2). Everything else in the slice is a smaller instance of the same question - is this number the quantity I think it is - asked of an imputed maturity (`2309.17379v1`), a settlement-window print (`2606.31675v1`), an indexed on-chain aggregate (`2301.13009v2`), a sampling frequency (`2501.17490v2`) or a fitted window (`1805.12110v1`).

The TradingAgents reader already refuses to fabricate: `market_data_validation_tools.get_verified_market_snapshot` returns a `NO_DATA_AVAILABLE` degradation instead of guessing, `data_quality.panel_statistic` refuses a statistic over a padded window, and `coverage_window` names the `survivor_only` label a current-constituent universe carries. What this slice adds is the discipline of recording *where a value came from* and *whether it survives a holdout*, because a computed read is only as good as the frame it reads and the tested model that consumes it.

## The short answer

1. A recorded outcome is not the event it names: the pump.fun terminal label is a joint function of the platform-side graduation and the collector's 60-second top-50 polling design, so `TIMEOUT` cannot be read as confirmed non-graduation (`2607.02823v4`, p.2).
2. A model built on such a label can look strong in sample and be worthless out of sample: development AUROC 0.8594 falls to 0.4642 on a 14-day holdout, with the 95% interval [0.4112, 0.5196] containing 0.5000 (`2607.02823v4`, p.1).
3. Calibration, not only discrimination, is the tell: the held-out calibration slope is 0.013 and the intercept +2.816 against pre-registered bands [0.85, 1.15] and [-0.10, 0.10], and two of nine stability gates pass (`2607.02823v4`, p.12).
4. The functional form of a single covariate was not stable: categorical mcap odds ratios rose at every contrast (3.66, 8.88, 8.88) while a natural cubic spline fell at every contrast (0.927, 0.937, 0.933), so a pre-registered direction-agreement gate failed at all three contrasts (`2607.02823v4`, p.11).
5. Missing maturities are first-order in illiquid fixed income: the reference series was complete only 65% of the time, so 35% missingness was injected and six fillers compared (`2309.17379v1`, p.6).
6. Simple imputation beat the sophisticated alternatives on this data: previous-value filling gave the lowest errors across coupon, price and yield, and linear regression was close behind, while missForest failed badly on prices (p-value 0.0009) (`2309.17379v1`, p.9, p.11).
7. Settlement-time prints can be manufactured: after a five-minute Bitcoin contract launched, final-ten-second Binance order flow ran about 50% above the pre-launch level and then reverted within ten seconds (`2606.31675v1`, p.6).
8. The detection signature is the reversal, not the spike: in near-even cycles the price reverted by about a quarter within ten seconds, and 93% of the manipulator's profit came from retail accounts (`2606.31675v1`, p.6, p.7).
9. A vendor's own aggregate can contradict the chain it indexes: the Uniswap v3 subgraph's `PoolDayData` was unusable for total value locked because it disagreed with Ethereum data cross-checked on Etherscan, so the authors rebuilt a proxy from mint and burn events (`2301.13009v2`, p.5).
10. Universe construction is a data-quality decision: filtering 6,000+ pools down to a final 34 required explicit transaction-count, proxy-TVL and interconnectedness thresholds, and six overlapping sub-windows were used to lower survivorship bias (`2301.13009v2`, p.5, p.6).
11. Sampling frequency is a measurement parameter: five-minute returns were chosen explicitly to remove microstructure noise before building realized variance, and the resulting return kurtosis was 64.87 (`2501.17490v2`, p.10, p.11).
12. Liquidity filters change the sample you think you have: only 520 carbon options qualified, options with maturities under one day or over 365 days were excluded, and a supply/demand regression held only for a chosen window, with residuals that stayed high even at higher polynomial order (`2501.17490v2`, p.13, p.19; `1805.12110v1`, p.3).

## 1. The endpoint is a measurement, not the event

`2607.02823v4` is the only paper in the slice that audits where a label comes from. The setting is pump.fun, a Solana memecoin platform where a token "graduates" when its bonding curve crosses a threshold and moves to an AMM pool (p.1). The frozen cohort is 749,816 unique mints observed by a V3 off-chain collector between 2026-05-12 and 2026-06-10, of which 1,597 carry a GRADUATED label and 748,219 a TIMEOUT label (p.3). The collector polls the platform's top-50 newest-coins feed on a 60-second loop and reconstructs terminal states from successive poll responses (p.2). The consequence, stated in the pre-registered disclaimer, is that a mint whose curve fills after scrolling off the top-50 feed is recorded as GRADUATED only if it reappears with `complete === true` before its 24-hour timeout is processed; otherwise the collector records TIMEOUT (p.3, p.4).

This is the cleanest statement in the slice of a defect that survives every downstream cleaning step, because it is not a bad value - it is a correct value of the wrong variable. The author retracts an earlier version of the paper in full because its 24-hour graduation rate, its "3.18x decline", its wallet-intent narratives and its Kaplan-Meier/Cox specifications all depended on reading the collector's classification as the platform-side event (p.2). A supporting measurement is the 42/43 first-invisible finding: mints whose graduation was not first visible in the collector's feed are precisely the ones the ascertainment mechanism can mislabel (p.16). The practical detection move is to audit the collector's source code against the platform's actual event stream and to carry the label's provenance beside the label.

## 2. Temporal generalization and calibration as the data-quality test

The same paper supplies the most useful backtest lesson in the slice. A locked logistic association model fitted on a 15-day development cohort (387,152 mints, 794 GRADUATED) and evaluated on the following 14-day validation cohort (362,664 mints, 803 GRADUATED) achieved development AUROC 0.8594 but validation AUROC 0.4642, with 500 of 500 successful bootstrap replicates below the development figure and the 95% percentile interval [0.4112, 0.5196] containing 0.5000 (p.1, p.3, p.15). Validation calibration deteriorated to a slope of 0.013 and an intercept of +2.816 against the pre-registered bands (p.12). Of nine automated stability-gate evaluations, two passed, six failed and one was not evaluable (p.12). Two covariate associations survived within the development window - `has_twitter` odds ratio 1.44 with 95% CI [1.06, 1.97] and `has_telegram` odds ratio 7.51 with 95% CI [5.71, 9.88] - but the gate failure prohibits extending either to the validation window (p.11, p.14).

Two further details matter for an operator. First, the mcap functional form was not stable: the categorical specification gave odds ratios 3.66, 8.88 and 8.88 across three contrasts, a natural cubic spline gave 0.927, 0.937 and 0.933, and log1p gave 1.083, 1.084 and 1.091, so a pre-registered direction-agreement gate failed at every contrast, with the spline and log1p models flagged suspect for extreme coefficients (p.11). Second, the bootstrap itself was fragile: 183 of 500 development replicates failed with singular-matrix errors, so the reported interval is conditional on 317 successful replicates (p.15). A related stopping rule fired because reconstructed censoring adequacy hit 2.6925% against a pre-registered 2% ceiling (p.15). The paper's own candidate explanations are a regime shift in the feature-outcome relationship, a shift in the collector's observation process, feature drift on the covariates, and under-adjustment for observation-process contamination (p.14).

One small methodological detail is worth copying: the author computes the identity AUROC(Y, -p) = 1 - AUROC(Y, p) on the validation cohort as an internal-consistency check on the score-ordering code path, and notes that a deviation would indicate a numerical problem rather than a modelling one (p.6). That is the cheapest possible guard against a scoring bug masquerading as a data result.

## 3. Manufactured prints and the reversal signature

`2606.31675v1` documents a data defect that a backtest would happily consume as signal. Polymarket launched a five-minute Bitcoin up/down contract on 2026-02-12 (p.3); the contract settles against a Chainlink oracle that aggregates several spot exchanges, and the Binance mid sits about 2.5 basis points from the oracle and finishes on the same side as the resolution about 85% of the time (p.6). After the launch, absolute order flow in the final ten seconds before each close rose about 50% above the pre-launch level, and volatility rose with it (p.6). The spike was concentrated where a push is pivotal: in the roughly 6% of cycles whose contract price implied a near-even outcome just before the close, the near-settlement order-flow jump was about 3.9 times that in the rest (p.6).

The reason this is a data-quality finding and not just a microstructure finding is the detection test. Within ten seconds the price reverted, by about a quarter in the near-even cycles and a tenth in the others (p.6), which is the opposite of what information would do. Cycles in the top decile of final-ten-second net flow relative to their own typical flow - about 1,600 cycles - were classified as manipulated, and they clustered in thin-liquidity hours: 56% overnight and 44% on weekends, against 40% and 27% for normal cycles (p.6). In those cycles a push against the favoured side flipped the winner 65% of the time versus 41% ordinarily (p.7), and even cycles the market priced at a 90-to-100% chance reversed 34% of the time versus 1% when there was no push (p.7). Of the roughly 243,000 wallets that traded the contract, 821 took $8.2 million in the pushed cycles while breaking even otherwise, and 93% of the loss fell on retail accounts, which were on the losing side in 65% of manipulated cycles against 48% of normal ones (p.7).

## 4. Missing maturities and the imputation choice

`2309.17379v1` studies the Burundian sovereign bond market, where auctions are irregular and some maturities are simply absent, so the database is unbalanced in maturity (p.2). The authors state the missingness mechanism as Missing Completely at Random, reasoning that a maturity is missing when nobody bids or no issuance occurs, both driven by treasury needs rather than the value of the missing series (p.5). Their reference dataset had complete maturities only 65% of the time, so they injected 35% missingness into a complete series and then compared six fillers: previous value, next value, k-nearest neighbours, MICE, missForest and linear regression on prior issues of the same maturity (p.5, p.6). Accuracy was scored by mean absolute error between simulated and observed values, repeated to build an error distribution, and then tested for normality with the Shapiro test (p.6).

The result is a caution against assuming that a modern multivariate imputer wins. Filling with the previous value produced the lowest errors for bond prices, coupon rates and yields, and linear regression was a close second (p.9). In the normality tables, previous value reached a coupon p-value of 0.9530 (p.11), linear regression reached a yield p-value of 0.8639 and a price p-value of 0.0666 (p.11, p.12), MICE reached a yield p-value of 0.9425 (p.12), while missForest failed for prices with a p-value of 0.0009 (p.11). The paper concludes that linear regression is preferable because it both approximates normality and predicts, but that previous-value filling is the better choice when there is little information to fit with (p.13). The limit of the evidence is that the missingness is synthetic masking of a short, single-market series, so the ranking is a statement about one illiquid market, not a general law.

## 5. Vendor aggregates that disagree with the ledger

`2301.13009v2` builds a reproducible workflow over Uniswap v3 and reports a defect that is directly transferable to any indexed on-chain feed. The authors state that they could not rely on the subgraph's `PoolDayData` values because of incoherences found when cross-checking against Ethereum blockchain data on Etherscan, so they reconstructed a proxy total value locked from cumulative liquidity mint and burn operations instead (p.5). The universe was filtered in stages: from more than 6,000 pools (p.1) to 696 pools with at least 1,000 transactions and tokens traded in at least three pools (p.5), then to 282 pools with at least one million USD of proxy TVL at some point in the window and for two consecutive observations (p.5), and finally to a union of 177 pools across six windows (p.6), of which 34 were selected for the liquidity-taker analysis (p.9).

Two aspects are worth carrying over. First, the filtering was explicitly designed to lower survivorship and loser bias by using six overlapping sub-windows rather than one long window, since pools appear and disappear (p.6). Second, the paper notes that 4,889 pools were created directly through the Factory contracts and that many contain no relevant liquidity but do not disappear because the blockchain is immutable (p.4) - a reminder that an on-chain universe is a permanently growing archive, not a live venue list. The count of distinct participants over the window was 479,161 liquidity takers, 23,952 liquidity providers, and 13,640 addresses that did both (p.7). The limitation is that the proxy TVL is the authors' own reconstruction, so the paper demonstrates a cross-check discipline rather than a validated vendor field.

The paper also shows how atomic multi-action transactions complicate event attribution: when a single transaction contains two swaps of the form X to Y and Y to Z, the authors treat the intermediate token Y as a bridge between pools and build a directed graph from those co-occurrences to infer fund flow between venues (p.8). Because such actions execute "instantaneously but in order" within one validated transaction (p.8), a data pipeline that reads only the top-level transaction and not the internal log order will misattribute the direction of the flow, the same class of defect as the transaction-reordering risk the paper names as miner extractable value (p.3).

## 6. Sampling frequency, liquidity filters and period dependence

`2501.17490v2` prices carbon allowance options from ICE tick data covering 2015-12-15 to 2020-12-15 (p.9). Two measurement decisions dominate its data section. The first is sampling: the authors build five-minute log-returns because market liquidity was low on many days, and they chose that frequency explicitly to get rid of possible microstructure noise before constructing realized variance (p.10). The resulting panel is 155,238 observations across 1,283 trading days, with prices ranging from under 5 euros to over 30 euros and a five-minute return kurtosis of 64.87 (p.10, p.11). The mean realized variance of 9.6% corresponds to an annualized volatility of 49.5% (p.11). The second is the option sample: 520 options, 263 puts and 257 calls, with a filter that excludes maturities shorter than 1 day or longer than 365 days (p.13, p.19).

The modelling payoff is a decomposition: realized variance was split into continuous and jump components, and the HAR-class auxiliary models that use jumps and negative leverage outperformed plain HAR, with an adjusted R-squared of 0.622 for LHAR-CJ against 0.605 for HAR (p.15). This is a small improvement, and the paper itself notes that the benchmark LHARG-ARJ model priced at least as well in most moneyness and maturity buckets (p.24, p.25). The transferable point is not the model but the discipline: the frequency, the maturity filter and the roll rule were stated as measurement choices, because each one changes what the realized measure means.

`1805.12110v1` is the weakest deep read and is included only for the point it makes about period selection. It regresses a quarterly oil price change rate on the supply-to-demand ratio over the first quarter of 2010 to the second quarter of 2014 (p.3), and reports that the relationship is not stable: the authors state that one cannot simply choose any arbitrary period and impose the regression results to fit all time, and that residuals stayed high even with higher-order polynomials (p.3). Their motivating example is a price move from $45.51 on 13 August 2015 to a four-year low of $38.18 on 25 August 2015 driven by expectation changes rather than realized supply or demand (p.4). The paper is a system-dynamics simulation with a 0.0625-day time step (p.3), it validates only visually against real and potential scenarios (p.5), and it reports no data-quality measurement at all.

## Caveats

Five of the twelve records are only nominally about market data, one (`2608.00858v3`) has no local PDF and was skipped, and one of the six deep reads (`1805.12110v1`) is a system-dynamics simulation with no data-quality measurement, so the deep-read set is effectively five papers and only one of them (`2607.02823v4`) links a defect to an evaluation outcome. Every result here is single-market and single-period: Burundian sovereign bonds over one auction regime, one memecoin platform over 29 days, one prediction-market contract over two months, Uniswap v3 over January-June 2022, ICE carbon futures over Phase 3, and a quarterly oil series over 2010-2014. The slice disagrees with itself on imputation in that the simple methods outperformed the multivariate ones on this data (`2309.17379v1`), which the sibling brief `Strategies/books/03-data-quality.md` already documents; nothing here should be read as contradicting that library, only as adding a provenance-and-holdout lens. No paper in the slice measures exchange-feed outages, sequence gaps, crossed books, corporate-action adjustment error, or the effect of any defect on a live or backtested PnL distribution; the only defect-to-outcome link is through a classification metric, not a trading simulation. The take-away rows point at research-layer surfaces only: the reversal and settlement-window diagnostics have no execution-layer landing in this repo, so they are routed to the closest research-layer guard (the validated snapshot and the cross-vendor caliber check) rather than to a matching engine that does not exist here.

## Papers read in depth

- `2607.02823v4` - Auditing Collector-Generated Graduation Labels on Pump.fun: Measurement Error and Temporal Non-Generalization (2026). Audits an outcome label against the collector's own source and reports a pre-registered temporal-generalization failure; limited to 29 days on one platform and a bespoke collector.
- `2606.31675v1` - Settlement Manipulation in Prediction Markets (2026). Shows that settlement-window spot prints are manufactured and detectable by their reversal; limited to two months of one Polymarket contract and one exchange.
- `2309.17379v1` - Handling missing data in Burundian sovereign bond market (2023). Compares six imputation methods on a 35%-missing series and finds the simplest competitive; synthetic masking and a single illiquid market limit it.
- `2301.13009v2` - DeFi: data-driven characterisation of Uniswap v3 ecosystem & an ideal crypto law for liquidity pools (2022). Reports that an indexed vendor aggregate disagreed with the blockchain and builds a filtered pool universe; the replacement TVL is the authors' own reconstruction.
- `2501.17490v2` - Pricing Carbon Allowance Options on Futures: Insights from High-Frequency Data (2025). States sampling frequency, maturity filters and roll rules as measurement choices and decomposes realized variance; the pricing gain over the benchmark is marginal.
- `1805.12110v1` - A Data-Driven Approach for Modeling Stochasticity in Oil Market (2018). Makes a period-dependence point about fitted relationships; it is a simulation with visual validation and no data-quality measurement.
