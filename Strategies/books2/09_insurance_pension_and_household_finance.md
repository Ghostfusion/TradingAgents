# Insurance, pensions and household finance: the household demand side is lumpy, and the products that serve it are priced by fees, taxes and mortality rather than by the market

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from the insurance, pension and household-finance slice of the local arXiv q-fin harvest (115 records, 2002-2026, 32 with a journal reference; 40 candidates screened). It is written for TradingAgents, a research and analysis layer: deterministic calculators under `tradingagents/strategies/`, wired as LangChain tools for the analyst, researcher, risk-debate and manager agents, gated by `enable_*` keys and recorded in the score registry and the decision contract. It does not write an execution engine, a matching engine or an order gateway. The question it asks is narrow: what does this literature change about what the research layer computes, and what does it leave untouched because the result is a product-design or demographic fact with no deterministic landing?

## What this covers

The slice runs from the design of retirement-income products (tontines, life annuities, guaranteed-withdrawal variable annuities, term life insurance) through public-pension means tests to the measured behaviour of retail portfolios, household leverage and consumer credit, and it closes with the microfoundation of inflation-expectation formation. Two findings organise the whole slice for this repository. The first is that the demand side is concentrated: the median Finnish household stock portfolio holds an effective two stocks or fewer, and has never been more diversified than that over 2001-2021 (`2503.17778v1`, p.16), even while the single-stock share fell from 52.9% to 31.3% (p.8). The second is that retirement-income demand is switched, not smoothly priced: a retiree with high longevity-risk aversion abandons a fairly priced life annuity for a tontine once the insurer's one-time loading passes about 7.5% (`1610.10078v1`, pp.16-17).

The practical consequence for a research layer is a filtering one. The measured household and credit papers (`2503.17778v1`, `2407.01539v1`, `2211.09176v3`, `2009.11557v1`) supply tables, thresholds and state variables that a deterministic calculator or score engine can carry directly. The product-design papers (`1610.10078v1`, `1610.09384v1`, `1307.2824v1`, `1102.2263v1`, `1901.11296v2`) mostly do not: their optimal controls and optimal-policy surfaces cannot be re-derived by a lightweight, offline calculator, and they are recorded here as no-landing rather than dressed up as signals.

## The short answer

1. Retail concentration is extreme and persistent: the median Finnish household portfolio's Herfindahl-Hirschman index never falls below 0.5 over 2001-2021, i.e. an effective two stocks or fewer, while the single-stock share falls from 52.9% to 31.3%; `2503.17778v1`.
2. That concentration does not aggregate: the all-share market HHI falls from about 0.4 to 0.1 over roughly ten years and then hovers near 0.06, and the aggregated household portfolio is less concentrated than the median household because stock selection is idiosyncratic; `2503.17778v1`.
3. The annuity-to-tontine switch is a discrete threshold: a retiree with longevity-risk aversion 9 prefers a pool of about 20 to a life annuity once the one-time loading exceeds 7.5%, and a low-risk-aversion retiree (gamma = 0.5) accepts only 0.30 bp of loading at a pool of 5,000; `1610.10078v1`.
4. The "natural" tontine whose payout is proportional to survival probabilities is exactly optimal under logarithmic utility and loses at most about 1% of certainty-equivalent wealth versus the individually optimal design at age 80, gamma = 2; `1610.10078v1`.
5. A pure lifetime-income tontine cannot be fair to every cohort: at least one cohort must have expected present value per dollar below 1, and the equitable share price is non-linear in the amount invested (a 55-year-old at $200 a share against a 75-year-old at $40); `1610.09384v1`.
6. Deterministic drawdown rules are dominated: a trained state-dependent policy beats all six rules on all 100,000 test paths after fewer than 1,000 iterations (about ten minutes), and a 4% rule realises lifetime utility of -1.29e6 against -5.62e4 for the policy on the same path; `2007.09911v2`.
7. Means-tested public pensions break proportionality: the January 2017 rebalancing doubles the Australian asset-test taper from 0.039 to 0.078 per dollar, the income test overtakes the asset test near A$508,066 for a single non-homeowner, and the unconstrained optimum for a 65-year-old couple with A$500,000 is about 11.1% against a 5% regulatory minimum; `1611.08330v1`.
8. The option to buy life insurance makes the optimal risky portfolio more conservative, not more aggressive (Corollary 4.13), because insurance substitutes for self-insurance; optimal insurance demand falls with wealth and can be negative, so a wealthy wage earner optimally shorts his own life; `1102.2263v1`.
9. Guaranteed-withdrawal value is set by tax and rate assumptions: a GMWB fair break-even fee is 43.18 bp with tax against 69.35 bp without, a 3% premium tax needs a gross premium of 103.09 to deliver a net 100, and the policyholder's subjective value ranges 110.13-115.91; `1901.11296v2`.
10. Household leverage is a slow regime variable that locally amplifies booms and busts: one standard deviation of six-year private-label mortgage growth explains 59.19% of the 1999-2005 leverage boom and 97.93% of the 2008-2014 bust, and 168.59% / 192.06% of the house-price moves, against a government-sponsored-enterprise placebo first stage of F = 0.773; `2407.01539v1`.
11. Consumer credit risk converges with loan age rather than credit band: deep-subprime loans season to subprime risk in 36 months and to super-prime in 52, subprime to prime in 42, and band default rates run 52% / 37% / 21% / 10% / 4% for an overall 37%; `2211.09176v3`.
12. Inflation-expectation news is asymmetric and its reach halved after 1983: the share of households hearing inflation news fell from 11.33% to 5.61%, higher-inflation news moved expectations by 0.70% against -0.49% for lower-inflation news in the first sub-sample and 0.46% against -0.22% in the second; `2009.11557v1`.

## 1. Household portfolios: concentrated individually, diversified in aggregate

`2503.17778v1` uses the Euroclear Finland register, the only Western ownership register kept for each legal entity, so it is the one place in this slice where household portfolios are measured rather than modelled. Household portfolios grew from 592,465 in April 2001 to 776,621 in December 2021 (p.7). The distribution of the Herfindahl-Hirschman index spans almost the entire admissible interval and carries prominent spikes at HHI = 1, 0.5, 1/3 and 0.25, the signatures of one-, two-, three- and four-stock equal-weight portfolios (p.8). The single-stock fraction fell from 52.9% to 31.3% (p.8) and the mean HHI fell from about 0.8 to about 0.6 (p.8), but the median HHI never goes below 0.5, so more than half of all household portfolios hold an effective two stocks or fewer (p.16).

The aggregation result is the one a market participant most needs. The market portfolio's own HHI fell from about 0.4 to 0.1 over roughly ten years and then settled near 0.06 (p.16), and the aggregated portfolio of all Finnish households is less concentrated than the aggregate of institutional or foreign investors (pp.10-11). The mechanism is idiosyncratic stock selection: quartile-by-quartile, the low concentration of aggregated portfolios survives within every wealth quartile, so it is stock choice, not wealth, that does the work (p.15). Wealth is nonetheless concentrated, with the top 1% of households owning 55.5% of household stock value in 2001 and 47.9% in 2021 (p.9), and women's portfolios are systematically more concentrated than men's at both the individual and the aggregate level (p.16). The reading for this repository: individual and aggregate concentration are two different numbers and neither substitutes for the other.

## 2. Retirement income: tontines, annuities and the loading that flips the choice

`1610.10078v1` starts from a 1,000-retiree pool of $1,000 each in a 3% perpetual bond: $30 each while all survive (p.2), $37.50 when 800 remain (p.2), and $1,000 each when only 30 remain (p.3). The traditional tontine is therefore a lottery on longevity, and the paper solves the Euler-Lagrange condition for the payout function that maximises lifetime utility subject to a no-loss budget (p.10). The natural tontine, whose payout is proportional to survival probabilities, is exactly optimal for logarithmic utility (p.12) and near-optimal otherwise; the certainty-equivalent cost of using it instead of the bespoke optimum is about 1% at age 80 for gamma = 2 and far less elsewhere (p.39).

The economically useful output is the switch point. A retiree with longevity-risk aversion 9 will prefer a tontine of pool size at least 20 to a life annuity whenever the one-time loading exceeds 7.5%, while at gamma = 0.5 the acceptable loading collapses from 72.6 bp at a pool of 20 to 0.30 bp at a pool of 5,000 (p.38). The asymptotic aggregate loading the pool can bear is 0.6593, but pools of 10, 100 and 1,000 deliver only 0.2858, 0.3377 and 0.3671 (p.35), so the bound is approached very slowly with headcount. `1610.09384v1` extends the same machinery to mixed cohorts and proves the awkward part: fairness is impossible, because whatever the participation rates at least one cohort must have expected present value per dollar strictly below 1 (p.12). Cohort pricing is non-linear in the amount invested, so a naive pro-rata allocator is wrong (p.14). `1307.2824v1` supplies the historical anchor: the 1693 English tontine raised only $377,000 of a $1,000,000 target with about 3,750 nominees (p.6), its 10%/7% stream was worth roughly $133 per age-10 nominee at the 6% official rate against almost $185 for the 14% life annuity offered in its place (p.15), and the longest-lived nominee reached age 100 and drew a final dividend of $1,081 (p.7).

## 3. Decumulation and the means-tested safety net

`2007.09911v2` solves the decumulation problem with a deep neural network policy trained on a seven-factor economic scenario generator calibrated to Australian data. For a 67-year-old single male homeowner with A$500,000 in the Balanced (70% growth) allocation, the policy consumes A$51,917 in the first year and holds A$440,506 at age 68 (p.9). On one simulated path the realised lifetime utilities are -5.62e4 for the trained policy, -7.61e4 for the rule of thumb, -2.22e5 for the regulatory minimum and -1.29e6 for a 4% rule (p.9); after fewer than 1,000 training iterations (ten minutes) it beats all six comparison rules on all 100,000 test paths (p.10). Because of the means-tested Age Pension, consumption is not proportional to wealth and lower-wealth retirees draw down at a higher rate (p.13).

`1611.08330v1` measures the reforms directly. As at September 2016 the full Age Pension was A$22,721 (single) and A$34,252 (couple) a year, with an income-test taper of A$0.5 and an asset-test taper of A$0.039 per dollar (p.7); the January 2017 rebalancing doubles the asset-test taper to A$0.078 (p.8). Only 5% of retirees exhaust their allocated pension accounts, so the regulatory minimum withdrawal schedule (4% under 65 rising to 14% at 95 and over; p.9) often binds as a guideline rather than a constraint. The unconstrained optimum for a 65-year-old couple with A$500,000 is about 11.1%, above the 5% minimum (p.10), and the income test overtakes the asset test as the binding constraint near A$508,066 for a single non-homeowner and A$574,242 for a couple (p.12). This is liability-driven allocation with a kinked, policy-defined payoff, and the kinks are the numbers worth carrying.

## 4. Life insurance and guarantee products: what makes a portfolio conservative

`1102.2263v1` adds term life insurance to a Merton-style life-cycle problem with one risk-free and N risky assets driven by an M-dimensional Brownian motion and a random lifetime independent of the market (p.1). The CRRA controls are explicit: the optimal insurance premium p*(t,x) and portfolio theta*(t,x) are closed-form functions of wealth and of human capital b(t), the fair value of future income (p.11, p.13). The two results worth keeping are directional. Insurance demand is decreasing in wealth and can be negative, so late in the career a wealthy wage earner optimally sells a policy on his own life (p.15). And Theorem 4.12 / Corollary 4.13 states that a wage earner with access to life insurance holds a strictly more conservative risky portfolio than the otherwise identical earner without it (pp.18-19). A household hedging a mortality liability withdraws risk from the book; it does not add to it.

`1901.11296v2` prices a guaranteed-minimum-withdrawal-benefit variable annuity in a Black-Scholes Hull-White world by a trinomial tree. US variable-annuity sales exceeded $100 billion in 2019, about half of all annuity sales, and GMWB maturities can run almost 25 years (p.2). The headline is that taxation and rate dynamics roughly halve the fair break-even fee: 43.18 bp with tax against 69.35 bp without at r0 = 0.03 and sigma = 0.16 (p.15), and higher interest-rate volatility raises the cost because of the chance of very low or negative rates (p.15). With a 3% premium tax the insurer needs a gross premium of 103.09 to deliver a net 100, while the policyholder's subjective value ranges 110.13-115.91, so a sale price in between is mutually beneficial (p.16). Taxation also discourages withdrawals: the optimal withdrawal with tax is never larger than without (p.16).

## 5. Credit and leverage: the household balance sheet as a slow state variable

`2407.01539v1` identifies the household leverage cycle with a gravity-model instrument on net export growth. In the 1999-2005 boom, household leverage rose more in high net-export-growth metros than low (0.696 against 0.643; p.9); it kept rising 2005-2008 (0.288 against 0.130; p.10) and then fell sharply 2008-2014 (p.10). The instrument is strong (clustered Kleibergen-Paap F = 11.43; p.24), one standard deviation of six-year private-label mortgage growth explains 59.19% of the leverage boom and 97.93% of the bust (p.24), and 168.59% of the house-price boom and 192.06% of the bust (p.28). The GSE placebo fails the first stage (F = 0.773; p.26). The transferable idea is that credit expansion and contraction are a multi-year regime backdrop against which long-horizon allocation is set; the IV elasticities themselves are regional aggregates, not a signal.

`2211.09176v3` studies 58,118 securitised US auto loans from four ABS trusts (p.9, p.10). Default rates fall monotonically with credit quality, from 52% in the deep-subprime band to 4% in super-prime, for a 37% overall rate (p.14). The central result is convergence: a deep-subprime loan seasons to subprime risk in 36 months and to super-prime in 52, subprime to prime in 42, and near-prime to prime in 13 (p.25). Risk-adjusted lender returns are roughly equalised near 7.5% across deep-subprime through prime, with super-prime lower near 2.5% (p.35), and borrowers forgo $1,153-$2,327 of credit-based refinancing savings through delayed prepayment (p.1). For this repository, loan age is a state variable: hazard curves, not static ratings, describe the cash flows.

## 6. Inflation expectations and the asymmetric news channel

`2009.11557v1` uses 208,777 Michigan Survey records from January 1978 to February 2016 (p.9) and finds a structural break in September 1983 (p.10). Median expected inflation fell from 6% to 3% and the cross-sectional variance from 34.3 to 15.2 (p.11), while the share of households hearing inflation news roughly halved, from 11.33% to 5.61%, and monetary-policy news from 12.47% to 5.96% (p.12). Hearing higher-inflation news raised expectations by 1.03% in the first sub-sample, and contractionary-policy news by 0.49% (p.14). The asymmetry is the durable part: higher-inflation news moved expectations by 0.70% against -0.49% for lower-inflation news in the first sub-sample, and 0.46% against -0.22% in the second (p.21). At the zero lower bound the inflation-news coefficient was larger (1.22; p.25) and monetary-policy news moved consumer sentiment by -7.56 (p.26), so the channel ran through sentiment rather than through the transmission mechanism.

## 7. A negative control: a strategy paper with no reported evidence

`2109.03740v1` is the one paper in the read set that proposes a tradeable strategy, and it is worth stating plainly that it establishes nothing. The "bounce basket" is long heavily shorted but fundamentally sound securities, ranked by a short score built from securities-lending factors (short interest, loan rate, days to cover, loan balance growth, inverse loan availability) with liquidity, beta and rating filters (pp.12-14). The evidence is one hundred hypothetical securities simulated over about 252 trading days (pp.19-20), the variables are seeded geometric Brownian motions, and the paper states that the portfolio performance is not shown because the data are fictitious (p.22). No return, Sharpe ratio or benchmark comparison appears anywhere in it. A factor schema and closed-form weights are implementable, but with nothing to calibrate against, and the paper is recorded here as a negative result rather than a source.

## Caveats

Most of this slice is theory or illustrative calibration, not measured data. The tontine trio (`1610.10078v1`, `1610.09384v1`, `1307.2824v1`) solves models and reports parameter sweeps under one Gompertz mortality basis and one risk-free rate, with stochastic mortality and credit risk explicitly excluded; `1102.2263v1` and `1901.11296v2` are closed-form or lattice models with illustrative calibrations and no market fit, and `1901.11296v2` even reports sigma values in its results tables that are inconsistent with the sigma it lists in its parameter table. The Australian papers (`2007.09911v2`, `1611.08330v1`) are model-only, reuse one calibration and hold deeming rates constant. The measured papers are each single-country and single-period: Finland 2001-2021 (`2503.17778v1`), US metros around 1999-2014 (`2407.01539v1`), US auto ABS over 2017-2019 vintages (`2211.09176v3`) and the US Michigan survey (`2009.11557v1`). `2109.03740v1` is simulation-only with no reported performance and no out-of-sample test. The slice also disagrees with itself in one useful way: individual household portfolios stay concentrated while the market index becomes less so, so the two measurements must be published separately. No paper here links household demand to order flow, fills or costs; none covers FX, futures, crypto or prediction-market participants; and none establishes that any of these behavioural effects is exploitable after costs. Treat the numbers as mechanisms, thresholds and calibration anchors, never as return forecasts.

## Papers read in depth

- `2007.09911v2` - Deep neural network for optimal retirement consumption in defined contribution pension system (2020). State-dependent decumulation beats six deterministic rules on all 100,000 simulated paths and supplies the Age Pension parameter block; evidence is Australian, model-simulated and single-agent.
- `1610.09384v1` - Equitable retirement income tontines: Mixing cohorts without discriminating (2016). Shows fair cohort-mixed tontines are impossible in general and prices shares non-linearly; the design is conjectural and the numerics use one mortality law.
- `1610.10078v1` - Optimal retirement income tontines (2016). Derives the utility-optimal payout, the natural tontine and the annuity-loading switch point; purely theoretical with illustrative pools.
- `1307.2824v1` - Optimal Retirement Tontines for the 21st Century (2013). Reconstructs the 1693 English tontine and the annuity conversion; the historical reconstruction uses a Gompertz mortality proxy and three surviving data snapshots.
- `1102.2263v1` - Optimal Life Insurance Purchase, Consumption and Investment on a financial market with multi-dimensional diffusive terms (2011). Explicit feedback rules and the result that insurance access makes the portfolio conservative; theoretical, no data.
- `1901.11296v2` - Taxation of a GMWB Variable Annuity in a Stochastic Interest Rate Model (2020). Quantifies tax and rate effects on the break-even fee; illustrative calibration, no market fit, and an unreconciled sigma discrepancy.
- `1611.08330v1` - The 2015-2017 policy changes to the means-tests of Australian Age Pension (2016). Models the means-test reform and the housing/asset split; reuses one calibration and fixed deeming rates.
- `2503.17778v1` - Heterogeneity of household stock portfolios in a national market (2025). Full-population Finnish register showing extreme retail concentration and clean aggregation; nominee accounts cannot be split by owner type.
- `2407.01539v1` - Household Leverage Cycle Around the Great Recession (2024). Causal IV evidence that mortgage credit drove the local boom and bust; identifies a cross-sectional incentive, not the aggregate cause.
- `2211.09176v3` - On the Convergence of Credit Risk in Current Consumer Automobile Loans (2024). Seasoning and convergence ages for auto-loan risk bands; ABS-trust data only, with truncation, censoring and a COVID-affected window.
- `2009.11557v1` - A note on the impact of news on US household inflation expectations (2020). Microdata evidence on asymmetric news effects; pooled OLS and reliant on self-reported news exposure.
- `2109.03740v1` - Behavioral Bias Benefits: Beating Benchmarks By Bundling Bouncy Baskets (2021). Gives a factor schema and weights but no reported performance; simulation-only with no out-of-sample test, so it lands as a negative control.
