# Regimes, breakpoints and state switching: in this 2006-2026 harvest the only state model that beat its benchmark out of sample was the one filtered and re-estimated online, and every detector read in hindsight was the wrong instrument for a live gate.

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads eight papers from the arXiv harvest that fall under regimes, breakpoints and state switching: the eighteen records whose titles or abstracts concern regime switching, structural breaks, change-points or latent state, published between 2006 and 2026, three of them with a journal reference. It is written for a research and analysis layer, not an execution engine: the readers are the deterministic calculators in `tradingagents/strategies/`, the analyst tools in `tradingagents/agents/utils/`, the config gates and the score engines, and the question it answers is which statistic to add, which measurement to distrust, and which comparison to fix when a detector is used as an input to a research decision. It deliberately does not repeat the sibling chapter `Strategies/books/07-regime-changepoint.md`, which already covers the filtered-versus-smoothed discipline, the jump-model persistence penalty, Bayesian online change-point detection and the heavy-tailed-emission evidence from a much larger 663-paper sweep; this brief adds the concrete numbers, the negative findings and the corpus-specific papers that chapter does not, and it re-targets every take-away onto this repository's actual research surfaces.

## What this covers

The slice moves in one direction. It opens with retrospective dating of breaks in historical and macroeconomic series (`0710.0745v1`, `0905.3873v1`, `physics/0607197v1`), moves to hidden-state models that describe volatility and return regimes in sample (`2011.03741v2`, `1410.6005v1`), and closes with three systems that put a state model in front of a decision (`2309.00875v3`, `2604.21734v1`, `2609.02900v1`). The method families are five: a Markov-switching autoregression combined with a self-organising map (`0710.0745v1`), a Bayesian non-homogeneous hidden Markov model (`2011.03741v2`), a regime-switching GARCH multi-factor model (`1410.6005v1`), a mean-reverting autoregression modulated by a hidden Markov chain and estimated by a filter-based EM (`2309.00875v3`), and a bivariate Gaussian HMM with a macro covariate (`2604.21734v1`); the other two are a distance-matrix change-of-convention detector (`physics/0607197v1`) and a measurement-channel theory for regime-conditioned betas read from text (`2609.02900v1`).

The single most consequential finding for this repository is a discipline result, not an alpha result: the only paper here that both fitted a regime model and traded it out of sample won with an online filter that re-estimates as data arrives, not with a state label read off a full-sample fit, and it beat a backward-looking rule by 17.6 percentage points of annualized return even after per-instrument transaction costs (`2309.00875v3`, p.21). Everything else in the slice is either an in-sample description with no decision attached or a detector whose authors state outright that it "has not a predictive goal" (`0710.0745v1`, p.7). The repository already enforces the filtered-only construction in `regime.py::hmm_filtered_regime` and documents it; the value this brief adds is the boundary conditions: which detector is legitimate for a live gate, at what frequency a state model is even identified, and what error budget a text-conditioned regime variable must carry.

## The short answer

1. A regime model earns its keep only when it is filtered and re-estimated online: a prediction-interval rule returned 15.18% annualized (Sharpe 1.18) against -2.44% (Sharpe -0.15) for a backward-looking realized-increment rule over one test year of crude-oil futures `2309.00875v3` (p.21).
2. Change-point detection is retrospective by construction: the monetary-history study assumes the whole series is observed, computes the breaks a posteriori, and writes that the method "has not a predictive goal" `0710.0745v1` (p.7).
3. Break dates come as intervals, not triggers: Mexico's four integration breaks are dated 1992:12, 1994:12, 2001:5 and 2005:12, each with a 95% window a few months to about a year wide `0905.3873v1` (p.6-7).
4. A hidden state is mostly a volatility state: the four-state Bitcoin model splits into a high-volatility bear state (daily sd 5.91%) and two bull states (2.49% and 1.69%) whose means are nearly identical `2011.03741v2` (p.11).
5. The margin over a random walk is thin: the best crypto model averages CRPS 1.78 on Bitcoin against 1.98 for the random walk over thirty one-step forecasts `2011.03741v2` (p.8).
6. Filtered and smoothed posteriors are different objects that "should not be confused", and only the filtered one is used for the one-step prediction `2604.21734v1` (p.6).
7. Regime duration must match sampling frequency: the same two-state model persists across consecutive quarters but alternates almost week to week on the same loss series `2604.21734v1` (p.14).
8. A macro covariate helps only where a regime exists: adding VSTOXX cut the weekly two-state exceedance MSE from 1.55 to 0.923 for internal fraud and did nothing for physical damage or system failures `2604.21734v1` (p.15-16).
9. Regime conditioning flips a textbook relationship: the market price of risk is positive and significant only in low-volatility states, and non-significant or negative at extreme high volatility `1410.6005v1` (p.10, p.12).
10. Persistence is state-dependent, so a pooled estimate is biased: lagged-variance coefficients exceed unity in high-volatility states while the low-volatility state sits below unity `1410.6005v1` (p.12).
11. The number of states is not settled by the data: a three-regime model was rejected for instability and the paper calls the choice of regime count "quite subjective" `0710.0745v1` (p.6).
12. Costs decide whether a regime signal is tradeable: the estimated round-trip cost is 53.71 bps for Shanghai crude futures against 5.80 bps for Brent `2309.00875v3` (p.12).

## 1. Change-point detection is retrospective by construction

Three papers search for breaks in a fixed sample, and each states the difference between that and a live detector rather than implying it. The monetary-history study is explicit: the whole series is assumed observed and the change-points are computed a posteriori, so the method "has not a predictive goal" but is aimed at explaining the series by a piecewise-stationary process (`0710.0745v1`, p.7). Applied to the London-Paris-Hamburg gold-silver spread over 1821-1860 it finds seven change-points in the mean and four in both mean and variance, read as three shock episodes - a speculative one from week 21 of 1824, an institutional one from week 45 of 1839, and a technological one from week 46 of 1850 - each absorbed after one to four years (`0710.0745v1`, p.8-9). The economics is convincing; the timing is not available to a reader watching the series arrive.

The Mexico paper makes the same trade explicit in its method: it first estimates a time-varying degree of integration from a conditional CAPM with segmentation, then runs a Bai-Perron mean-shift search with a minimum segment length (`0905.3873v1`, p.5). Its output is four break dates with 95% confidence windows - 1992:12 (1992:7-1993:2), 1994:12 (1994:7-1995:6), 2001:5 (2000:12-2001:7), 2005:12 (2005:7-2006:4) - and the confidence window is the honest part of the answer: a break is located, not signalled (`0905.3873v1`, p.6-7). The integration degree itself averages 57% over January 1988 to February 2008 and is significantly time-varying (Wald 176.10 on three degrees of freedom, p=0.000), so the object being segmented is itself an estimate with an uncertainty band (`0905.3873v1`, p.6).

The lead-lag paper is the most careful about causality. Its thermal optimal path method searches a distance matrix for the best-matching path between two series, and the generalisation here is a mixed distance that takes the minimum of the co-monotonic and anti-monotonic distances so a sign change in the correlation does not break the path search (`physics/0607197v1`, p.4). On a synthetic series with lag 10 for t=1..100, lag 5 with a sign flip for t=101..200 and lead 5 for t=201..300, the mixed distance recovers the path where each single-sign distance fails on one segment, and the best temperature range is around T=0.1 to 0.2 (`physics/0607197v1`, p.7-8, p.13). The reconstructed sign is then smoothed with a Savitzky-Golay filter using only points to the left of each time step, which is what keeps the reading causal (`physics/0607197v1`, p.9).

## 2. Break dates are intervals, and the state count is a choice the data does not settle

Two design decisions dominate every result in the slice and neither is resolved by the likelihood. The first is that a break is an interval. The Mexico paper's four break dates each carry a 95% window; the authors note the dates "are precisely estimated since the corresponding confidence intervals cover a few months before and after" (`0905.3873v1`, p.7). A detector that returns a point date is over-claiming the resolution of its own search, and a consumer that treats that point as a timestamp will date the regime change to the wrong bar. The second is the number of states. The monetary-history paper took a two-regime model because "no three regime model was found with an acceptable stability", and states that "the criteria for selecting the right number of regimes are quite subjective from a statistical point of view" (`0710.0745v1`, p.6). The operational-risk paper makes the same point with numbers: at quarterly and monthly frequency, adding states past three causes excessive regime switching and overfits, with the four-state quarterly internal-fraud MSE of 2.59 not clearly better than models with fewer states once the extra switching is counted (`2604.21734v1`, p.15). The crypto paper selects four states after testing two through five and finding that more than five fit worse out of sample, and its own footnote ties the choice to parsimony rather than to an information criterion alone (`2011.03741v2`, p.2). The only defensible selection rule any of them uses is out-of-sample predictive score, not in-sample likelihood.

## 3. A hidden state is a volatility state, not a direction

When the states are finally described, they are almost always volatility bands. The four-state Bitcoin model assigns 35% of time to a state with average return 0.46% and standard deviation 5.91%, 30% to a state with 0.49% and 2.49%, 24% to 0.47% and 1.69%, and 11% to 0.02% and 0.53% (`2011.03741v2`, p.11). The two "bull" states have nearly identical means and differ in kurtosis, which is a warning that state labels can be splitting second moments rather than first moments. For Ether the process alternates between a high-volatility state (sd 8.90%) and a low-volatility state (2.61%) for about 95% of the sample, and Ripple spends 80% of its time in two states whose standard deviations are 4.46% and 1.77% (`2011.03741v2`, p.11).

The regime-switching GARCH paper reaches the same conclusion from the other direction. Its two states are identified by their variances, with median scaled market variance 8.5023 in state 1 against 19.8916 in state 2, and the authors use that to label state 1 low-volatility and state 2 high-volatility (`1410.6005v1`, p.12). A detector built on these states is therefore a volatility detector, and any strategy that treats the state as a directional forecast is using it for something the fit does not support. The crude-oil paper's two regimes differ in long-run mean and volatility while having comparable mean-reversion speeds, which is consistent: the state changes the level and variance of the spread, not its speed of adjustment (`2309.00875v3`, p.18).

## 4. Filtered versus smoothed, and the online re-estimation premium

The operational-risk paper states the distinction cleanly: the smoothed posterior uses observations from n+1 to N as well as the past, while the filtered posterior uses only past and present, and the two must not be confused (`2604.21734v1`, p.6). This is the single most reusable sentence in the slice. A regime plot drawn from smoothed probabilities will look decisive and will not be reproducible live, and the paper uses smoothed marginals only for parameter estimation and decoding, never for the one-step prediction (`2604.21734v1`, p.6, p.9). The sibling chapter `Strategies/books/07-regime-changepoint.md` already records that this repository enforces the filtered-only recursion in `regime.py::hmm_filter_step` and canonicalises states by mean return; the addition here is the corroboration and the cost of the discipline.

The crude-oil paper is the counter-example that shows what causal discipline buys. It uses a filter-based EM whose recursions update without re-running the whole pass when a new observation arrives, and it re-estimates parameters every ten observations (a batch of two weeks) rather than every step, which stabilises the estimates (`2309.00875v3`, p.6-8). It documents that the filtered parameter estimates are unstable at the start of the sample and move around the onset of the war in Ukraine, coinciding with a volatility spike (`2309.00875v3`, p.18). That instability is the real-time cost of the approach and it is disclosed, not hidden. The reward is that the prediction-interval strategy is the best of five over the test year, and the White Reality Check repeatedly identifies it as the best strategy in the set (`2309.00875v3`, p.21-22). The two lessons for a research layer are that a warm-up prefix of unstable estimates must be labelled and not consumed, and that re-estimation cadence is a parameter the record should carry.

## 5. Frequency and state count are the two knobs that decide the answer

On the same internal-fraud loss series, the two-state model persists across consecutive quarters but alternates almost weekly at weekly frequency, because a homogeneous HMM absorbs high-frequency noise as state switching (`2604.21734v1`, p.14). Adding the VSTOXX covariate makes the weekly states more persistent, which the authors read as the covariate supplying the structure the noise was destroying (`2604.21734v1`, p.14-15). The crude-oil paper makes the opposite frequency choice for a stated reason: it runs the cointegration analysis weekly because daily data are noisier, but runs the filter and the trading on daily data because it wants short-term variation and daily execution (`2309.00875v3`, p.13-14, p.17). The choice of frequency and the choice of state count interact: on the operational-risk data the covariate "can partially substitute for an increase in the number of hidden states by stabilising regime inference at high frequencies" (`2604.21734v1`, p.15). A detector fitted on the finest available bar with an unexamined state count is therefore not a neutral measurement; it is a choice that will over-switch and overfit.

## 6. Regime conditioning flips the sign of a relationship

The most useful structural result in the slice is that pooling regimes can invert a sign. On 724 monthly US excess market returns from March 1953 to June 2013, the linear multi-factor model finds a positive but non-significant market price of risk, the classic "no trade-off" result (`1410.6005v1`, p.9). Splitting into two volatility states recovers a positive and significant price of risk in the low-volatility state across every proxy for the hedging component, while the high-volatility state shows no clear pattern and is negative at extreme volatility (`1410.6005v1`, p.10). The low-volatility state occupies 71% to 77% of the sample, so a quarter of the data was enough to blur the pooled estimate (`1410.6005v1`, p.12). The same paper finds that persistence is state-dependent: lagged-variance coefficients are below unity in low-volatility states but exceed unity in high-volatility states, so a linear GARCH model overestimates persistence in calm periods and underestimates it in turbulent ones (`1410.6005v1`, p.12).

The crypto paper finds the same structure in the cross-section of predictors. Most predictors are not significant in the linear mean equation, yet removing the insignificant ones degrades out-of-sample accuracy, and the state-dependent coefficients change sign across states (`2011.03741v2`, p.10-11). Only VIX and the US Treasury yield have predictive power on all three coins, and the predictors that move the transition probabilities differ by coin, so a single conditioning variable set does not transfer (`2011.03741v2`, p.11). The practical reading is that a regime variable is not a feature to add to a linear model; it is a variable that changes the coefficients of the other features, and those coefficients must be estimated per state.

## 7. A causal sign detector beats a rolling correlation by about two years

The lead-lag paper's contribution for a research layer is not the economic result but the reactivity of the detector. Applied to the historical volatilities of the US inflation rate and the GDP growth rate from 1947 to 2005, the reconstructed sign moves gradually from synchronous to anti-phased around 1954-1958, and the authors report that the traditional rolling cross-correlation "suffers from a serious lack of reactivity, predicting a change of correlation sign two years or so after it actually happened", while the sign signal is robust to the smoothing parameter over NL=5 to 15 (`physics/0607197v1`, p.12). A rolling correlation is a lagging detector by construction because it averages a window that straddles the change; a causal path search that admits both correlation signs is not. The mixed distance is the enabling detail: taking the minimum of the co-monotonic and anti-monotonic distances lets the search follow a sign flip instead of breaking on it (`physics/0607197v1`, p.4, p.7).

## 8. Regime-conditioned betas from text need an error budget and an adaptive blend

The DisclosureBeta paper is a theory preprint with its empirical program pre-registered and its outcome forthcoming, so everything it reports is a bound rather than a measured result, and it must be read at that strength (`2609.02900v1`, p.1, p.7). It formalises a large language model as a noisy measurement channel on a firm's latent risk characteristics, writes the channel noise into the asset-pricing error budget, and shows that for price-history-thin names - an S-1 filer, a recent listing, a firm just past a regime break - the detector misclassification probability and the disclosure noise are unavoidable terms of any estimator that observes only returns, factors, LLM features and a regime estimate (`2609.02900v1`, p.3, p.5-6). Its practitioner statement is an adaptive convex combination of a rolling-window beta and a text-based beta with weight w = V/(V+M), which is never worse than either component and shifts its weight toward text exactly when the price history is short, stale or straddles a detected regime break; the weight itself is published as a diagnostic for how much to trust price history for a name (`2609.02900v1`, p.4-5). It also shows that a text factor can fail a spanning test purely through poor disclosure, with a worked example where a three-read ensemble loses about 4% of its premium to reading noise against about 34% for a single-read pipeline (`2609.02900v1`, p.5-6). For a research layer that already reads disclosures with an LLM, this is the missing accounting: a text-derived regime variable must carry its measurement error and its detector error, and it should be blended with, never substituted for, the price-fitted estimate.

## Caveats

Reading strength is uneven across the eight. Two papers contain no market data at all: the monetary-history study is a 19th-century gold-silver spread exercise (`0710.0745v1`) and the lead-lag paper is a methodological contribution validated mainly on synthetic series plus one macroeconomic application (`physics/0607197v1`), so neither licenses a claim about a modern tradeable instrument. The Mexico paper is a single-market, single-period study of one integration series (`0905.3873v1`). The two papers that do trade are single-market and single-period: crude-oil futures over one test year of 261 daily observations (`2309.00875v3`) and the crypto study over one exchange-rate regime window (`2011.03741v2`), so neither establishes that a regime model works generally - only that it worked once, in-sample descriptions of state characteristics being the weakest of all. The operational-risk paper is a bank-loss study, not a market study, and its labels are regulatory event types rather than returns (`2604.21734v1`); its Gaussian emissions required an inter-quartile-range outlier filter, which the authors acknowledge is a simplification they intend to relax toward heavy-tailed families (`2604.21734v1`, p.12, p.17). The DisclosureBeta paper is a theory preprint with no empirical results yet, its pretested outcome still forthcoming, so every numeric it reports is a bound or a worked example and not a measured out-of-sample performance (`2609.02900v1`, p.1, p.7). The slice is also methodologically overlapping: several papers report in-sample coverage or full-sample fits, and the filtered-versus-smoothed distinction means an in-sample state plot overstates live reproducibility, so any number quoted from a full-sample fit here should be treated as descriptive. Finally, none of the eight carries a research-layer cost model, and the one that does net out costs finds the answer turns on the per-instrument cost, which is not transferable across markets (`2309.00875v3`, p.12).

## Papers read in depth

- `2309.00875v3` - A hidden Markov model for statistical arbitrage in international crude oil futures markets (2023). Filter-based EM on a three-contract cointegration spread, re-estimated every ten observations; the only out-of-sample trading test in the slice, and it wins, but over one test year and three futures contracts only.
- `2604.21734v1` - Modeling dependency between operational risk losses and macroeconomic variables using Hidden Markov Models (2026). The clearest statement of the filtered-versus-smoothed distinction and of the frequency/state-count trade-off; bank-loss data, not market data, and Gaussian emissions that need outlier filtering.
- `1410.6005v1` - The non-linear trade-off between return and risk: a regime-switching multi-factor framework (2014). Shows the risk-return trade-off exists only in low-volatility states and that persistence is state-dependent; US monthly data only, and no trading rule.
- `2011.03741v2` - Exploring the Predictability of Cryptocurrencies via Bayesian Hidden Markov Models (2020). Four-state non-homogeneous HMM beats single-regime benchmarks on density forecasts; states are volatility bands, the margin over a random walk is small, and the states alternate frequently rather than persisting.
- `0710.0745v1` - Mixing Kohonen Algorithm, Markov Switching Model and Detection of Multiple Change-Points: An Application to Monetary History (2007). Retrospective break dating with an explicit acknowledgement that the method has no predictive goal, and the honest statement that the regime count is a subjective choice; 19th-century monetary data, no modern market evidence.
- `0905.3873v1` - Structural Breaks in the Mexico's Integration into the World Stock Market (2009). Four break dates with 95% confidence windows from a Bai-Perron search; one market, one period, and no live detector.
- `physics/0607197v1` - Lead-lag cross-sectional structure and detection of correlated-anticorrelated regime shifts (2006). A causal sign-detection method that beats rolling correlation by about two years on the inflation/output-volatility nexus; validated mainly on synthetic data.
- `2609.02900v1` - DisclosureBeta: A Measurement-Channel Theory for Regime-Conditioned Betas from LLM-Read Risk Disclosures (2026). Gives a text-conditioned regime-variable error budget and a never-worse adaptive blend; theory only, with the empirical program pre-registered and not yet reported.
