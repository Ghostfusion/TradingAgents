# Quantum and other exotic methods: the slice yields no tradable signal, but its measurement failures - unbalanced samples, nominal-confidence calibration, and unlogged optimisation cost - map directly onto this repo's calibration and evaluation surfaces

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers the quantum and exotic-methods slice of a local harvest of the arXiv q-fin.GN (General Finance) category: 55 records spanning 2003-2025, 24 of which carry a journal reference. Forty candidates were screened and eight were read from the PDFs. The audience is the research layer of TradingAgents - the deterministic calculators under tradingagents/strategies/, the @tool wiring, the score engines, and the research decision contract - and the question asked of every paper is blunt: does anything here change what this layer should compute, or how it measures what it already computes? The honest answer is that almost none of it does, and where a paper does land it lands on measurement, never on signal. The pricing and portfolio papers are execution-layer or pure-theory work with no research-layer landing, and this brief says so per item. What transfers is negative and specific: a balanced 200-row toy reported as a 0.98 F1, a covariance matrix that is not positive semidefinite, a search whose cost is never charged against its result - these are exactly the failures this repo's own calibration, conformal and evaluation modules exist to prevent.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from a dense 2010-2015 band of quantum-mechanical price models, through the 2017-2020 wave of quantum-computing-for-finance applications (annealer portfolio selection, a financial-network QUBO), to a 2022-2025 group that either surveys the field honestly or fits real return data with a quantum walk. Its single most consequential result for this repo is negative and self-reported: on a 40-stock and then a 60-stock portfolio problem a D-Wave annealer matched but did not beat classical methods that finished in seconds to minutes, and the authors write that at lower asset levels efficient classical algorithms do better (`2007.01430v1` p.10, p.13; `2008.08669v1` p.15-16). The most useful positive result is also not a signal: two papers show that long-horizon (beyond roughly 100 trading days) return distributions are asymmetric and sometimes bimodal, and that a discrete-time quantum walk reproduces that shape - while a plain two-component Gaussian mixture fits it better still (`2505.13019v2` p.19, p.22).

## The short answer

1. Quantum annealing is a heuristic sampler, not an advantage: the D-Wave 2000Q beat classical random sampling on average but underperformed a genetic algorithm, and the authors state that at lower asset levels efficient classical algorithms do better (`2007.01430v1` p.10, p.13).
2. The 60-stock follow-up found five classical methods plus the annealer all reaching the ideal portfolio in under a minute, so the quantum run adds a seed, not a result (`2008.08669v1` p.15-16).
3. The binding constraint was the QUBO, not the qubits: at 59 assets the linear terms spanned -1 to 0.2 while the quadratic terms spanned only 0.0037 to 0.0045, a dynamic range of roughly 250x, and chain strength above 2.0 produced no valid results at all (`2008.08669v1` p.9, p.11).
4. Covariance matrices built from real returns carried slightly negative eigenvalues of order 1e-8, enough to give a variance-minimising objective a portfolio with negative variance and a Sharpe ratio orders of magnitude too large; the fix is a Cholesky test then eigenvalue flooring (`2007.01430v1` p.11).
5. The survey of the field filters to exactly 13 peer-reviewed implemented finance applications with zero using machine learning, no rigorous end-to-end speed-up claim, and a quoted industry timeline of at least five years to significant impact (`2204.10026v1` p.4, p.7).
6. The quantum speed-ups that would matter here need deep circuits and quantum random access memory, which the same survey calls difficult to construct (`2204.10026v1` p.8).
7. A discrete-time quantum walk reproduces asymmetric long-horizon return distributions with a lower Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic than a Gaussian, but a two-component Gaussian mixture achieves a lower KS statistic and lower mean absolute error on every bimodal case (`2505.13019v2` p.19, p.22).
8. Volatility scaling exponents across the tabulated assets clustered at 0.43-0.54, not the assumed square-root-of-time 0.5, with one asset at 0.34 (`2505.13019v2` p.12).
9. Neither quantum-walk paper contains any forecast, backtest or cost model; the theoretical one states outright that a quantum model does not imply markets are quantum systems (`2403.19502v2` p.2, p.21).
10. Ball mapper is the one exotic method runnable today and it ships its own stability protocol - sweep radii, normalise axes, require the mean of the colouring function to exceed its dispersion, and re-run on permuted points; the paper bootstraps 10,000 times (`2206.03622v1` p.9-10, p.13).
11. The quantum-ML fraud benchmark's headline 0.98 F1 was measured on a purpose-built balanced subset of 200 rows drawn from a 594,643-row dataset whose true fraud rate is 7200/594643, about 1.2 percent; the result says nothing about detection at natural prevalence (`2308.05237v1` p.17, p.21, p.23-24).
12. No paper in this slice shows a quantum algorithm beating a well-tuned classical method on a trading-relevant objective, and none measures profit, turnover or cost.

## 1. Quantum annealing is a stochastic sampler, and the QUBO - not the qubits - is the cost

The two Chicago Quantum papers are the most concrete artefacts in the slice because they ran on real hardware and benchmarked against classical alternatives that actually finished. The 40-stock study selects a subset of US liquid equities, a binary problem with 2^40 or 1.1 trillion possible portfolios, on a D-Wave 2000Q with 2,041 qubits (`2007.01430v1` p.2, p.13). Because the Sharpe ratio is a ratio of a linear form to the square root of a quadratic form, it cannot be handed to the annealer directly; the authors substitute a quadratic proxy, the Chicago Quantum Net Score, and are explicit that it is a proxy whose only tie to Sharpe is a visual proximity to the efficient frontier (`2007.01430v1` p.3, p.6). The result is a tie the paper reports honestly: the annealer beat classical random sampling on average for portfolio sizes 3, 25, 27, 28 and 33 but underperformed the genetic algorithms, and to match one optimal 3-of-40 portfolio by random sampling took roughly 500 million portfolios and hours, while a genetic algorithm seeded with 1,028 random portfolios reached it far faster (`2007.01430v1` p.4, p.10, p.13).

Read as engineering rather than physics, the conclusion is that an annealer is one more stochastic sampler in the same family as simulated annealing, and must be benchmarked as such. The 60-stock follow-up makes this plain. Five classical methods - fat-tailed Monte Carlo, a genetic algorithm, a simulated annealer, a Tabu sampler and the D-Wave hybrid solver - each find the ideal portfolio in under a minute, while the D-Wave run's 21 seconds is the accumulated account time across the whole project rather than a single-run benchmark (`2008.08669v1` p.2, p.15-16). The Tabu sampler returned portfolios worse by a factor of ten or more and the hybrid sampler returned none at all, so the paper's candidate baselines are not all good ones; the fair comparison remains the strongest classical method, not the weakest (`2008.08669v1` p.6-7).

The cost that actually bites is the encoding. At 59 assets the linear terms span -1.0 to 0.2 while the quadratic terms span 0.0037 to 0.0045, a linear-to-quadratic magnitude ratio of roughly 250x to 300x, and the paper itself calls the rescaling problem open (`2008.08669v1` p.9, p.16). Chain strength above 2.0 returned no valid results, and annealing time was only tested up to 200 microseconds of a possible 2,000 (`2008.08669v1` p.11). Embedding 60 assets consumed 1,300 to 1,700 of 2,048 qubits, extrapolating to a ceiling near 72 assets, which contradicts the paper's own hundred-to-five-hundred-stock framing (`2008.08669v1` p.16). The 40-stock paper documents a failure mode with nothing to do with hardware: covariance matrices came out with negative eigenvalues of order 1e-8, a variance-minimising objective could then find a portfolio with negative variance, and the computed Sharpe ratio would be several orders of magnitude too large; their fix is a Cholesky test followed by eigendecomposition with sub-threshold eigenvalues zeroed (`2007.01430v1` p.11). The same paper notes that moving from include-or-exclude to a weight grid of 0.01 steps multiplies the search space by roughly 68 orders of magnitude and reduces tractability to about 32 assets - at which size classical methods suffice (`2007.01430v1` p.12).

## 2. A financial-network crash QUBO: an honest reduction, a toy test, and a finite-size argument against prediction

The crash-cascade paper is the clearest example of a genuine algorithmic contribution that is not yet usable. It starts from the fact that determining the maximum number of institutional failures after a shock to a network of cross-holdings is NP-hard, inherited from Hemenway and Khanna, and quotes a classical runtime of more than the age of the universe - 13.7 billion years - for 20 to 30 institutions (`1810.07690v2` p.1). That scare number has no operation count, machine or model behind it and is not a quantum runtime; it should be read as complexity-class rhetoric, not a benchmark. The actual contribution is an encoding: market values become binary strings, the discontinuous failure term is approximated by a truncated Fourier-Legendre polynomial of order r, and the resulting many-qubit Hamiltonian is reduced to two-qubit interactions with ancilla qubits (`1810.07690v2` p.2-4).

The resource estimate is explicit and unflattering: the logical qubit cost is n(2q+1) for n institutions at q bits of precision, plus an ancilla term of order r times (e n q / r) to the power 2r, which the authors concede grows quickly and hinders practical application (`1810.07690v2` p.4-6). The only numerical experiment is a randomly generated network of 10 institutions and 20 assets with prices from 0 to 100, perturbed by a vector of amplitude up to 80; the crash appears as a phase transition near amplitude 60, with the transition region spanning roughly 55 to 62 (`1810.07690v2` p.5).

The sharpest sentence in the paper is the one that argues against its own title. Applying finite-size scaling, the authors state that the width of this transition region is expected to shrink as the number of institutions grows, making the transition sharper and the prediction of the crash harder (`1810.07690v2` p.5). The quantity one would want to forecast - how close is the system to the cliff - becomes numerically unresolvable exactly in the regime of interest. There is no calibration to any observed failure data, and the paper explicitly scopes itself to the consequence of a given shock, not the probability of a shock (`1810.07690v2` p.2). For a risk engine the honest reading is that this is a small exact stress computation expressible as a QUBO, not a systemic-risk forecasting tool; ten nodes and a hand-built cross-holding matrix are nowhere near a real interbank network.

## 3. The field's own readiness assessment

The structured survey is the most useful paper in the slice precisely because it is not selling anything. It filters the literature to 13 peer-reviewed papers describing a concrete financial application implemented by 2021, and reads them through a four-layer framework of use case, methodology, quantum algorithm and hardware (`2204.10026v1` p.4). Five address portfolio optimisation and three derivative pricing; eight use optimisation, six use Monte Carlo, and none use machine learning (`2204.10026v1` p.7). Four run on quantum annealers and nine on gate-based machines (`2204.10026v1` p.9). The authors write that to their knowledge there is no rigorous end-to-end consideration that paints the full picture, that gate-based optimisation algorithms need large-depth circuits and remain beyond near-term devices, and that quantum machine-learning algorithms impose requirements such as quantum random access memory that could be difficult to construct (`2204.10026v1` p.6, p.8).

The candid details are worth carrying as the prior. Reverse annealing was reported more than 100 times faster than forward annealing with no known classical comparison, and a quantum annealer was said to approach the performance of commercial solvers on Nikkei 225 and S&P 500 examples (`2204.10026v1` p.9-10). Classical approximation techniques for the same constraint-satisfaction problems improved over the same period, so the survey records that the exact quantum advantage remains an open research question (`2204.10026v1` p.7). The industry timeline quoted is at least five years to significant impact, with no estimate of what running the hardware costs (`2204.10026v1` p.4). A research layer's notes should carry that as the default, and treat any vendor claim of quantum advantage as unvalidated until a classical baseline is reported on the same instance.

## 4. Quantum walks as distribution models: real shape, wrong mechanism, beaten by a mixture

This is where the slice turns from physics to statistics, and it turns into a genuine distributional finding with a genuine loss attached. The theoretical paper replaces the Wiener process in geometric Brownian motion with a discrete-time quantum walk whose position variance grows quadratically with the number of steps, a behaviour the authors call ballistic diffusion, against the linear growth of a classical random walk (`2403.19502v2` p.7). A freely chosen function f(t) proportional to t to the power -1/2 restores the classical diffusive scaling, and the paper is explicit that f(t) has no quantum-mechanical interpretation and that a quantum model does not imply markets are quantum systems (`2403.19502v2` p.2, p.7-8). The knobs are interpretable: the Cayley-Klein coin parameters control skew and dispersion, and decoherence - modelled as randomly broken links with probability p - collapses the distribution to the classical one at p = 0.5 while p = 0.1 to 0.3 stays non-Gaussian with fat tails (`2403.19502v2` p.11, p.14, p.16). That p is chosen by the analyst, not estimated from data, which makes the fat tails a tunable regime rather than an emergent market property. This paper uses no data at all (`2403.19502v2` p.3, p.21).

The empirical paper does the work the first one omits. It analyses 31 assets over up to 30 calendar years and shows that return distributions deviate from Gaussian beyond roughly 100 trading days and become strongly asymmetric or bimodal at 504 trading days, two trading years (`2505.13019v2` p.6-7, p.11). It defines a bimodality measure on a 0 to 1 scale, fits the quantum walk with four parameters at a fixed step count of 100 by hill climbing on mean absolute error, and reports that the walk's Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic is lower than the Gaussian's for every asymmetric case (`2505.13019v2` p.7, p.13-14, p.19). The volatility-scaling exponents across the tabulated assets cluster between 0.43 and 0.54, consistent with but not equal to the square-root-of-time 0.5, with one asset at 0.34 (`2505.13019v2` p.12).

The limits are stated as clearly as the results, and one of them is decisive. For the bimodal cases a two-component Gaussian mixture achieves both a lower mean absolute error and a lower KS statistic on every case; the walk's only defence is that its parameters are a generative dynamic rather than purely morphological (`2505.13019v2` p.19, p.22). The authors also say the bimodality most plausibly reflects non-stationarity and the mixing of regimes such as bull and bear markets rather than any interference in markets, and the samples are overlapping windows, so the KS statistics are descriptive distances rather than valid hypothesis tests (`2505.13019v2` p.6, p.20-21). There is no out-of-sample forecast and no trading test anywhere in either paper: this is a distributional model whose use is in how a platform describes and prices horizon risk, not in when to buy.

## 5. Ball mapper: a stability protocol worth copying

Ball mapper is not quantum at all and it is the most immediately usable item in the slice. It builds a graph from a point cloud by covering the cloud with balls of a single fixed radius, making each ball a vertex, drawing an edge wherever two balls intersect, sizing each vertex by the number of points it contains and colouring it by any function of those points such as a forward return (`2206.03622v1` p.9). The only user choice is the radius, which is also the weakness: the paper states plainly that there is no algorithm for the optimal radius and that reporting several radii is good practice (`2206.03622v1` p.9, p.44). The landmark selection is order-dependent, because the implementation allocates the first uncovered point in input order, so two runs on the same data and radius can give different covers (`2206.03622v1` p.9).

What makes the paper worth keeping is the validation protocol it supplies, which most nonlinear feature work would otherwise lack. It recommends normalising all axes, sweeping radii, checking that the mean of the colouring function within a ball exceeds its standard deviation before trusting the colour, and re-running on permuted input points so that only inference stable across permutations is believed; its own confidence intervals come from 10,000 repetitions (`2206.03622v1` p.9-10, p.13). The two applications are illustrative rather than predictive. In the corporate-failure cloud of 3,605 firm-years described by Altman's five ratios for 2015 with a failure dummy for 2016, failing firms occupy a very small subspace; the ball count falls from about 926 at radius 0.1 to under four at radius 1, and at radius 0.4 one ball had 20 percent of its constituent firms failing (`2206.03622v1` p.35-36, p.39). In the market cloud of eight dimensions spanning January 1960 to September 2020, 693 months in which the index beat the three-month bill in 56.7 percent of periods, the high end of the shape shows 70 percent or more up-months and the low end below 40 percent, so no ball is deterministically up or down (`2206.03622v1` p.39-40, p.42). The applications carry no out-of-sample evaluation of their own.

## 6. Class imbalance and calibration: what the 0.98 F1 actually measured

The quantum-ML fraud paper is the only classifier with labels in the slice, and its headline is a textbook class-imbalance artefact. The dataset is BankSim, an agent-based simulation of bank payments: 594,643 records over 180 steps, of which 587,443 are regular and 7,200 fraudulent, a fraud rate of about 1.2 percent (`2308.05237v1` p.17). The preprocessing section then states that specific subsets were extracted to create a balanced dataset containing 200 records, 100 fraudulent and 100 non-fraudulent (`2308.05237v1` p.21). On that balanced 200-row sample the quantum support vector classifier with the ZFeatureMap reports precision, recall and F1 of 0.98 for both classes, against 0.90 for the variational classifier, 0.78 for the estimator QNN and 0.58 for the sampler QNN (`2308.05237v1` p.23-25). Everything runs on the Qiskit Aer Qasm simulator with COBYLA capped at 200 iterations, so no hardware is involved (`2308.05237v1` p.23).

Two lessons follow, and both are measurement lessons rather than signal lessons. First, a classifier score computed at a 50 percent positive rate is not the score the classifier would obtain at 1.2 percent prevalence, and a research layer that quotes it as detection quality is publishing the wrong number; the correct report is precision and recall, or a precision-recall area, at the natural base rate, with an always-negative baseline beside it. Second, the paper reports accuracy and F1 with no calibration curve, so a stated confidence of 0.9 carries no measured meaning. Both failures are the exact ones this repo's calibration module already instruments: an excess-accuracy read against an always-up or always-negative baseline, and an isotonic or bucketed mapping from a declared confidence to its realized win rate.

## 7. Not applicable: the metaphor branch and the pre-hardware branches

Most of the slice has no research-layer landing, and the correct result is to say so rather than to invent one. The gauge-theory branch derives algebraic parameter constraints under local invariance, pins a volatility-correlation parameter to its extreme values of plus or minus one, and reports a volatility level equal to minus the interest rate; it has no data, no calibration and no out-of-sample test, so it is a formal equivalence between two volatility models and nothing more (`2306.03237v1`, unread in depth here; recorded from the shortlist). The quantum-ML branches - quantum neural networks, quantum kernels and quantum fraud classifiers - are either simulator demonstrations on toy samples as above or survey entries with no classical-baseline win, so they add no calculator this layer can run. The hardware branches - annealers, gate-based portfolio and derivative algorithms, quantum random access memory - have no research-layer landing because the layer runs offline deterministic calculators, not samplers, and the surveys quoted above supply the reason. The blockchain branches - quantum attacks on Bitcoin and proof-of-work by quantum sampling - bear on custody and settlement at an execution boundary this repo does not own, so they are recorded as leads and not claimed. The remaining physics models - quantum Brownian motion, minimal-length uncertainty, quantum Nash equilibria and duopoly - are price-process reparameterisations or game-theory translations with no estimation-discipline content this brief can extract, and are left unread.

## Caveats

Nothing in this slice establishes a tradable edge, and the papers with the strongest numbers are the weakest evidence. The hardware-facing studies use one year of daily data on 40 and 60 US liquid equities in a single market, select portfolios by a bespoke quadratic proxy rather than the Sharpe ratio, and are buy-and-hold exercises with no transaction costs, no out-of-sample period and no capacity analysis (`2007.01430v1` p.2; `2008.08669v1` p.2). The crash-cascade result is a randomly generated ten-institution, twenty-asset network with hand-set thresholds, no units and no calibration to observed failures, and its own finite-size argument says the transition narrows as the network grows (`1810.07690v2` p.5). The quantum-walk empirical study fits overlapping windows, so its samples are correlated and its KS statistics are not valid hypothesis tests; it selects assets precisely because they show asymmetry or bimodality, so it gives no base rate for how often those shapes occur; and it is a purely in-sample morphological fit with no forecast (`2505.13019v2` p.6, p.19). The theoretical walk paper uses no data at all (`2403.19502v2` p.21). The fraud benchmark is a simulator-only study on a balanced 200-row subset, so its 0.98 F1 is not transferable to natural prevalence (`2308.05237v1` p.21, p.23). The ball-mapper applications are illustrative robustness demonstrations with no out-of-sample evaluation, and its radius has no principled optimum (`2206.03622v1` p.9, p.44). Read the slice for measurement discipline, never for a signal: every paper that measured something measured a computational resource, a distributional fit or a map, and none measured profit.

## Papers read in depth

- `2204.10026v1` - A Structured Survey of Quantum Computing for the Financial Industry (2022). Reads 13 implemented finance applications through a four-layer framework and is candid about missing speed-up claims, the absence of machine learning and hardware limits; its limitation is that it stops at 2021 and covers only peer-reviewed, implemented work.
- `2007.01430v1` - Portfolio Optimization of 40 Stocks Using the D-Wave Quantum Annealer (2020). Benchmarks a quantum annealer against brute force, random sampling, a genetic algorithm and a simulated annealer and documents covariance positive-semidefiniteness failure; its limitation is one year of in-sample data on one market, no costs, and an ad-hoc proxy objective.
- `2008.08669v1` - Portfolio Optimization of 60 Stocks Using Classical and Quantum Algorithms (2020). Extends the exercise to 60 assets and documents embedding, chain-strength and dynamic-range limits; its limitation is that five classical methods finish in under a minute while the quantum run adds only a seed.
- `1810.07690v2` - Forecasting financial crashes with quantum computing (2018). Reduces an NP-hard network-equilibrium problem to a QUBO with an explicit qubit count; its limitation is that the only experiment is a ten-institution toy with no calibration and its own finite-size argument against prediction.
- `2403.19502v2` - On the potential of quantum walks for modeling financial return distributions (2024). Introduces the discrete-time quantum walk as a replacement for the Wiener process with interpretable skew, dispersion and decoherence parameters; its limitation is that it is a theoretical study that uses no data.
- `2505.13019v2` - Characterizing asymmetric and bimodal long-term financial return distributions through quantum walks (2025). Fits 31 assets over two-year horizons and reports error statistics against a Gaussian and a mixture; its limitation is an in-sample morphological fit, overlapping windows, and a mixture that fits better.
- `2206.03622v1` - Topological Data Analysis Ball Mapper for Finance (2022). Documents a shape-based visualisation with a stability protocol and two finance applications including firm failure; its limitation is that no optimal radius exists and the applications are illustrative rather than predictive.
- `2308.05237v1` - Financial Fraud Detection: A Comparative Study of Quantum Machine Learning Models (2023). Compares four quantum classifiers on a labelled fraud task and exposes a class-imbalance artefact; its limitation is a balanced 200-row subset, simulator-only runs and no calibration curve.
