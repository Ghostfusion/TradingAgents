# Language models, news and text signals: the model that reads the news is part of the measurement

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from the arXiv q-fin.GN (General Finance) and cs.CL harvest that turn text into numbers: large language models used as sentiment scorers, return forecasters and analyst surrogates; disclosure and filing text used as a firm-level signal; narrative and tone measurement; and the evaluation problems that are specific to text-derived features. It is written for an operator of a research and analysis layer - the deterministic calculators under `tradingagents/strategies/`, wired as LangChain tools for LLM analysts, gated by `enable_*` config keys, scored by the sentiment and news engines, and checked by the report verifier - who has to decide whether a text-derived number can be trusted in a backtest and in production. The sibling library under `Strategies/books/` covers the market and microstructure half of the same corpus; this one covers the language, news and disclosure half.

## What this covers

The slice runs from hand-built word lists scored over U.S. filings, through the first wave of transformer sentiment classifiers, to generative models that are queried as analysts, as forecaster agents, and as frozen snapshots of the public record. Every stage has a concrete surface in this repository: `tradingagents/strategies/text_factors.py` carries a reduced Loughran-McDonald seed dictionary with a `DICTIONARY_VERSION`; `tradingagents/strategies/news_relevance.py` scores and admits news articles; `tradingagents/strategies/sentiment.py` and `tradingagents/strategies/news_score.py` aggregate tone and novelty; and the sentiment analyst prefetches news and social rows into its prompt. The arc matters because the repo's own LLM analysts are text consumers, so the papers that measure how text features behave are papers about this layer.

The single most consequential finding is that the model is itself a research choice. Seven independently developed LLMs scoring the same 1,946 earnings-call transcripts produce a mean pairwise rank correlation of only 0.52 across thirteen measures, and in a variance-components decomposition the provider term accounts for 33.4% of score variation against 34.4% for the transcript itself (`2609.31013v1`, p.3, p.4). For a platform whose sentiment and news reads are produced by a language model, that is not a caveat bolted onto the results - it is the results. A text feature is a measurement with a provider attached, and every other number in this brief has to be read through that lens.

## The short answer

1. Model choice changes the number: seven providers scoring the same 1,946 earnings calls give a mean pairwise rank correlation of 0.52, and the provider term explains 33.4% of score variance against 34.4% for the transcript (`2609.31013v1`, p.3, p.4).
2. Agreement is construct-dependent, not a single reliability figure: sentiment reaches a mean pairwise correlation of 0.85, innovation 0.76, integrity 0.32, and an explicitly rule-based specificity ratio only 0.23 (`2609.31013v1`, p.3, p.16).
3. The typical within-call standard deviation across providers is 0.75 of the total standard deviation, rising to 0.97 for the specificity ratio and 0.91 for uncertainty, so on those constructs nearly all variation is which model answered (`2609.31013v1`, p.16).
4. Contextual sentiment beats word lists on a human benchmark: transformer indices reach a macro-F1 of 0.67 to 0.69 against 0.187 for the lexicon measures on the same 588 articles (`2607.13968v1`, p.23).
5. Frozen model checkpoints extract information the market has not fully priced: a sector-neutral outlook score predicts one-month returns with t = 6.02 in a pooled panel of 14,918 observations (`2604.21433v1`, p.3).
6. Anonymising company names improves in-sample GPT news returns, meaning the distraction effect outweighs look-ahead bias: replaced headlines earn 30.97 versus 25.08 basis points per day on the scraped set (`2309.17322v1`, p.8).
7. LLM return forecasts inherit human extrapolation and make it worse: the estimated degree of extrapolation is 38.1 for GPT-4 against 12.09 for the Forcerank crowd (`2409.11540v1`, p.18).
8. A prompted GPT-4o with few-shot examples reaches 0.86 accuracy on the Financial PhraseBank, close to a fine-tuned FinBERT at 0.88 (`2410.01987v1`, p.5).
9. GPT earnings forecasts underperform analysts on average but beat them in more than one quarter of firm-quarters, and a one-standard-deviation rise in metric overlap cuts forecast error by 16.8% (`2412.01069v2`, p.19, p.21).
10. Task-specific disclosure dictionaries beat both general psychological dictionaries and the Loughran-McDonald lists on bankruptcy classification: 0.79 versus 0.74 versus 0.63 test accuracy (`2101.00719v1`, p.23, p.26).
11. Analysts under-react to earnings-call uncertainty and over-react to sentiment, a gap measurable only because the text can be morphed while numbers are held fixed (`2511.15214v2`, p.5).
12. The literature itself is young: in finance, 64.7% of the reviewed ChatGPT papers are classified as potential applications rather than value-realization studies (`2412.05731v1`, p.17).

## 1. The provider is a measurement choice

`2609.31013v1` is the paper to read before wiring any LLM-derived feature into a platform, because it holds the text, the prompt, the construct definition and the numeric scale fixed and varies only the provider. Seven models from seven developers score 1,946 S&P 500 earnings calls from 2024 on thirteen measures (p.2, p.9); of 177,086 requested scores, 98 (0.06%) could not be parsed and are treated as missing (p.11). Across the thirteen measures the mean pairwise Spearman correlation is 0.52 (p.3, p.16). Agreement is construct-dependent: sentiment 0.85, innovation 0.76, integrity 0.32, and a rule-based specificity ratio only 0.23 (p.3). Two models, Anthropic and Qwen, assign a political-risk score of exactly 0.00 to more than 99% of calls and are dropped; the others find exposure in 12% to 42% of the same calls (p.12).

The level differences are large enough to matter. The average within-call standard deviation across providers is 0.75 times the overall standard deviation, ranging from 0.42 for political risk and 0.48 for sentiment to 0.91 for uncertainty, 0.95 for integrity and 0.97 for the specificity ratio (p.16); on those constructs nearly all score variation reflects which model answered rather than which call was scored. A variance-components decomposition puts the transcript component at 34.4% of variation, the provider component at 33.4% and the provider-by-transcript residual at 32.2%, with the transcript share 74% for sentiment but only 13% for integrity and 4% for the specificity ratio (p.4). Two operational corollaries follow. First, self-reported confidence is not a reliability signal: about 30% of all scores carry a confidence of exactly 0.80, and conditioning on both models being confident lowers the average pairwise correlation from 0.52 to 0.48, falling to 0.33 as the confidence definition tightens (p.16). Second, agreement in scores need not imply agreement in reasoning: the average cosine similarity of the written justifications is only 0.69 for sentiment, the construct with the highest score agreement (p.18).

The paper also prices the substitution risk against older methods. The seven-model ensemble correlates with the non-LLM benchmark at 0.56 for corporate culture, 0.55 for innovation and specificity, 0.47 for climate exposure, but only 0.29 for sentiment and 0.13 for management clarity (p.17). Sentiment is the clean example: the highest cross-model correlation (0.85) coexists with one of the lowest correlations to the dictionary measure (0.29), so strong inter-model agreement does not imply agreement with the operationalization the repo already runs. Model choice propagates into inference: for sentiment and uncertainty the sign and significance of return regressions are stable but magnitudes vary by up to 60%, and for other measures the sign and significance themselves can flip (p.6, p.16). Averaging the seven providers meets a 0.80 reliability benchmark for relative rankings on ten of thirteen measures but for absolute score levels on only five (p.6).

## 2. Lexicon versus contextual sentiment

`2607.13968v1` builds daily news-mood indices from 143,755 English-language Factiva articles published in 2025 (p.8), scoring sentence-level sentiment with FinBERT and aggregating to article and daily measures through several normalization schemes. The validation is unusually strong: 444 paid participants rated 588 articles on a five-point scale, three ratings each, 1,776 evaluations, with a performance bonus tied to the consensus rating (p.19). Inter-rater agreement is high, with a mean individual-minus-article difference of -0.07 and most observations within +/-0.5 points (p.20). Against those human labels the transformer indices reach accuracies of 66% to 68% and macro-F1 of 0.67 to 0.69, while the Shapiro-style and Barbaglia lexicon indices reach 39% accuracy and 0.187 macro-F1 (p.23). Correlations with the human benchmark are about 0.75 to 0.76 for transformer measures against 0.70 to 0.71 for lexicon measures (p.23).

The internal structure is instructive for anyone aggregating several feeds. Among the six transformer indices, contemporaneous (lag-0) pairwise correlations average 0.903 and all pairs exceed 0.8; correlations decay fast, to a mean of 0.741 at lag 1 and 0.645 at lag 2 (p.17). The two vocabulary-based indices correlate with the transformer family at a mean of 0.765 (Barbaglia) and 0.769 (Shapiro) at lag 0, but only half of those pairs clear 0.8 and none clear 0.9, and the lag-1 correlation falls to 0.658 and 0.670 (p.18). Vocabulary methods are smoother and lower-amplitude because they aggregate word-level polarity, whereas the transformer measures are more sensitive to contextual polarity. The practical reading: a lexicon tone and a transformer tone are related but not interchangeable, and mixing them into one unlabeled series hides a scale and method difference.

`2410.01987v1` benchmarks prompt engineering against fine-tuning on the 4,848-sentence Financial PhraseBank, with 3,101 sentences used for training (p.3). A fine-tuned FinBERT reaches 0.88 accuracy and 0.87 F1; GPT-4o with few-shot examples reaches 0.86 and 0.85, and zero-shot 0.85 and 0.84; GPT-3.5-turbo reaches 0.78 and 0.80 (p.5). The narrow claim is that, on a clean sentence-level task, a prompted general model can match a domain fine-tune. The broader claim the authors do not make is that prompting removes the need for domain training. The dataset is small, the result is task-specific, and the models were queried at one fixed version.

## 3. Model checkpoints as timestamped information sets

`2604.21433v1` turns the knowledge cutoff into the research design. Twelve frozen OpenAI checkpoints with knowledge cutoffs from September 2021 to August 2025 are queried once per firm for roughly 7,000 U.S. equities, with no retrieval, so the parameters act as a timestamped digest of pre-cutoff text (p.2, p.9). A 100-question trivia check returns a mean accuracy of zero percent on post-cutoff events, which is the audit the design needs (p.17). The sector-neutral outlook score predicts one-month returns with t = 4.25 in the primary specification (GPT-4.1, June 2024 cutoff) and t = 6.02 in a pooled panel with model fixed effects (N = 14,918), surviving industry fixed effects (t = 5.74), micro-cap exclusion (t = 4.42), aggressive winsorisation (t = 4.53) and a bootstrap Fama-MacBeth procedure (p = 0.0002); a long-short sort would have earned 2.55% monthly (30.6% annualised) in sample (p.3). Predictability broadly increases with horizon (Spearman r = 0.91), with the authors stating that the six unique analysis months prevent a formal time-series alpha test (p.3, p.17). The score also validates against outcomes: realised twelve-month revenue growth (t = 10.89) and analyst target-price revisions (t = 4.80) (p.3).

The multiple-testing discipline is part of the result. Twelve checkpoints are estimated separately; seven produce individually significant coefficients against an expected 0.6 false positives under the global null, and four survive a Bonferroni threshold of 0.05/12 = 0.0042 (|t| > 2.87) (p.18). The Spearman correlation between cutoff recency and the coefficient is 0.62 (p = 0.03), and within the June 2024 cutoff cluster the larger model gives the stronger signal (GPT-4.1 coefficient 0.0122 versus GPT-4.1-nano 0.0065), suggesting model capability amplifies the signal even when the information set is held fixed (p.3). The rule this yields for a text feature is that a model checkpoint and its training window are dataset provenance: a feature derived from a model with a cutoff must carry that cutoff as metadata.

`2309.17322v1` makes the cleanest decomposition of what a training window does to a backtest. GPT-3.5's training runs through September 2021, leaving October 2021 to December 2022 as the only out-of-sample window (p.4). The authors build long-short strategies from GPT sentiment on 129,431 scraped headlines (6,723 companies, median market cap 2.59bn USD) and 181,908 Thomson Reuters headlines (678 S&P 500 names, median 56.74bn USD) (p.4), then repeat everything with company names and products replaced by a random string; the algorithm rewrites 100,401 of the 129,431 scraped headlines and 180,237 of the 181,908 Reuters headlines (p.6). In-sample the anonymised headlines do better: 30.97 versus 25.08 basis points per day on the scraped set (p = 0.066) and 81.64 versus 77.27 on the Reuters set (p = 0.017) (p.8). That is surprising only until the classification table: the original headline is wrong 17.6% of the time against 38.95% for the anonymised one, yet when the original is wrong and the replaced response is neutral the original strategy loses 242 basis points on average, against only 97 in the mirror scenario (p.10, p.11). The original headline makes some very bad trades that the replaced strategy sits out, and the pattern is strongest for larger companies - the ones with the most training text. Out-of-sample the difference largely loses significance, consistent with most of the in-sample distraction coming from knowledge of the future. Both papers give the same operational rule: strip entity identifiers before scoring any news backtest, and log how many headlines were rewritten.

## 4. Language models as forecasters and analysts

`2409.11540v1` puts GPT-4o into a crowd-sourced ranking contest. Across 1,283 weekly contests covering 200 tickers (p.12), the correlation between the average human rank and the GPT-4 rank is 0.279 (p.4), and the regressions show both place positive, decaying weight on the past twelve weekly returns (p.14). The catch is that realised weekly returns show short-term reversals, so the extrapolation is counterproductive. Fitting an exponential-decay model, the degree of extrapolation is 38.1 for GPT-4 against 12.09 for humans (p.18): the model is more recency-biased than the crowd it is being compared to, and the positive coefficients on lagged returns are actually negatively predictive of future returns (p.19). It is also optimistic: the mean expected next-month return is 2.2% against a historical mean of 1.4% and a realised 1.1% (p.6), and only 0.45% of forecasts are below zero, as if expected returns were assumed nonnegative (p.22). Calibration is better than humans but not good: the 80% interval contains the realised return 76.9% of the time, versus 79.0% from simply using the historical 10th and 90th percentiles, and versus 36% for the CFO survey it is modelled on (p.22). A second, independently trained model (Claude) gives a sentiment measure correlated 0.78 with the GPT one (p.21), so the extrapolative behaviour is not confined to one vendor.

`2412.01069v2` gives the counterweight and a conditional view. Over 7,114 earnings press releases from 1,000 firms around GPT-4 Turbo's April 2023 cutoff (p.4), GPT's mean absolute forecast error falls monotonically from 0.117 when its chosen financial metrics do not overlap the analysts' top-three metrics to 0.061 at the highest overlap, roughly 48% lower (p.19). In the multivariate specification, a one-standard-deviation increase in metric overlap is associated with a 16.8% lower forecast error (p.21). Against analysts, the mean error difference is positive, so GPT loses on average, but the 25th percentile of the difference is -0.001, meaning GPT beats the analyst consensus in more than a quarter of firm-quarters (p.19). The paper's most transferable contribution is the diagnostic frame: narrative selection is stable across firms and human-like (earlier sentences favoured, -11.0% probability per position; guidance-bearing sentences +18.2%; a one-standard-deviation rise in sentence length +5.2%), but accuracy is higher when the selected sentences are more readable and forward-looking and lower when they are more negatively toned, so GPT overweights a salient cue that adds noise (p.5, p.12, p.13, p.21). A composite reliability score built from observable processing features rises monotonically with forecast accuracy - a better gate than the average error.

## 5. Disclosure and filing text as a signal

`2101.00719v1` builds a task-specific dictionary and shows it beats general ones. From 500 bankrupt and 500 matched non-bankrupt Management Discussion and Analysis sections, a balanced design (p.3), bankrupt firms use debt words at 3.04% versus 2.58% for healthy firms and distress words at 0.33% versus 0.21% (p.14); the debt-word gap appears four years before the filing and the distress-word gap widens sharply two years before (p.17). The evidence is directional and parametric rather than large: debt words t = 12.32, negative words t = 8.28, restructure words t = 7.67, distress words t = 17.38 (p.21). In logistic models the Loughran-McDonald word lists reach 0.74 test accuracy against 0.63 for the broad LIWC dictionary, and the custom stress dictionary reaches 0.79; AUC is 0.72, 0.78 and 0.86 respectively, rising to 0.87 for the combined model (p.23, p.26). A one-percent increase in distress words multiplies the odds of bankruptcy by a factor of about 154 in the fitted model (p.25). The honest caveat is the design: 500 versus 500 is a balanced experiment, so the reported accuracy is not a base rate and the model classifies a hypothetical firm rather than the population.

`2501.01763v1` extends filing text to a thematic index. It measures AI engagement from 10-K filings of 3,395 NASDAQ firms, 2010 to 2022, using AI-term frequency and TF-IDF normalised by document length (p.2, p.14). AI mentions grow from 7 filings in 2010 to 527 in 2022 (p.15). Validation is an event study on the ChatGPT launch: 582 AI-classified stocks had a cumulative average abnormal return of 17.25% (t = 8.24) against 11.59% for the other 1,959, a statistically significant 5.68-point gap (p.21). Constructed indices average a 17.48% cumulative abnormal return over the event window, comparable to the best of the AI ETFs studied, while the average across all twelve ETFs is 13.00% and only six of twelve show significant returns (p.26). The authors state the limitation themselves: the event-study regressions have R-squared between 0.002 and 0.010, and low R-squared is acceptable in an event study but not evidence of a mechanism (p.23).

## 6. Narrative, analyst belief and the text-morphing design

`2511.15214v2` predicts analyst forecasts from earnings-call text alone and then intervenes on the text. Using 35,203 transcripts from 5,370 companies (p.9), embeddings of management remarks predict 25% to 40% of the variation in expected changes and forecast dispersion even without numerical fundamentals (p.19), and a six-dimension narrative score reaches about 40% of the full-embedding performance (p.5). The method is a text-morphing experiment: a model generates counterfactual executive remarks that vary one narrative dimension while holding quantitative content fixed, and the predicted treatment effect is compared for analyst expectations (AEE) and future realised earnings (FRE) (p.5). The result is a measured asymmetry. Analysts under-react to uncertainty (average PTE -9.22 basis points for AEE versus -41.09 for FRE), confidence (6.09 versus 25.76) and guidance (10.84 versus 19.43), and over-react to sentiment (34.88 versus 25.76) and global perspective (10.84 versus -19.43) (p.5). Jargon is the one dimension where attention is roughly rational. Against the analyst consensus, adding the text model improves accuracy by 8.38% at one year but 27.50% at two years and 31.64% at three years, with mean-squared-error reductions of 6% to 13% that are significant under a Clark-West nested test (p.20, p.21). The look-ahead discipline is the strongest in the slice: every numeral, date, quarter and percentage is masked to the model's [MASK] token, and the FinBERT checkpoint used was pretrained only through 2017 on text that predates the evaluation windows (p.16).

The mechanism matters for a research layer. If analysts attend to a narrative dimension that does not predict realised earnings, then a sentiment read built the same way inherits the same bias; the fix is not a better tone model but a horizon- and dimension-specific test of which narrative legs predict outcomes. The six dimensions are a template for the kind of named exposure the repo's registers could carry rather than a single scalar sentiment.

## 7. Evaluation problems specific to text methods

`2310.08678v1` supplies the capability ceiling. On mock CFA exams, GPT-4 scores 73.2% zero-shot, 74.0% with chain-of-thought and 74.6% with ten-shot prompts on Level I, and 57.4%, 61.4% and 60.5% on Level II; ChatGPT scores 58.8% to 63.0% on Level I and 46.6% to 47.6% on Level II (p.3). Level II prompts are about ten times longer (p.5), and when chain-of-thought makes an answer worse the dominant error type is missing knowledge, 55.2% of such errors for ChatGPT and 50.0% for GPT-4, with pure calculation errors at 17.2% and 28.6% (p.6, p.7). So the failure is comprehension of long, table-heavy cases and gaps in financial knowledge, not arithmetic per se - the same regime this platform's filing reads and call transcripts live in. The practical rule is to scope which text tasks a model is allowed to perform, and to prefer retrieval or a calculator over free-form reasoning on long numeric passages.

`2412.05731v1` is the map of this literature and a warning about its maturity. It screens 264 SSRN papers and 144 published papers, retaining 33 relevant studies (11 accounting, 22 finance) (p.2, p.14). Sentiment analysis is the single most common task at 21 papers (22.3%), ahead of question-answering at 18 (19.1%) and classification at 15 (16.0%); GPT-4 is the most-used model at 26 papers (17.7%) (p.15). In finance, 44 of 68 reviewed studies (64.7%) are potential applications and only 11 (16.2%) are value-realization studies, while in accounting 38 of 48 (79.2%) are potential applications (p.15, p.16, p.17). Quoting Gartner, it notes enterprise adoption was 5% in 2023 with a forecast above 80% by 2026 (p.2). The reading is that most published LLM-finance work is demonstration, not validated production evidence, so a feature backed by a single paper is backed by a demonstration.

Three evaluation rules fall out of the slice. First, do not treat self-reported confidence as a reliability signal (`2609.31013v1`, p.16). Second, diagnose conditionally: build a composite score from observable processing features and use it as a gate (`2412.01069v2`, p.6, p.21). Third, state and respect the multiple-testing and short-window limits the strongest papers set for themselves (`2604.21433v1`, p.3, p.18).

## Caveats

Several of these papers establish only that a measurement is unreliable or that a capability exists, not that anything is tradable. `2609.31013v1` studies measurement invariance and makes no return claim; `2412.05731v1` is a review; and `2310.08678v1` is a set of mock exams at one model snapshot. The return-bearing results are single-market and mostly single-period: `2501.01763v1` covers NASDAQ-listed firms 2010-2022 and its own regressions have R-squared near 0.01; `2604.21433v1` rests on six unique analysis months, which the authors say prevents a formal alpha test; `2607.13968v1` covers one year (2025) of one publisher mix; `2511.15214v2` covers U.S. annual earnings calls and is in-silico and model-dependent; `2309.17322v1` and `2409.11540v1` use strategies and contests that are explicitly infeasible, with no transaction costs, no short-sale constraints and, in the Forcerank case, a crowd platform that shut down in 2018. `2101.00719v1` is a balanced 500-versus-500 design, so its accuracy is not a base rate. `2410.01987v1` rests on one small labelled dataset. None of the sentiment or news numbers here comes with an implementation cost, a capacity estimate or a live out-of-sample track. The one result that is not a caveat but a design constraint is the cross-model divergence of `2609.31013v1`: it makes the provider part of the measurement, so any number quoted in this brief is a number for a particular model and prompt, and the honest unit is a distribution across providers rather than a point.

## Papers read in depth
