# Energy and commodity markets: the transferable content for an equity-research layer is storage, carry and mean reversion, not the trading of power itself

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads the electricity-and-storage cluster of the arXiv q-fin.GN harvest together with the commodity-curve, gas-power and commodity-ETF papers that sit beside it. The slice holds 53 records from 2006 to 2026, 14 of them with a journal reference. It is written for TradingAgents, a research and analysis layer that prices equities, scores them through deterministic calculators and an LLM analyst graph, and records every decision against a schema. The repository is equity oriented and has no commodity instrument, no power contract and no futures curve, so the useful content of this category is not the trading of energy: it is the storage-and-carry structure of commodity markets, the mean-reversion and volatility-panel statistics that transfer to energy and materials equities, and the ways in which one asset class is mis-measured when it is treated as another.

## What this covers

The slice has one arc. Commodity markets are the asset class where the deliverable itself is the state variable: an agricultural or power price is set by a physical inventory and a production lag, so its curve, its mean-reversion speed and its upper tail are all consequences of storage rather than of sentiment, and the papers here measure each of those consequences. The single most consequential finding for this repository is that the commodity object this project would actually need - a low-dimensional curve state - is real and cheap to state: across 21 commodity futures the Nelson-Siegel level, slope and curvature explain 96.5% of the variation in the curve on average, and dropping the slope term alone collapses the fit to 37.5% (`2308.00383v1`, p.11). The second most consequential is a negative one: electricity price volatility does not carry the equity leverage effect, because the apparent asymmetric response disappears once the price level and mean-reversion are conditioned on (`2605.13320v1`, p.1), so any energy-equity "vol rises when price falls" claim this layer emits is a claim the commodity literature does not support.

## The short answer

1. The commodity futures curve is a low-dimensional object: level, slope and curvature explain 96.5% of curve variation on average, and removing the slope term alone drops the fit to 37.5% (`2308.00383v1`, p.11).
2. The tradeable leg is slope continuation, and it is small and decaying: 1.77% annualised gross (t-statistic 7.23, Sharpe 1.41) falling to 0.55%-1.31% net of cost, with the net Sharpe dropping from 1.74 to 0.71 after the 2000 financialisation and from 1.47 to 0.20 after 2009 (`2308.00383v1`, p.13, p.16, p.24).
3. Storage is an option, not a project: the payoff is max(S + B - O, 0), with the service or energy spread as the payoff and the wear cost as the strike, so an NPV framing understates operational flexibility (`2112.09816v2`, p.9).
4. At an empirical wear cost of 100 EUR/MWh (300 EUR/kWh over 3000 equivalent full cycles), energy arbitrage is not profitable in most European day-ahead markets; it only becomes attractive below roughly 40-50 EUR/MWh in Great Britain, France, Germany and Italy and below 20 EUR/MWh in Spain (`2112.09816v2`, p.11, p.13).
5. Remunerated frequency services dominate energy arbitrage: the Danish FCR-N service was theoretically profitable in almost every hour of January-September 2021 even at 100 EUR/MWh of wear cost, whereas Norwegian FCR-N stayed under 20% (`2112.09816v2`, p.19, p.20).
6. In Spain, day-ahead arbitrage needs the wear cost to fall to 15 EUR/MWh before the potentially profitable utilisation rate exceeds 20%; ancillary services raise that to 35 EUR/MWh and upward secondary reserve to 50 EUR/MWh, and the secondary band price fell from 19.58 to 8.31 EUR/MW between 2015 and 2019 (`2007.00486v2`, p.1, p.14, p.16).
7. Storage is price-making: more independent investors cut each investor's profit but raise total installed capacity, and a small efficiency edge captures most of the market - a single Type-1 investor (0.95 round-trip versus 0.94 and 0.93) held about 70% of the profit share (`2201.02290v2`, p.4, p.5).
8. Power volatility is a panel, not a series: each day is a curve of 24 (or 96) delivery periods, logs are unavailable because prices can be negative, and about 40% of observed price variation is deterministic mean-reversion rather than innovation (`2605.13320v1`, p.2, p.10, p.13).
9. Electricity volatility does not exhibit the equity leverage effect: the apparent asymmetric response disappears once price level and mean-reversion are conditioned on (`2605.13320v1`, p.1).
10. The volatility panel is forecastable and the forecast is worth money: a matrix-HAR with a quarterly component reaches a Mincer-Zarnowitz R2 of 40-45% and 94.7% coverage on pointwise 95% bands, and replacing a backward-looking 28-day variance with it lifts the explanatory power of a spread-risk-premium regression from 24.4% to 47.2% (`2606.05991v1`, p.8, p.11, p.16).
11. Renewables remove the upper tail rather than the mean: in PJM a move from 0-1% VRE cut the 0.90th quantile of the detrended price by $10 against $1.6 at the median, and temporal volatility fell in five of the six US ISOs studied, reversing in SPP once VRE exceeded about 40% (`2112.11338v3`, p.14, p.20).
12. Gas and power must be modelled jointly: dropping the cross-commodity correlation moved a power-plant portfolio's 95% VaR from 1081 to 2213 over a three-year horizon, while collapsing the spike component to a Gaussian lowered it to 565.5 (`0910.0236v3`, p.16).

## 1. The commodity curve is a low-dimensional, tradeable object

Commodity futures do not need a state vector per contract. Fitting Nelson-Siegel daily to 21 commodity futures from January 1992 to June 2019, the three parameters explain on average 96.5% of the in-sample variation of the curve; removing the slope component leaves 37.5% and removing curvature leaves 82.7%, so all three are load-bearing (`2308.00383v1`, p.11). The level beta is always positive, and 17 of the 21 average slope betas are negative, meaning these markets were in contango on average over the sample (`2308.00383v1`, p.11). Liquidity concentrates hard at the front: the nearest four contracts carried 82.2% of total open interest on average, which is why the strategies trade only those maturities (`2308.00383v1`, p.9). The cross-sectional bet is continuation of the previous day's curve movement. The level strategy is unprofitable; the slope strategy earns 1.77% annualised (t-statistic 7.23, Sharpe 1.41) and the curvature strategy 0.68% (t-statistic 5.34, Sharpe 1.23), all gross (`2308.00383v1`, p.13). These are not free: daily turnover is 1.17 and 1.20, and after three transaction-cost scenarios the slope strategy nets 0.55% to 1.31% per year (Sharpe 0.44 to 1.04) while the curvature strategy nets -0.55% to 0.24% and is negative under two of the three cost scenarios (`2308.00383v1`, p.16). The slope premium is also regime-dependent: the net Sharpe falls from 1.74 to 0.71 across the December 2000 financialisation and from 1.47 to 0.20 across the 2009 crisis, so a gross Sharpe read as current is wrong (`2308.00383v1`, p.24).

## 2. Storage is an option written on the spread, and the wear cost is the strike

The general battery payoff is conditional dispatch: the asset runs only when the service payment plus the balancing term exceeds the operating expense, so payoff_t = max(S + B - O, 0), an option on a stochastic underlying with the wear cost as the strike (`2112.09816v2`, p.9-10). The wear cost is the pivot and it is measurable: an empirical 300 EUR/kWh battery cost over 3000 equivalent full cycles implies a pure wear cost of 0.1 EUR/kWh, that is 100 EUR/MWh (`2112.09816v2`, p.11). At that cost, day-ahead arbitrage is not profitable in most European markets; it turns attractive below roughly 40-50 EUR/MWh in Great Britain, France, Germany and Italy, needs under 20 EUR/MWh in Spain, and is barely viable in Norway at any cost because hydropower already supplies the flexibility (`2112.09816v2`, p.13-14). Frequency containment dominates: from January to September 2021 the Danish FCR-N service was theoretically profitable in almost 100% of hours even at 100 EUR/MWh, while Norwegian FCR-N stayed under 20% (`2112.09816v2`, p.19, p.20). The Spanish study sharpens the same result into market design. Day-ahead arbitrage needs the wear cost to fall to 15 EUR/MWh for the potentially profitable utilisation rate to exceed 20%; adding ancillary services raises the threshold to 35 EUR/MWh and upward secondary reserve to 50 EUR/MWh (`2007.00486v2`, p.1). The reason is payment structure: secondary reserve pays a capacity band as well as energy, and its band price fell from 19.58 EUR/MW in 2015 to 8.31 EUR/MW in 2019, shrinking profitable hours from 2347 to 596 upward and from 1880 to 74 downward (`2007.00486v2`, p.14, p.16). Even with a zero wear cost, and assuming perfect foresight of the daily minimum and maximum, day-ahead arbitrage in Spain clears only about 310 days of 2019 (`2007.00486v2`, p.16-17).

## 3. Power is a panel, not a series, and it has no leverage effect

Electricity is the asset whose cross-section is a curve: the day is a vector of 24 hourly (or 96 quarter-hourly) delivery periods, and because spot prices can be negative, the usual log transformation is unavailable (`2605.13320v1`, p.2). Interpreting each observed price as the average of a latent price over its delivery period, and using three European zones over 2551 daily observations (61368 data points per zone, aggregated to 365 weeks) from October 2018 to September 2025, the study estimates a semigroup-adjusted realized covariation and finds that deterministic mean-reversion - the "propagation share" - accounts for about 40% of the observed variation in all three zones (`2605.13320v1`, p.10, p.13). Ignoring it leaves a badly contaminated estimate. The estimated semigroup matrix is not diagonal, and its largest eigenvalue (about 0.8 in Germany) implies a common-shock half-life of roughly 3 days in Germany and about 6 days in Norway (`2605.13320v1`, p.12). The headline negative finding is that the leverage effect is absent: there is an unconditional weak inverse-leverage pattern (high prices with high volatility), but once price level and mean-reversion in volatility are conditioned on, the asymmetry largely disappears, so electricity volatility does not generally respond asymmetrically to price shocks (`2605.13320v1`, p.1). Extreme prints are real and large: over the six-ISO US window the detrended minimum was -$2504.87/MWh in NYISO, -$208.37/MWh in ISONE and -$76.29/MWh in CAISO, and Spain's day-ahead bids are capped at 0 to 180.3 EUR/MWh so it cannot print a negative at all (`2112.11338v3`, p.14; `2007.00486v2`, p.11).

## 4. The volatility panel is forecastable, and the forecast is worth money

The follow-on paper shows the panel can be predicted one week ahead and that the prediction is economically useful. A parsimonious matrix-HAR model with daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly components beats an exponential-moving-average benchmark; the quarterly component is necessary, and models that include renewable generation shares dominate on the model confidence set (`2606.05991v1`, p.8). On German spot data from October 2018 to October 2025, with 1984 scored out-of-sample forecast origins, the best models reach a Mincer-Zarnowitz R2 of 40-45% on the realized variance and an average pointwise 95%-band coverage of 94.7% (nominal 0.950) (`2606.05991v1`, p.8, p.11-12). Variance-targeting ratios on four zero-net-volume spread portfolios are statistically indistinguishable from 1 for the DRD-HAR models but not for the EWMA benchmark, and the Mincer-Zarnowitz calibration test still rejects (0,1) for all models because the observed RCV is strongly right-skewed - the models are not reactive enough in crisis times (`2606.05991v1`, p.8, p.9, p.10-11). The economic payoff is the sharpest number in the paper: in a spread-risk-premium regression on German monthly forward contracts, the backward-looking 28-day rolling variance gives an R2 of 24.4%, while the forecast realized covariation raises it to 47.2% at best, with all RCV models landing between 35% and 48% (`2606.05991v1`, p.15-16).

## 5. Renewables and the merit order: the upper tail is what VRE removes

Zero-marginal-cost generation displaces expensive plant and pulls the clearing price down, and the effect is not uniform across the distribution. Across six US ISOs (2014-2020, ERCOT excluded) an increase in variable renewable energy penetration lowered the detrended system price in all of them, with the largest reduction at the 0.90th quantile (`2112.11338v3`, p.14). In PJM a move from 0-1% VRE cut the median detrended price by $1.6 and the 0.90th quantile by $10, while a later 5-6% move cut them by only $0.2 and $0.8 - the effect is strongly non-linear, largest at the first increments of penetration (`2112.11338v3`, p.14). Temporal volatility fell with VRE in five of the six ISOs, the exception being SPP, where volatility falls up to about 40% VRE and then rises as wind oversupply drives negative prices (`2112.11338v3`, p.20). Estimated skewness trends toward symmetry for four ISOs and turns negative for SPP and CAISO, the two highest-penetration systems (`2112.11338v3`, p.15, p.18). Crucially, the slice finds no clean trend between the frequency of negative prices and VRE share: negative prints concentrate at high VRE in SPP and CAISO, but the authors conclude that at current penetration levels other temporal and geographic factors carry the effect (`2112.11338v3`, p.18-19). The authors' own framing is a portfolio-theory one - adding diverse, low-correlated assets lowers total price volatility - and they bound it carefully to the studied penetration ranges.

## 6. Cross-commodity coupling and the price-making of storage

Power and gas are linked through the spark spread and fuel switching, and the joint model matters for risk. A two-factor arithmetic specification - a common slow mean-reverting factor plus an independent stationary diffusion with a normal-inverse-Gaussian invariant density - reproduces both the autocorrelation of each price and the cross-correlation between them without introducing jump processes (`0910.0236v3`, p.9). Calibrated on Powernext electricity and NBP gas from 14 January 2003 to 20 August 2008, the fast speeds imply a correlation half-life of roughly 2 days for gas and roughly 30 days for electricity, with a slow common reversion of about 64 days (`0910.0236v3`, p.5). Both series are far from normal: excess kurtosis 4.5 for gas and 2.3 for electricity, skewness 0.77 and 0.57 (`0910.0236v3`, p.4). The risk consequences are large. For a portfolio short a gas-fired power plant (heat rate 3, generation cost 5 EUR/MWh, interest rate 5%), the estimated 95% VaR is 262.4 at six months, 495.4 at one year and 1081.0 at three years; removing the cross-commodity correlation raises those to 250.1, 880.2 and 2213.1, and collapsing the spike component to a Gaussian lowers them to 107.7, 275.9 and 565.5 (`0910.0236v3`, p.16). On the investment side, storage is not a price-taker. A non-cooperative game over CAISO 2019 data (12 monthly scenarios, price linear in net demand, storage at 90 $/kWh energy and 180 $/kW power over a 20-year life at 5%) shows that more investors lower every investor's profit but raise total installed capacity, and that a small efficiency edge dominates: a single Type-1 investor at 0.95 round-trip efficiency held about 70% of the profit share, and adding low-efficiency investors raised the high-efficiency investor's share further (`2201.02290v2`, p.4, p.5).

## 7. Financialization: who captures the rent, and when the flow turns negative

The financialization paper is a stylized equilibrium, not a data study, and it is useful precisely because it states the flow direction. Producers set output over a "time to produce" and face a liquidity constraint, while financial investors trade a much shorter horizon with an information advantage; the equilibrium price clears against both (`1607.07582v1`, p.5-6). Comparative statics show a critical level of market integration below which the financial investor's expected return is negative after transaction cost, because the cost is not covered by the information rent, and above which the investor profits (`1607.07582v1`, p.12). Farmers' investment return stays roughly constant and positive, but its variance grows sharply with integration, as does the volatility of produced quantity (`1607.07582v1`, p.12-13). The default rate is approximately 30% and almost constant in integration, while the variance of the default rate rises, and the production-volatility amplification is much larger in the high-default market than in a low-default one (`1607.07582v1`, p.14). The model's stated policy conclusion is that more elastic markets tolerate larger integration, and that the propagation channel is the missing liquidity-risk hedge rather than price volatility per se (`1607.07582v1`, p.15). It is a negative result for anyone who reads commodity financialization as a one-directional volatility story: in this model it lowers price volatility while raising output and default-risk volatility.

## 8. Volatility decay and the leveraged commodity wrapper

A leveraged commodity ETF is long momentum and short variance, and the wrapper's cost is not its prospectus fee. For a beta-times-leveraged fund, the log-return carries a -(beta^2 - beta)/2 times realized variance term, so the fund decays in any high-volatility path that goes nowhere (`1610.09404v1`, p.12). Measuring 23 commodity LETFs from December 2008 to May 2013, the mean 1-day tracking error is near zero but turns increasingly negative with horizon and with the magnitude of leverage, and bear funds are penalised more than their bull counterparts (`1610.09404v1`, p.9, p.11). Replication quality separates the markets: gold and silver funds hedged through over-the-counter swaps had 1-day regression R2 below 40%, while oil-and-gas funds backed by liquid exchange-traded futures had 1-day R2 above 90% (`1610.09404v1`, p.5). The paper's realized effective fee quantifies the gap: DUG (inverse 2x oil and gas) charged 1134 bps against a 95 bps prospectus fee, SMN (inverse 2x building materials) 1625 bps, while the bull counterparts DIG and UYM had negative implied costs of -142 bps and -204 bps over the same window (`1610.09404v1`, p.16). For a research layer, the lesson is that the product's fee is not its cost and that a long-horizon leveraged-ETF return cannot be compared against a leveraged index return without the variance term.

## Caveats

Several papers here are model-only or single-market and their numbers are upper bounds, not backtests. `2112.09816v2` and `2007.00486v2` both assume perfect day-ahead foresight to pick the daily minimum and maximum, so their utilisation rates are ceilings, and neither nets capital cost into its "profit". `2201.02290v2` is a game-theoretic equilibrium with a linear price model on CAISO 2019 data and only 12 monthly scenarios. `0910.0236v3` is calibrated on one French and one British market over 2003-2008 and prices risk under the historical measure on an incomplete market. `1610.09404v1` is 23 commodity LETFs over 2008-2013, a window that contains one commodity boom and one crash, and its realized-effective-fee figures are sensitive to that path. `1610.09404v1` has no local PDF issue but is a five-year, single-cycle study. `1607.07582v1` is a stylized equilibrium with no empirical estimation, and its own limitations section concedes the model has not been tested on data. `2605.13320v1` and `2606.05991v1` are functional-data estimators of a matrix object, not P&L, and the standardization of the semigroup is not identified in general, so the measured volatility proxy is a smoothed version of the integrated variance. `2112.11338v3` is association rather than causation across six US ISOs, excludes ERCOT for data reasons, and explicitly finds no clean negative-price-versus-VRE trend. The whole slice is one arXiv category, so it over-represents what gets posted there. No paper here establishes that any of these strategies is net profitable in a live account, none prices intraday power liquidity or negative-price settlement penalties, and none covers options or prediction-market mechanics for energy. Finally, this repository has no commodity instrument: every finding above lands as a measurement, a caveat or a valuation normalization on equity research, never as an order in a commodity market.

## Papers read in depth

- `2308.00383v1` - Exploiting the dynamics of commodity futures curves (2023). Nelson-Siegel level, slope and curvature strategies on 21 commodity futures; gives the 96.5% curve fit and the decaying slope premium. Limitation: gross Sharpe reported beside net, and the edge weakens after 2000 and 2009.
- `2112.09816v2` - Potential utilization of Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) in the major European electricity markets (2021). The payoff-as-option model and the wear-cost pivot across seven European markets. Limitation: assumes perfect day-ahead foresight, so utilisation rates are upper bounds.
- `2007.00486v2` - Barriers to grid-connected battery systems: Evidence from the Spanish electricity market (2020). Break-even wear costs for four Spanish applications and the secondary-reserve band-price collapse. Limitation: single market, 2015-2019, perfect foresight.
- `2201.02290v2` - Strategic Storage Investment in Electricity Markets (2022). Non-cooperative equilibrium of competing storage investors and the efficiency-edge profit share. Limitation: linear price model on CAISO 2019 with 12 monthly scenarios.
- `0910.0236v3` - Joint Modelling of Gas and Electricity spot prices (2009). Two-factor joint model with NIG spike densities and the VaR application. Limitation: calibrated on 2003-2008 French and British data only.
- `2605.13320v1` - The fine structure of electricity price volatility (2026). Functional-data estimator of the realized covariation panel in Germany, Norway and Spain, and the absence of the leverage effect. Limitation: matrix estimands, not P&L, and the semigroup is only identified up to smoothing.
- `2606.05991v1` - Forecasting of volatility and risk premia in electricity markets (2026). Matrix-HAR forecast of the volatility panel and its use in the spread-risk-premium regression. Limitation: one zone (Germany) in the horserace and a right-skewed target the models under-react to.
- `2112.11338v3` - Role of Variable Renewable Energy Penetration on Electricity Price and its Volatility Across Independent System Operators in the United States (2021). Six-ISO quantile and skew-t study of the merit-order effect. Limitation: association not causation, ERCOT excluded, and one ISO reverses above 40% VRE.
- `1610.09404v1` - Understanding the Tracking Errors of Commodity Leveraged ETFs (2016). Volatility-decay theory and the realized effective fee across 23 LETFs. Limitation: five years (2008-2013) covering one boom and one crash.
- `1607.07582v1` - Modelling the impact of financialization on agricultural commodity markets (2016). Stylized equilibrium of producers and financial investors with a critical integration level. Limitation: theory and numerical comparative statics with no empirical estimation.
