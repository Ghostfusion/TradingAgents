# Order flow, high-frequency trading and latency: at the research layer the transferable part is order flow as a feature and the data-quality gates that decide whether that feature is measurable at all

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers the arXiv harvest on order flow, high-frequency trading and latency, read for a research and analysis layer rather than an execution layer. The slice is 29 records spanning 2008-2026, of which only 2 carry a journal reference; ten were read in depth at the PDF with a page marker on every number. Nothing here lands on a matching engine, a crate or an order gateway: TradingAgents computes offline calculators, exposes them as agent tools, gates them with `enable_*` config keys and writes a research decision. So the brief is split explicitly. The parts of the literature that only pay inside a venue or a colocation rack - sweep mechanics, ISO order marking, cancel-race latency economics, maker concentration as a quoting strategy - are reported as context and then routed to the smallest research-layer consequence that exists, which is usually a data-quality flag or a refusal. The parts that transfer are order flow as a computable feature and the measurement caveats that decide when a flow number is real.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from measured high-frequency activity in real exchange data, through order flow that extends below the best quote, into long memory in market-order flow, then into the estimator and data-quality warnings that gate every signed-flow statistic, and finally into flow regimes and the event clock. Its single most consequential finding for this repository is a split one. Order flow beyond the top of the book carries information - adding deeper levels cuts the out-of-sample RMSE of a mid-price regression by about 65-75% for large-tick and 15-30% for small-tick Nasdaq names (`1907.06230v2`, p.25-26) - but on the newer venue class the sign of the flow cannot be recovered from the feed at all: feed-inferred trade direction matches on-chain ground truth only about 59% of the time, against roughly 81% for Lee-Ready on Nasdaq (`2604.24366v2`, p.4, p.12).

That split is the operating instruction. A research layer should add multi-level imbalance to its order-book read, and should refuse any signed-flow statistic it cannot ground in a verified trade direction. Latency and venue mechanics do not transfer: the mini-flash-crash evidence (`1211.6667v1`) and the cross-venue synchronisation simulations are about how a matching engine resolves a sweep, which this repository does not do; their research-layer residue is the data-quality state they expose (locked and crossed quotes, fleeting liquidity, spread shocks) and the mechanical-volume caveat they imply for volume-based signals.

## The short answer

1. Order flow beyond the best quote is informative: adding deeper levels cuts the out-of-sample RMSE of a mid-price regression over level-1 OFI by about 65-75% for large-tick and 15-30% for small-tick Nasdaq names, and for the small-tick names more than half of all order-flow events happen beyond the best quotes (53.10% for Amazon, 53.42% for Netflix, against 29.75% for Oracle) (`1907.06230v2`, p.12, p.25-26).
2. The estimator is part of the finding: multi-level component flows are correlated (above 0.5 for small-tick and 0.7 for large-tick stocks), so multivariate OLS leaves deep-level coefficients weakly significant while Ridge makes them strongly significant, which is a warning about the estimator rather than the market (`1907.06230v2`, p.16-17, p.21).
3. High-frequency trading is the mechanism that synchronises prices: a stock's 30-second return correlation with the equal-weighted market rises with its HFT volume share (Pearson 0.80, R^2 0.64), and the timescale of cross-stock price incorporation collapsed from several minutes in 2000 to under 10 seconds in 2010 (`1211.1919v1`, p.2, p.5).
4. Mini flash crashes are a venue-rule artefact: of 5,140 crashes in four volatile months of 2008-2010, 67.85% were ISO-initiated and 86.98% of the trades inside the crashes were ISO-marked (`1211.6667v1`, p.8, p.11).
5. The crash aftermath is a durable liquidity shock and a computable data-quality state: the NBBO spread rose 141.60% and the exchange spread 188.05%, and locked or crossed NBBO quotes went from 8.01% to 24.40% and back to 8.23% (`1211.6667v1`, p.13, p.14).
6. Fleeting liquidity - a displayed quote cancelled faster than the consolidated tape could carry the removal - is present in 37.99% of the crashes, so a displayed quote cannot be assumed to be resting liquidity (`1211.6667v1`, p.17).
7. Asynchronicity is a first-order measurement problem, not a footnote: even the ten most frequently traded NYSE large-caps in 2014 have an average missing-observation probability above 0.8 at one-second sampling, and naive previous-tick correlation is biased toward zero (the Epps effect) while DCC and EWMA stay biased even after subsampling (`1803.04894v2`, p.10-13, p.15).
8. On-chain prediction markets break the signed-flow measurement: feed-inferred trade direction matches on-chain ground truth only about 59% of the time (volume-weighted 0.592) against roughly 81% for Lee-Ready on Nasdaq, and the effective half-spread sign-flips on 67% of comparable markets (window A) and Kyle's lambda on 60% (`2604.24366v2`, p.4, p.12-13).
9. Prediction-market microstructure is an order of magnitude more expensive and flatter than equities: about 400 basis points median quoted half-spread in the central price decile (1,818 basis points below 0.10), top-of-book carrying 0.136 of cumulative top-10 depth against a 0.10 uniform null, and a median of about 32 volume-weighted effective makers per market (`2604.24366v2`, p.7, p.8, p.9).
10. Long memory in market-order flow is quantitatively consistent with the Lillo-Mike-Farmer prediction gamma = alpha - 1 on the Tokyo Stock Exchange over 2012-2020, where typically about 25% of traders are order-splitters but issue about 80% of all market orders and the metaorder exponent alpha lies in (1, 2) (`2301.13505v2`, p.3-4).
11. Informed-trading detection on prediction markets is layered and blind to the canonical one-shot insider: the skill classifier needs at least ten events (3.14% classified skilled, only 44% retention out of sample) and the separate lifecycle heuristic flags 1,950 accounts with no measured precision (`2605.02287v2`, p.6, p.8).
12. High-frequency flow has regimes that are visible but window-dependent: crypto automated-market-maker log-price potentials are double-well for 2 of 3 two-month windows at sampling frequencies under an hour, and revert to single-well for windows of one month or less (`2509.02941v1`, p.5, p.7).

## 1. Order flow beyond the best quote is informative, and the estimator decides how informative

The base fact is that the best-quote order-flow imbalance explains most of the contemporaneous mid-price change, and the interesting question is whether anything deeper adds. Using LOBSTER event data for six Nasdaq stocks over all of 2016, restricted to 10:00-15:30, hidden orders excluded, and fitting 11 thirty-minute blocks times 180 ten-second windows - 2,772 regressions per stock - the level-1 order-flow-imbalance slope is significant in 100% of samples for every one of the six names, with slope t-statistics from 11.86 (Tesla) to 30.08 (Micron) (`1907.06230v2`, p.10, p.13, p.14). The economic question is the deeper levels. Comparing out-of-sample RMSE of a multi-level order-flow-imbalance vector against level-1 OFI, adding levels improves the fit by about 65-75% for the large-tick names and 15-30% for the small-tick names under Ridge, and about 60-70% and 5-35% under OLS (`1907.06230v2`, p.25-26). The raw activity split explains why: for the small-tick names more than half of all order-flow events occur beyond the level-1 quotes (53.10% Amazon, 53.42% Netflix) against 29.75% for Oracle (`1907.06230v2`, p.12). Relative tick size, not liquidity, decides where the flow lives.

The estimator is part of the finding and is the part a research layer most easily gets wrong. The multi-level component flows are collinear - the sample correlation between the first and tenth components stays above 0.5 for small-tick stocks and above 0.7 for large-tick stocks - so multivariate OLS leaves the deep-level coefficients only weakly significant, while Ridge makes them strongly significant (for Oracle the level-7 to level-10 t-statistics run 8.47-9.61, significant in 94-97% of samples) (`1907.06230v2`, p.16-17, p.21). The authors argue OLS directly overfits for some stocks. The limitation is scope: six liquid Nasdaq names, one venue, one year, and the LOBSTER feed covers only the primary venue, so the result is about that book and not the consolidated market.

## 2. High-frequency activity synchronises prices, and the crash is a venue artefact

The strongest real-data result in the slice is that high-frequency trading is what makes related securities move together. In a NASDAQ dataset that flags HFT in 120 stocks during the last week of February 2010, taking the 40 largest, the initial price response of one stock to another's move is attributed overwhelmingly to HFT activity, and the timescale of that synchronisation collapsed within a decade: several minutes in 2000, about one minute in 2005, under 10 seconds in 2010 (`1211.1919v1`, p.2). The cross-sectional evidence is the usable part: across the 120 stocks the correlation between each stock's 30-second returns and the equal-weighted average of all 120 has a Pearson correlation of 0.80 with the stock's HFT volume share, and a linear fit gives R^2 = 0.64 (`1211.1919v1`, p.5). The economic-efficiency numbers in the same paper (transaction cost, pricing error, informed profit) come from a stylized two-security Glosten-Milgrom model, not from data, so they are not a target for this repository.

The mini-flash-crash study is the operationally sharpest evidence and the clearest case of a finding with no execution-layer landing here. Defining a crash as a price moving at least 10 ticks in one direction within 1.5 seconds and by more than 0.8%, over September-November 2008 and May 2010, the paper counts 5,140 crashes: 67.85% ISO-initiated, 4.64% auto-routing-initiated and 27.51% unclassified, with ISO-marked trades at 86.98% of the trades inside the crashes (`1211.6667v1`, p.5, p.8, p.11). The average crash moves 2.45% in 413 milliseconds on 14,352.39 shares across 18.94 trades (`1211.6667v1`, p.8). The reason to care at the research layer is the aftermath and the data state it produces. In the 60 seconds after a crash the NBBO spread rises from 0.24% to 0.58% and the exchange spread from 0.46% to 1.32%; locked or crossed NBBO quotes average 8.01% before, jump to 24.40%, and fall to 8.23% after; quoted NBBO bid volume falls 19.23% and offer volume 9.35% (`1211.6667v1`, p.13, p.14, p.15). Fleeting liquidity occurs in 1,953 of the 5,140 crashes, 37.99% (`1211.6667v1`, p.17). Separately, on 6 May 2010 ISO volume was 32% of total volume but over 50% of the contribution to price variance (`1211.6667v1`, p.5), which is the paper's own statement that a mechanically large volume print is not necessarily informative.

## 3. Long memory in market-order flow, and the estimator warning that comes with it

The only journal-referenced record read in depth is a quantitative test of the Lillo-Mike-Farmer model on the Tokyo Stock Exchange. Using full order flows for all TSE stocks from 4 January 2012 to 30 December 2020, with virtual-server IDs aggregated into effective trader IDs and per-stock-per-year sign sequences as the datapoints, the paper validates the model's core prediction gamma = alpha - 1, where gamma is the exponent of the market-order sign autocorrelation C(tau) ~ c0 * tau^-gamma and alpha is the metaorder-length exponent (`2301.13505v2`, p.1, p.2). The microstructure is concentrated: typically about 25% of traders behave as order-splitters but issue about 80% of all market orders, and the metaorder exponent alpha lies in (1, 2) across markets (`2301.13505v2`, p.3). For Toyota in 2020 the aggregated metaorder-length complementary CDF obeys alpha of about 1.62 (`2301.13505v2`, p.3).

The estimator warning is as valuable as the result. The naive non-linear least-squares estimate of gamma carries finite-sample bias, so the authors construct an approximately unbiased estimator; the prefactor route to the number of splitters is only a lower bound and relies on an unrealistic homogeneity assumption that they say may need theoretical updating (`2301.13505v2`, p.3-4). The scope is a single venue: the result establishes the prediction for the TSE, not in general.

## 4. Asynchronicity and noise gate every high-frequency covariance and flow read

The most transferable methodological record measures the bias directly. At one-second sampling of the ten most frequently traded NYSE assets in 2014 (23400 seconds per day, 251 days), the average probability of a missing observation exceeds 0.8 for every name (XOM 0.816 to WMT 0.858) and the average inter-trade duration is 5.4-7.0 seconds (`1803.04894v2`, p.15). Naive previous-tick synchronisation manufactures zero returns and biases correlations downward toward zero - the Epps effect - and DCC and EWMA remain biased toward zero even after subsampling; the local-level state-space model, which treats asynchronicity as a missing-value problem and models microstructure noise as additive measurement error, is far less affected (`1803.04894v2`, p.10-13). In simulation the model beats DCC in mean-squared error in every scenario tested (`1803.04894v2`, p.13). The estimated market factor accounts for about 50% of total variance at 15:45 and the signal-to-noise ratio is U-shaped across the day, near 10 at the open and 0.5 mid-day (`1803.04894v2`, p.19). The limits are that this is ten liquid names on one venue in one year, and the model assumes a diagonal noise covariance.

## 5. On-chain venues destroy sign, so the signed-flow feature must be refused

Prediction markets are now a large enough venue class to measure at scale, and the measurement itself is the finding. The Polymarket archive covers 52 calendar days to 15 April 2026: 30,287,264,368 order-book event rows over 385,198 markets, with 255,425,405 on-chain fills matched over a 28-day window (`2604.24366v2`, p.2, p.5, p.6). The venue is hybrid - matching off chain, settlement on chain - so `change_side` in the feed records which side of the book moved, not which side initiated the trade (`2604.24366v2`, p.4). That distinction makes a whole class of signed-flow statistics unusable. Feed-inferred trade direction agrees with on-chain ground truth only about 59% of the time (volume-weighted 0.592, panel mean 0.615), against roughly 81% for Lee-Ready on Nasdaq; on the comparable market subset the effective half-spread sign-flips on 67% of markets (16 of 24) in one window and 50% in a second, and Kyle's lambda on 60% then 43% (`2604.24366v2`, p.4, p.12, p.13). After the sign is fixed from the chain, a Glosten-Harris decomposition of the top-100 markets leaves a median effective half-spread essentially at zero (-0.0003 probability points) with a median adverse-selection component of 0.0 (`2604.24366v2`, p.11).

The quoted-level facts are worth carrying into any venue model. The median quoted half-spread is about 400 basis points in the central price decile and 1,818 basis points for markets trading below 0.10, against single-digit basis points for liquid US equities post-decimalisation (`2604.24366v2`, p.7, p.8). Depth is flatter than folklore: the top level carries a median 0.136 of cumulative top-10 depth against a 0.10 uniform null, and maker concentration is low with a median HHI of 0.031, about 32 effective makers (`2604.24366v2`, p.8, p.9). Two measurement-hygiene numbers apply to any on-chain venue: archive-ingestion latency has a median of 41.5 milliseconds with a p99 of 6,108 milliseconds, which the authors are explicit is the collector pipeline rather than the exchange, and the self-counterparty wash share has a median of 0.97% with a maximum of 22.2%, an explicit lower bound (`2604.24366v2`, p.9, p.10). A cross-sectional regression that looks like a time-to-close effect is not one: the coefficient on log seconds-to-close falls from 0.818 in a bivariate specification to 0.008 in the full one (`2604.24366v2`, p.10-11).

## 6. Informed-trading detection needs layers, and each layer has a stated blind spot

The complementary record compares three detection frameworks on the same Polymarket population and is valuable mostly for what each layer cannot see. A wallet-market composite screen covers more than 210,000 wallet-market pairs and estimates about $143 million in aggregate anomalous profit (`2605.02287v2`, p.2). A sign-randomisation skill classifier over 1.72 million accounts re-runs each account's history 10,000 times with buy/sell direction randomised at the event level: 3.14% (54,477) are classified skilled winners, 6.4% unskilled losers, 29.0% lucky winners and 61.4% unlucky losers, with out-of-sample skill persistence of only 44% (`2605.02287v2`, p.6). The classifier requires at least ten events, so a one-shot informed trader is excluded by design and the flagged Maduro-cluster accounts appear as lucky winners (`2605.02287v2`, p.6, p.8). A separate lifecycle heuristic flags 1,950 accounts created within seven days before an event with no further trades after resolution, clearing $1,000 of volume and $1,000 of realised profit, with mean realised profit $15,012.92; its order imbalance predicts next-period price changes (t = 2.54) and final outcomes (t = 8.65), but its precision against any external labelled set is not reported (`2605.02287v2`, p.8). The per-market alternative is a deadline-anchored leakage score that swings 0.444 across anchoring choices on the single case study (+0.113 article-anchored against -0.331 resolution-anchored) (`2605.02287v2`, p.10). This record performs no new data collection; its numbers are quoted from third-party studies whose labels and code were not released.

## 7. Cross-asset influence rotates, and high-frequency flow has regimes

Two further records show how the periodicity and the sampling choice change the answer. Building weekly directed weighted networks from statistically significant Granger-causal links between one-minute Kraken log-returns over January 2020 to March 2025 - 275 weekly windows of 10,080 points, with 99.06% of series passing an ADF test at the 1% level and the traded universe growing from about 30 to about 390 assets - the influence structure is sublinear (out-strength scales with in-strength at an exponent of 0.909, 95% CI [0.852, 0.965]) and the leader rotates (`2606.25466v3`, p.2, p.3, p.6, p.7). ETH holds the top rank through 2020-2025 while Bitcoin declines relatively, and 17 distinct assets appear at least once among the top five over the five years (`2606.25466v3`, p.7). A representative XBT to ETH link reduces residual variance by 3.24% at an optimal lag of 2 (`2606.25466v3`, p.5-6). The leader is not a stable input, so any signal that conditions on "the leading asset" conditions on a quantity that turns over.

The regime evidence is crypto automated-market-maker flow, and the finding is a sampling warning. Applying the Kramers-Moyal expansion non-parametrically to Uniswap v3 log-prices on Arbitrum - USDC-WETH for 2024 and the first half of 2025, WBTC-WETH for 2024, each row averaged over 15 transactions with a typical step near 100 seconds and the extreme 0.5% of observations trimmed - the estimated drift is strongly non-linear and the derived potential is distinctly non-quadratic (`2509.02941v1`, p.3, p.4). Two of the three representative two-month USDC-WETH 2024 windows produce a double-well potential at sampling frequencies from 10 minutes to 6 hours, while windows of one month or less revert to a single well for all windows, which the paper attributes to insufficient tail data rather than a market property (`2509.02941v1`, p.5, p.7). The companion event-clock record maps FX ticks into a directional-change and overshoot framework with twelve thresholds starting at 0.025% and doubling, at roughly one transition every 5 seconds, and its normalised liquidity measure (H(1) = 0.4604, H(2) = 0.70818) deteriorates weeks before two known FX shocks: a complete loss of liquidity in the three weeks before the 16 August 2007 yen carry unwind and in the week before the 9 August 2011 Swiss National Bank episode (`1402.2198v1`, p.14, p.15, p.16). That evidence is two single-pair episodes argued from figures, not a formal predictive test.

## Caveats

The slice is thin and uneven. Only two of the 29 records carry a journal reference; one of them (`2301.13505v2`, Physical Review Letters) is read here, the other (`1805.06080v1`) is a law-review note on insider trading without trading data and is not read. The most on-topic record, `2608.00858v3` "Data-Driven Measures of High-Frequency Trading", has no local PDF and was skipped, which is the largest single gap. Several read papers are single-venue or single-period: the order-flow-imbalance evidence is six Nasdaq names on one venue in 2016 (`1907.06230v2`), the asynchronicity evidence is ten NYSE large-caps in 2014 (`1803.04894v2`), the long-memory test is one exchange over 2012-2020 (`2301.13505v2`), the prediction-market evidence is one venue over 52 days (`2604.24366v2`), the network evidence is one exchange over 2020-2025 (`2606.25466v3`), and the potential-shape evidence is three pools on one AMM (`2509.02941v1`). The economic-efficiency numbers in `1211.1919v1` are model-only, and `2605.02287v2` performs no new measurement, quoting third-party figures whose labels and code were unreleased. Liquidity-stress evidence in `1402.2198v1` is two FX episodes argued from figures rather than a formal test, and the crash frequencies in `1211.6667v1` describe four volatile months rather than a normal month. None of the read papers prices a strategy net of cost, so no transferable performance claim is supported. Finally, the venue-mechanics and latency findings (ISO sweeps, fleeting liquidity, cancel-race economics) have no execution-layer landing in this repository, which is a research and analysis layer; they are routed here only to their data-quality residue.

## Papers read in depth

- `1907.06230v2` - Multi-Level Order-Flow Imbalance in a Limit Order Book (2019). Shows deep flow carries information and that OLS understates it; six Nasdaq stocks on one venue in one year, LOBSTER primary-venue only.
- `1211.1919v1` - High-Frequency Trading Synchronizes Prices in Financial Markets (2012). NASDAQ HFT flags show HFT supplies most of the cross-stock response (correlation 0.80); single week, and the efficiency results are model-based.
- `1211.6667v1` - High Frequency Trading and Mini Flash Crashes (2012). The strongest venue-mechanics evidence: ISO shares, spread and quote shocks, fleeting liquidity; four volatile months only, 27.51% of events unclassified.
- `1803.04894v2` - A Score-Driven Conditional Correlation Model for Noisy and Asynchronous Data (2019). Measures the Epps-effect attenuation directly and gives the missing-value remedy; ten liquid NYSE names, one year, diagonal-noise assumption.
- `2301.13505v2` - Can we infer microscopic financial information from the long memory in market-order flow? (2023). Validates gamma = alpha - 1 on the Tokyo Stock Exchange; single venue and a model-dependent metaorder classification.
- `2604.24366v2` - The Anatomy of a Decentralized Prediction Market (2026). 30 billion order-book events and 255 million on-chain fills; establishes that feed-inferred trade direction is unusable and documents eight stylized facts; one venue, 52 days.
- `2605.02287v2` - Per-Market Information Leakage and Order-Flow Skill (2026). Taxonomy of detection layers and their scope limits; no new data, and numbers are quoted from unreleased third-party studies.
- `2606.25466v3` - Time-dependent weighted directed networks of cryptocurrency interaction from high-frequency returns (2026). 275 weekly Granger networks with FDR control; influence is sublinear and leader turnover is high; one exchange.
- `2509.02941v1` - Non-Linear and Meta-Stable Dynamics in Financial Markets (2025). Non-parametric evidence of single-well and double-well potentials in crypto AMM flow; three pools, window-dependent results.
- `1402.2198v1` - Multi-scale Representation of High Frequency Market Liquidity (2014). An event-clock directional-change stress read that degrades before two FX shocks; two single-pair episodes argued from figures.
