# Crypto venues, DeFi and perpetuals: the cost of consensus stayed pinned to transfer value while market making, liquidations and stablecoin issuance ran on tail-heavy, cost-free mechanics the research layer can already measure

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers 193 records on crypto venues, DeFi protocols and perpetuals drawn from the arXiv q-fin harvest, spanning 2012-2026, of which 40 carry a journal reference. From a 40-record shortlist I read 12 papers in depth at the PDF: the industrial economics of proof of work, constant-product and concentrated-liquidity market making, DeFi lending liquidations, the Ethereum fee market, stablecoin issuance events, a stablecoin-lending run, hard-fork arbitrage, the coin-versus-token size distribution, corporate bitcoin treasuries, and the Granger influence network of crypto returns. It is written for the research and analysis layer of TradingAgents, where crypto is a bolt-on: a Tiingo/Twelve Data price tool, a symbol normaliser, a Polymarket event feed, and a generic set of volatility, tail and covariance calculators that were built for equities. The brief's job is to say which of these venue-mechanics lessons have a concrete landing in that layer and which do not.

## What this covers

The slice runs from the physical layer upward and every step is a measured quantity rather than a mechanism description. It opens with what consensus actually costs and whether it stays stable (`2004.04605v2`, `2606.03153v1`), moves to the market-making layer where a constant-product pool is a short-convexity position (`2106.14404v1`, `2301.13009v2`) and to the credit layer where over-collateralisation fails at a measured price move (`2009.13235v6`, `2303.08748v3`), then to the priced resource market that both borrowers and lenders pay in (`2406.06524v2`), and ends on the data-quality side: what a stablecoin issuance event does to bitcoin intraday (`2501.05232v1`), what a trust shock does to a lending protocol (`2207.06285v1`), how coins and tokens differ as size distributions (`1803.03088v2`), how corporate treasuries load on bitcoin (`2505.14655v1`), and how influence is redistributed across the asset network (`2606.25466v3`).

Its single most consequential finding for this repo is a negative one. Over 2010-2020, Bitcoin's mining cost held a stable share of transferred value, about 1% in the paper's framework and 0.14% on average in the data (`2004.04605v2`, Figure 6, p.13; p.1), while the two inputs moved nine orders of magnitude. That stability is a design property of proof of work, not a tradable signal, and it does not propagate to any of the layer's calculators. The findings that do propagate are the tail and covariance ones: a constant-product liquidity position is synthetically short variance and short convexity (`2106.14404v1`, p.8), a 3% collateral price move turned over 10 million USD liquidable on one protocol after its governance token launched (`2009.13235v6`, p.13, p.18), and crypto return tails are power-law with exponents that differ by asset class (`1803.03088v2`, Table 1, p.9; `2606.25466v3`, p.5). These are the same statistics the repo already computes for equities in `tradingagents/strategies/volatility_models.py`, `tradingagents/strategies/tail_risk.py` and `tradingagents/strategies/covariance_models.py`; the lesson is that crypto is an input-rich, mechanics-heavy asset class with almost no crypto-specific producer behind those generic calculators.

## The short answer

1. Bitcoin's proof-of-work spend is pinned to transfer value: the mining-cost/transaction-volume ratio averaged 0.14% over 2010-2020 and 0.3% since end-2017, in a band of one order of magnitude while both inputs moved nine (`2004.04605v2`).
2. The same arithmetic says the protection is thin: at six-block settlement, duplicating more than about 1.5% of global transfer volume can be profitable, and the estimate is a lower bound (`2004.04605v2`).
3. Mining capacity is concentrated and its stability is not automatic: the top six pools held 89.4% of capacity and the largest about 24.6%, and restoring stability in 2022-2023 would have needed fees above $135,000 per block (`2606.03153v1`).
4. A constant-product liquidity position is short volatility and short convexity, not a yield: the corrected impermanent-loss function gives -4.29% at a 50% price drop versus -5.72% at a 2x rise, and the paper's formula is the one to use, not the commonly quoted one (`2106.14404v1`).
5. Concentrated liquidity sharpens that risk: a v2 pool loses -0.56% on a -20% move while a [75%, 125%] range loses -4.75%, so narrower ranges are steeper short-gamma positions (`2106.14404v1`).
6. On one large DeFi lender, a 3% fall in a single collateral asset's price would have turned over 10 million USD liquidable after the governance token launched, and liquidators took about 70% of such opportunities in the same block (`2009.13235v6`).
7. Protocol interventions held through a hard fork: bad debt stayed under 0.01% of total debt on Compound and under 0.04% on AAVE, while borrowers extracted more than 300,535 ETHW worth over 13 million USD (`2303.08748v3`).
8. DeFi credit risk is event-driven and detectable before publication: one protocol's pools halved in a month and most series broke 31 hours before the triggering tweet (`2207.06285v1`).
9. Ethereum gas price is modelled as a mean-reverting fractional process with a heavy right tail, and it has no tradable underlying, so it is a cost line and not a hedge (`2406.06524v2`).
10. Stablecoin issuance moves bitcoin intraday only when sentiment is positive and the event is publicly announced: 0.68% at 30 minutes per billion minted, and nothing significant for burns (`2501.05232v1`).
11. Coins and tokens are different size universes: market-share tail exponents 0.48 against 1.29, and incumbent growth of 1.52% against 0.77% per period at near-identical volatility (`1803.03088v2`).
12. Bitcoin is the informational driver of corporate-treasury equities: mean BTC beta 0.62 across 39 firms, 12 above 1, and 31.6% of rolling windows show significant BTC-to-equity information flow against 13.8% in reverse (`2505.14655v1`).

## 1. Consensus cost is pinned to transfer value, and thin

`2004.04605v2` builds a lower bound on Bitcoin's energy cost over 2010-2020 by repricing each day's hashes at the most efficient ASIC then available and at the Brent crude spot price (Eq.3, p.8). Hardware efficiency doubled every 10 months, with best-fit lambda = 0.0025 (p.8); the extremes in the hardware table are a 2007 ARM Cortex A9 at 877,193 J/Th and a March 2020 Antminer S19 Pro at 30 J/Th (Table II, p.6). Daily hashes rose from 10^15 to 10^25 between September 2010 and May 2020, in four regimes with doubling times of 33, 261, 38 and 198 days (p.9), so hashing activity grew ten billion times while energy consumption grew ten million times (p.1, p.14). Estimated daily cost went from about 3 USD in 2010 to over 4 million USD in early 2020 (p.12), and daily transferred value grew nine orders of magnitude in the same window (p.12-13). The ratio of the two has no trend: the mean is 0.14% with 10th and 90th percentiles of 0.02% and 0.4%, and 0.3% on average after end-2017 (Figure 6, p.13) - a band of one order of magnitude while the inputs moved nine. The paper's reading is that proof of work must cost a sizeable fraction of what can be stolen: at six-block settlement and a 0.3% ratio, attacks duplicating more than about 1.5% of global transfer volume could be profitable, and since the cost is a lower bound the true fraction is likely larger (p.7, p.14).

`2606.03153v1` adds the friction that makes gaps possible: miners can delay. With an on/off hash supply, a mining gap exists exactly when the largest miner finds delay optimal (Theorem 2, p.9); calibrated to Bitcoin over 2022-2023, when the top six pools held 89.4% of capacity and the largest about 24.6% (p.22) and estimated network capacity reached 496,735,701 TH/s on 31 December 2023 (p.22), the network sat near an unstable region. Using a JP Morgan estimate of about $18,000 to mine one bitcoin and an operating cost of $112,500 per block, stability in the mining-gap steady state would require transaction fees above $135,000 per block, which the authors call currently difficult to achieve (p.24, p.25). Steady-state stability itself needs mu'(w) >= -2mu/w (Theorem 4, p.14), and with a gap plus a block reward above accumulated fees no epsilon-stability exists at all (Corollary 3, p.15). None of this is calibrated to a fiat cost of capital, which the authors list as the main limitation (p.25-26).

## 2. Market making is short convexity, and concentrated liquidity sharpens it

`2106.14404v1` derives the correct impermanent-loss function for a Uniswap v2 constant-product pool on the semi-infinite domain, and shows the commonly quoted formula is wrong because it divides by the final rather than the initial portfolio value (p.5). The corrected form, epsilon = (sqrt(R) - 1) * (R + 1) / 2 with R the new/old price ratio (Eq.14, p.5), gives a -4.29% impermanent loss at a 50% drop and -8.58% at a 2x rise, against the incorrect -5.72% in both directions (p.5). The position is long both assets and sells the rising one while buying the falling one (p.6), so it is what the authors call short volatility and short convexity, earning the fee like theta and looking like a short straddle without being identical to one (p.6, p.8). The corrected risk profile always trails a buy-and-hold portfolio of the same initial assets, and interactive calculators that show otherwise are wrong (p.8). For Uniswap v3 the same derivation applies over a bounded range: a plain v2 pool loses -0.56% on a -20% move whereas a [75%, 125%] range loses -4.75% and a [50%, 150%] range -2.34% (Table 1, p.7). Outside the range the pool is 100% of one asset and the value falls linearly with it, and above the upper tick it is constant while the asset appreciates (p.7). The biggest pool at the time earned about 1.5% weekly, roughly 78% annualised (p.9), but the paper is a June 2021 practitioner note, not a peer-reviewed study.

`2301.13009v2` is the ecosystem-level measurement. Uniswap v3 had more than 6,000 pools by late 2022; the authors filter by transaction count, by both tokens trading in at least three pools, then by a one-million-USD proxy-TVL threshold reached at least twice, and end with 282 pools, from which interconnectedness filtering and a bridge-transaction analysis leave a tractable 34-pool universe for takers and 19 pools for providers over January-June 2022 (p.3-9). Swaps do not change the invariant k while mint and burn do, and the pool keeps a proportional share of each swap as the fee tier (p.2). Concentrated liquidity discretises the price space into ticks and pays a range only while the exchange rate lies inside it (p.2), and the resulting venue structure is dominated by stablecoin, wrapped-ETH and wrapped-BTC pairs (p.6). The authors cluster liquidity takers by embedding their transaction graphs and propose an "ideal crypto law" connecting liquidity, consumption and price variation, arguing that pools adhering to it are healthier venues (p.1, p.3). The take-away that survives is that a pool is a structural object with a price-range exposure, not a yield instrument.

## 3. DeFi credit is event-driven and its rate curves kink

`2009.13235v6` is the first empirical study of liquidations on a protocol for loanable funds, covering Compound from 7 May 2019 to 6 September 2020 (p.1, p.10). Despite growth in the number of suppliers and borrowers, funds stayed concentrated: the top supplier held 27.4% of supplies and the top ten 49%, while the top borrower held 37.1% and the top ten 59.9% of borrows (p.11). The launch of the COMP governance token on 15 June 2020 changed behaviour: total borrows surpassed total locked funds from July 2020 (p.12), and 2,141 accounts used leveraging spirals for over 600 million USD, roughly half of all funds supplied (p.12). The risk number is a sensitivity: after the COMP launch, a 3% decrease in the price of DAI relative to its peg would have turned collateral worth in excess of 10 million USD liquidable (p.13, p.18). Liquidator efficiency rose over time: about 60% of liquidated collateral was taken in the same block it became liquidable, 85% after two blocks, and the same-block share rose from 26% in 2019 to 70% in 2020 (p.14). The paper also documents the negative externality of a governance token: borrowers' demand for DAI to farm COMP pushed DAI off its peg, and users who had collateralised marginally above the threshold were liquidated by the very volatility the incentive created (p.16-17).

`2303.08748v3` covers the Merge on 15 September 2022 across AAVE and Compound, which together held more than 85% of the volume locked in Ethereum lending (p.3, p.5). Anticipating that holders of borrowed ETH would keep the forked ETHW as a windfall, AAVE paused ETH borrowing on 7 September while Compound capped ETH borrowing at 100,000 ETH and switched to a kinked rate curve on 10 September (p.9-10). Borrow rates still jumped by two orders of magnitude and AAVE's ETH utilization reached 100% even with lending paused (p.1, p.14), while Compound's cap held utilization below 35% and the borrow rate below 10% (p.10-12). Losses were small: bad debt stayed below 0.01% of total debt on Compound and below 0.04% on AAVE, with no liquidation spike (p.12, p.17). The profitable trade was the fork: arbitrageurs extracted over 300,535 ETHW worth more than 13 million USD (p.3), with 49,206 ETH(W) traced to exchanges from Compound borrowers alone, more than 49% of the 100,000 ETH cap (p.11). The paper also records that Compound's contract assumed 2,102,400 blocks a year while the chain produced 6,245 blocks a day, or 2,279,425 a year (p.6) - a hard-coded constant that any venue model should check.

## 4. A priced resource market with no hedge

`2406.06524v2` documents Ethereum's fee mechanism and then prices derivatives against an assumed process. The effective fee per gas unit is an algorithmically adjusted and burned base fee plus a user-set priority fee, with any excess of the max fee refunded (p.4). The block target is 15 million gas against a 30 million cap, a plain transfer costs 21,000 gas, and opcodes range from 1 gas for a JUMPDEST to 32,000 gas for a CREATE (p.6, p.8, p.9). The authors argue for multidimensional gas, replacing the single EIP-1559 base fee with several resource-specific dynamic-gas coefficients that adjust on relative congestion while the user experience stays a single fee (p.6-11). For pricing, they model the median gas price as a mean-reverting fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, borrowing the weather-derivative framework (p.14-16), and plot call values for different initial gas prices and maturities (Figure 7, p.18). The paper is explicit that the underlying is not directly tradable: there is no gas fee certificate that is investable or transferable between blocks, so the series need not be a martingale under a change of measure and cannot be replicated or directly hedged (p.17, p.19). Basis risk between expected spot and forward gas fees is named as an open extension (p.20). The measured moments in the paper are fitted to about 900 days of minute-level data, not asserted as universal (p.14).

## 5. Issuance events, trust shocks and the informational chain

`2501.05232v1` is an intraday event study over 367 Tether minting and 220 burning events from 6 October 2014 to 9 January 2021 (p.7, Table 1, p.15). One billion USD minted lifts bitcoin 0.24% over 5 minutes, 0.38% over 10, 0.51% over 15 and 0.68% over 30, fading to 0.57% at 60 minutes (p.8, Table 2, p.15). Burning coefficients are insignificant in every window and Wald tests reject symmetry at 10 to 30 minutes (p.9, Table 3, p.16). The response is state-dependent: the minting coefficient reaches 1.56 at 30 minutes under positive sentiment against 0.08 under negative sentiment using the Crypto Fear and Greed Index (Table 4, p.11, p.17), and only minting events both positively received and tweeted by Whale Alert move the price, at 0.57 to 1.12 over 5 to 30 minutes (p.12, Table 5, p.18). Whale Alert tweeted 222 of 367 mints, 60%, but only 11 of 220 burns, 5% (p.12). The price series is spliced: Bitfinex to December 2016, then Bitstamp (p.7), and the authors' own framing is the fear-of-missing-out asymmetry in which good news moves the market and bad news does not.

`2207.06285v1` studies Abracadabra.Money around the doxxing of Wonderland's treasury manager at 06:47 UTC on 27 January 2022 (p.5-6). Loans in the MIM stablecoin protocol had reached more than 3.1 billion USD against almost 5.3 billion USD of collateral by end-December 2021 (p.4); from peak to end-January the wMEMO-MIM pool fell 97% (p.5), total Ethereum pools went from 3,134.72 to 1,616.02 million MIM, a fall of 48.4%, and Avalanche pools from 116.20 to 3.11 million, a fall of 97.3% (Table 1, p.10). The UST pool, 45.7% of Ethereum pools at end-January, fell 33.9% from a high of 1,116.50 to 737.99 (p.10). Bai-Perron breakpoints in most aggregate series land 31 hours before the tweet, which the authors read as insiders acting ahead of publication; the UST pool breaks 24 and 27 hours after it (p.6-7, Table 2, p.11). MIM's own price moved less than 2 cents (p.17, p.21), so this is a trust-driven deleveraging rather than a stablecoin run, and the authors attribute it to incomplete contracts that leave human discretion in the protocol (p.3, p.8).

## 6. Tail exponents, influence networks and treasury betas

`1803.03088v2` classifies 2,499 cryptocurrencies from 28 April 2013 to 7 February 2018, of which 1,497 were still alive at the end (p.2). Coins, which run their own chain, show a market-share tail exponent between 0.5 and 0.7, while tokens issued on top of another chain sit between 1.0 and 1.3 (p.3); the maximum-likelihood estimates are 0.48 [0.39, 0.57] for coins and 1.29 [0.83, 1.75] for tokens against theoretical values of 0.50 and 1.29 from a proportional-growth model with birth and death (Table 1, p.9). Proportional growth holds in both markets, with growth rates of 1.52% [1.45%, 1.59%] for coins and 0.77% [0.68%, 0.86%] for tokens at near-identical volatility of 24.67% and 24.6% (Table 1, p.9). Token birth size grew at 1.19% and birth intensity at 0.59%, so the token market's lightness is driven by entry, not by exceptional individual growth (p.8, Table 1, p.9). The model omits correlation, fitness and forks and the window ends before the token market matured (p.5-6).

`2606.25466v3` builds time-dependent weighted directed networks from one-minute returns on Kraken over January 2020 to March 2025, in 275 non-overlapping weekly windows, with the traded universe growing from about 30 to about 390 assets (p.2). Normalised log-returns are heavy-tailed with power-law fits; the positive and negative tail exponents are 3.91 and 3.78 for XBT and 4.08 and 3.66 for XRP (p.4-5, Figure 1). Link weights and nodal out- and in-strength are heavy-tailed, and out-strength scales sublinearly with in-strength, s_out ~ s_in^0.909 with the 95% CI [0.852, 0.965] excluding one, robust to adding traded volume as a control (p.6-7). Ethereum holds the top out-strength rank throughout while Bitcoin declines, and 17 distinct assets appear in the top five at some point (p.7-8), so influence is competitive and non-persistent. Removing the first principal component leaves the structure almost unchanged, with Spearman rank correlations of 0.9990 for out-strength and 0.9947 for link weights (p.9, Figure 6). Granger causality is a predictive, not a mechanistic, relation, as the authors state (p.3).

`2505.14655v1` regresses 39 listed bitcoin-treasury firms on BTC from their first acquisition to April 2025. Average beta is 0.62 with 12 firms above 1 (p.1, p.11); Strategy (MSTR) shows beta 1.37 with R^2 0.44 while holding 528,185 BTC against a cross-section median of 1,200 BTC (p.7, p.11-12). Same-day correlation averages 0.292, but one-day lagged correlations average -0.018 and 0.018, so there is no systematic linear lead-lag (p.10, Table 2). Rolling transfer entropy gives 0.0241 bits from BTC to MSTR against 0.0191 bits back, and 410 of 1,298 windows, 31.6%, are significant in the BTC-to-equity direction against 179, 13.8%, in reverse (p.14, Figure 7). The design is effectively a single-firm case study with 38 supporting names, and the sample starts in 2023 for the detailed analysis.

## Caveats

The slice is single-venue and single-period by construction. `2009.13235v6` is one protocol over 2019-2020; `2303.08748v3` one protocol pair around one event; `2207.06285v1` one protocol over a 96-hour window; `2501.05232v1` a 220-event burning sample with a price series spliced from Bitfinex to Bitstamp; `1803.03088v2` a window ending in February 2018 before the token market matured; `2606.03153v1` a 2022-2023 calibration that converts a JP Morgan dollar cost into BTC; `2606.25466v3` the Kraken venue only; and `2106.14404v1` an unreviewed June 2021 practitioner note. Two papers model the thing they price without a market to validate against: `2406.06524v2` prices gas derivatives against an assumed fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process because no gas-derivative market exists, and `2606.03153v1` uses a two-player illustration for its sharpest instability result. `2004.04605v2` is explicitly a lower bound priced at Brent crude with hardware, maintenance and taxes excluded, so its ratio is soft and its 1.5% attack fraction is a floor. `2505.14655v1` is effectively one firm with 38 supporting names and a 2023-started detail window. Finally, the repo itself imposes a limit: the research layer has no perpetual, funding-rate or on-chain pool-state producer, so every perp-mechanics lesson in this slice lands on `no research-layer landing`, and the statistics that can be measured today are the generic volatility, tail and covariance statistics applied to crypto closes.

## Papers read in depth

- `2106.14404v1` - UNISWAP: Impermanent Loss and Risk Profile of a Liquidity Provider (2021). Corrects the impermanent-loss formula and shows the LP is short convexity; a practitioner note, not peer reviewed.
- `2009.13235v6` - Liquidations: DeFi on a Knife-edge (2021). Compound liquidation risk and liquidator speed; one protocol, 2019-2020.
- `2301.13009v2` - DeFi: data-driven characterisation of Uniswap v3 (2023). Pool triage, taker clustering and the ideal-crypto-law framing; a six-month 2022 window.
- `2406.06524v2` - Gas Fees on the Ethereum Blockchain: From Foundations to Derivatives Valuations (2024). Fee mechanism plus gas-option pricing; the derivatives side has no market to validate against.
- `2501.05232v1` - The Intraday Bitcoin Response to Tether Minting and Burning Events (2022). Clean short-window event study; small burn sample and a spliced price series.
- `2207.06285v1` - Does DeFi remove the need for trust? Evidence from a natural experiment in stablecoin lending (2022). Hourly pool-level run on a trust shock; one protocol, 96-hour window.
- `2303.08748v3` - DeFi Lending During The Merge (2023). Rates, liquidations and hard-fork arbitrage through one event; direct-exchange transfers only.
- `2004.04605v2` - The cost of Bitcoin mining has never really increased (2020). Ten-year lower bound on mining cost; the ratio result is a bound, not a level.
- `2606.03153v1` - Mind the Gap in the Mining Game (2025). Mining gaps, pool concentration and stability thresholds; one two-year calibration.
- `1803.03088v2` - Classification of cryptocurrency coins and tokens by the dynamics of their market capitalisations (2018). Power-law tails by asset type; model omits correlation, fitness and forks.
- `2505.14655v1` - Cryptocurrencies in the Balance Sheet: Insights from (Micro)Strategy-Bitcoin Interactions (2025). Treasury-holder betas and transfer entropy; effectively a single-firm case.
- `2606.25466v3` - Time-dependent weighted directed networks of cryptocurrency interaction from high-frequency returns (2026). Granger influence networks and tail exponents; one venue, 2020-2025.
