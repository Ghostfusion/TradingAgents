# Execution, impact and the order book: the research layer can consume this corpus only as a calibration target and a set of measurement warnings, because the execution engine is not where this repository lives.

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers the execution, price-impact and limit-order-book slice of a local arXiv harvest: 24 records spanning 2009-2026, six with a journal reference, all 24 screened and 12 read in depth at the PDF. The companion `strategies/books/` library was written from the arXiv `q-fin.TR` harvest and already owns the Almgren-Chriss tradeoff, the square-root impact law and the queueing view of the book; the reference extraction of this same category was written for an event-driven Rust/Python engine whose `crates/` and `python/nautilus_trader/` paths do not exist here. This document re-reads the corpus for what TradingAgents is: a deterministic, offline research and analysis layer that emits a decision validated against `contracts/research_decision.v1.schema.json`, scores it through the engine registry, and writes it to the report surface. Every take-away below therefore lands on a calculator, a tool, a config gate, a score engine, a registry or the decision contract, and any finding that is only actionable inside a matching engine is said so and routed to the smallest research-layer consequence that does exist.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from the classical statement of what price impact is, through the measurement of who actually moves the book, to the point processes that describe the feedback and the two flows where execution cost is the entire economics (DeFi liquidations). The single most consequential finding for this repository is a measurement warning, not an alpha: on Polymarket, trade direction inferred from the public order-book feed agrees with the authoritative on-chain record only about 59% of the time, and the effective half-spread and Kyle's lambda flip sign on 67% and 60% of comparable markets when feed-inferred direction is swapped for the settled direction (`2604.24366v2`, p.1, p.12-13). A research layer that computes a signed flow statistic has exactly this exposure, so the first deliverable of this brief is a rule about stating the direction source and refusing to publish a signed number when only an inferred sign exists.

The execution engine is nautilus_trader's layer, so this brief does not describe a matching engine, a queue-position model or an order gateway. What it does describe is how a cost or impact model should be calibrated, and which microstructure statistics are worth adding as features, on the six modules this repository actually has: `tradingagents/strategies/evaluate.py` (net-of-cost returns, deflated Sharpe, purged CPCV), `tradingagents/strategies/backtest_models.py` (fixed and maker-taker fees, slippage, the square-root impact model, turnover, capacity, borrow), `tradingagents/strategies/market_tradability.py` (limit-up/down, suspension, participation caps, deal-price selection), `tradingagents/strategies/liquidity_risk.py` (Amihud ILLIQ, Kyle's lambda, Roll and Corwin-Schultz/Abdi-Ranaldo spreads, days-to-absorb, ownership HHI), `tradingagents/strategies/market_session.py` (the microprice and top-of-book order-book imbalance, gap and session reads) and `tradingagents/strategies/orderflow.py` (capital-flow tiers, distribution/divergence/exhaustion, VPIN). The split is explicit: the corpus supplies the calibration constants, the diagnostic statistics and, above all, the cases in which a published number should be withheld; the research layer supplies the modules that carry them.

## The short answer

1. Impact is concave and transient, not linear and permanent: the exponent psi is about 0.1-0.3 for individual trades and rises toward 1 only when the aggregation interval reaches thousands of trades, the sign autocorrelation decays as tau^-gamma with gamma about 0.5 for stocks, and the transient decay is beta = (1 - gamma)/2 (`0903.2428v1`, p.5, p.7).
2. Spread and impact are the same compensation: the market maker's bid-ask spread is S = 2*lambda*v^psi, so the volatility per trade is expected to be proportional to the spread and the two numbers must be published together (`0903.2428v1`, p.9).
3. The book is deeper than level 1 and depth carries price information: on six Nasdaq names in 2016, adding imbalance at each of ten levels improves the out-of-sample RMSE by about 65-75% for large-tick names and 15-30% for small-tick names, with Ridge regression making the deep-level coefficients significant where OLS does not (`1907.06230v2`, p.2).
4. Order splitting is the microscopic origin of order-flow long memory: on nine years of Tokyo Stock Exchange account data, splitting traders are typically about 25% of accounts but submit about 80% of market orders, the metaorder exponent obeys 1 < alpha < 2 for about 90% of stocks, and the LMF prediction gamma = alpha - 1 is verified directly (`2308.01112v1`, p.1, p.10-11).
5. Filtering ephemeral order flow does not sharpen book-based imbalance, but filtering the parent orders of trades does: on one BANKNIFTY session the modification-time filter removed only 168,058 order ids yet eliminated about 36 million ticks, and only the trade-parent-filtered imbalance showed stronger Hawkes cross-excitation to return regimes (`2507.22712v2`, p.1, p.12).
6. Cross-asset feedback is real and the system sits near criticality: calibrated multivariate quadratic Hawkes kernels on six futures have diagonal norms all above 0.6 and largest pairwise eigenvalues of 0.73-0.90, while cross-effects are about an order of magnitude smaller than self-effects and must be separated from noise step by step (`2509.21244v1`, p.1, p.11).
7. A Hawkes LOB simulator makes an RL market maker look strong (mean PnL 13.76 and Sharpe 1.62 against 8.39/1.20 and 7.68/1.42 for the benchmarks), but only against its own synthetic book, and the Sharpe falls from 1.38 to 0.86 as arrival intensities are perturbed (`2207.09951v1`, p.5).
8. Trade-direction inference from a public order-book feed is noise-dominated: on Polymarket the feed-inferred aggressor sign matches on-chain ground truth only 59.2% volume-weighted (panel mean 0.615, 95% CI [0.58, 0.65]), the effective half-spread changes sign on 67% of comparable markets in a first window and 50% in a second, and Kyle's lambda on 60% and 43% (`2604.24366v2`, p.1, p.12-13).
9. DeFi liquidation is a same-block race and the risk is knife-edge: about 60% of Compound's liquidated collateral cleared in the block in which it became liquidable (70% in 2020 versus 26% in 2019), and a 3% DAI de-peg would have turned over 10M USD of collateral liquidable (`2009.13235v6`, p.13, p.14).
10. Across four protocols the measurement is 28,138 liquidations moving 807.46M USD through 2,011 liquidator addresses, with 73.97% paying an above-average gas price and 623 flash loans totalling 483.83M USD; a 43% ETH decline would have made 1.07B USD liquidable on MakerDAO (`2106.06389v2`, p.1, p.6-7, p.9).
11. Timestamp granularity manufactures structure: CME Globex records are accurate to one second, a single second at 11:00:00 on corn carried 806 transactions against a session mean of 4.5 records per second, and second rounding creates zero waiting times that have no continuous density (`1312.2004v1`, p.15, p.21).
12. The zero-intelligence book has an explicit mean-field baseline: a systematic BBGKY derivation gives closed expressions for the spread, instantaneous impact and diffusion constant, works well for small market-order intensity and only partially for large, and identifies an error in the 2003 Smith et al. scaling for the diffusion constant (`2510.01814v1`, p.1, p.2).

## 1. What price impact is, and what it is not

`0903.2428v1` is the compact statement of the fact this repository must not get wrong. It separates three mechanisms - agents forecasting correctly, private information being revealed, and pure order-flow fluctuation - and notes that all three produce the same positive volume-price correlation, so the mechanism cannot be read off the correlation alone (p.1-2). The linear, permanent Kyle specification is rejected on two independent grounds: the measured volume dependence is sublinear with psi about 0.1-0.3 for single trades (rising toward 1 only for thousands of trades, p.5), and a permanent-impact model forces the trade signs to be serially uncorrelated, whereas real signs decorrelate only over thousands of trades or more (p.5-7). The square-root law R = A*sigma*sqrt(v/V) is the standard metaorder form (p.6), and cross-sectional rescaling R ~ M^-0.3 * v^0.3 * F(v/M) holds for stocks at the single-trade interval (p.6). The reconciliation is the transient kernel: with sign autocorrelation C(l) ~ l^-gamma and gamma about 0.5 for stocks, the impact kernel must decay as G(l) ~ l^-beta with beta = (1 - gamma)/2 (p.7), and Gatheral's condition beta + psi >= 1 rules out price manipulation (p.7). `2510.01814v1` supplies the null against which any measured exponent should be judged: the zero-intelligence Santa Fe book admits a closed mean-field solution whose scalings for spread, instantaneous impact and diffusion constant hold well for small market-order intensity and only partially for large, and the paper corrects the diffusion-constant scaling of Smith et al. 2003 (p.1-2). The practical reading for a research layer is that a single constant impact coefficient is a statement about an aggregation interval, not about the asset.

## 2. The order book beyond level 1

`1907.06230v2` is the paper that makes depth a first-class feature. It generalises Cont-Kukanov-Stoikov order-flow imbalance to a vector over the first M price levels and fits it against the contemporaneous mid-price change on the full 2016 LOBSTER event tape for Amazon, Tesla, Netflix, Oracle, Cisco and Micron (p.10-11). With M = 10 and 10-second windows, out-of-sample RMSE improves monotonically with each added level, by about 65-75% for the large-tick names (ORCL, CSCO, MU) and 15-30% for the small-tick names (p.2). The methodological result is as important: ordinary least squares gives weak and unstable deep-level coefficients because the level imbalances are strongly collinear, and it overfits in-sample, whereas Ridge makes the deep coefficients strongly significant (p.2, p.14). The microstructure reason is in the concentration table: for the small-tick names more than half of all order flow occurs beyond the level-1 prices, while for the large-tick names the majority sits at the best quotes (p.12). `1012.0349v4` fixes the vocabulary this feature assembly needs: an order is (price, size, time), an LOB's lot size and tick size are its resolution parameters, relative prices rather than absolute prices govern order arrival rates, priority is price-time on most venues but price-size and pro-rata exist, and depth is only the displayed subset because iceberg and hidden orders are real (p.2-10). The consequence is that a top-of-book imbalance computed from a daily bar is not the statistic the paper measured.

## 3. Who moves the book: order splitting and long memory

`2308.01112v1` supplies the account-level evidence for the 2005 Lillo-Mike-Farmer model. Using the Tokyo Stock Exchange's virtual-server identifiers over nine years (2012-2020) and merging them into trading desks, it classifies traders by a one-sided binomial test on run lengths into random and splitting traders (p.3-4, p.10). Splitting traders are typically about 25% of accounts (the 10th-90th percentile band is roughly 10-50%) but submit about 80% of market orders (p.10-11), their metaorder lengths follow P(L) proportional to L^(-alpha-1) with alpha in (1, 2) for about 90% of stocks (p.11), and the headline scatterplot verifies gamma = alpha - 1 across stocks and years (p.1). The paper is explicit about the data requirement: because of the intrinsic long memory, at least an order-sign sequence longer than about 0.5 million transactions is needed for a single gamma datapoint (p.2). It also removes the ten minutes around the open and close and works only inside the continuous double auction (p.6). `1012.0349v4` documents the same clustering from the other direction: on the Paris Bourse, conditional event-class frequencies exceed unconditional ones, and Bouchaud et al. attribute the phenomenon primarily to strategic order splitting rather than to traders mimicking each other (p.10). For a research layer the consequence is that a flow-persistence feature should be reported at the metaorder scale, and the daily tier-share number the vendor supplies is not that.

## 4. Order-flow filtration: keep the right events

`2507.22712v2` asks the operational question that follows from the fleeting-order literature: does removing ephemeral, heavily modified order flow sharpen the imbalance-return relationship? On three days of BANKNIFTY January 2023 futures it applies three lifecycle filters - order lifetime, modification count, and time between successive modifications - both to the book and to the parent orders of executed trades, and scores the result with a three-step ladder of Pearson correlation, discretised-regime regression and Hawkes cross-excitation norms (p.1, p.2, p.5-10). The answer is asymmetric and is the paper's value: filtering the aggregate book does not consistently improve alignment, but applying the same filters to the parents of trades produces a trade-based imbalance with systematically stronger Hawkes cross-excitation norms from imbalance to return regimes (p.1, p.12). The scale numbers are the diagnostic: a BANKNIFTY session produces about 10^8 events; the modification-time filter removed only 168,058 order ids yet eliminated approximately 36 million ticks relative to the unfiltered stream, more than the lifetime filter, because a small subset of orders generates a disproportionate volume of rapid modification without execution (p.12). The paper is careful to say it specifies no forecasting or execution strategy and that its result is a diagnostic under matched windows (p.1, p.11). For this repository the reading is that a signed-trade series and an aggregate-book series are different measurements, and the trade series is the one worth carrying into a feature.

## 5. Hawkes feedback: calibrate self first, cross second, and distrust simulator edges

`2509.21244v1` is the calibration counterpart to the theory of feedback. It fits a multivariate quadratic Hawkes (equivalently quadratic ARCH) model non-parametrically by the method of moments on 1-minute bins from 10:00 to 15:00, for six futures and 317 S&P 500 stocks over 2013-2023 (p.3-4). The methodological innovation is a multi-step procedure that fits self kernels first and then progressively adds cross-effects, because cross-effects are an order of magnitude smaller than self-effects and must be disentangled from noise with care (p.1). It identifies cross-Zumbach effects (past trends of one asset driving the volatility of another) for the first time, a covariance-to-volatility feedback that couples past realised covariance between two assets to their future volatility, and a cross-leverage effect (p.1-2). The system is close to criticality: the diagonal Hawkes norms are all above 0.6 and the largest eigenvalues of the pairwise kernel matrices are between 0.73 and 0.90 (p.11). `2207.09951v1` is the other half: a deep RL market maker trained inside a weakly consistent multivariate Hawkes limit-order-book simulator with eight event types and the market maker's own quotes feeding back into the intensities (p.2-3). Against a symmetric and a linear-inventory benchmark it reports mean PnL 13.7567 and Sharpe 1.6193 versus 8.3913/1.1970 and 7.6772/1.4184 (p.5, Table I), and it tolerates limit-order fees up to about 0.6% while the linear benchmark turns negative at 0.4% and the symmetric one at 0.2% (p.6, Fig. 4). But when the background arrival intensities are perturbed with Gaussian noise the Sharpe falls from 1.3766 to 1.0175 to 0.8638 as the noise variance grows (p.6, Table II), and every result is Monte Carlo inside the paper's own simulator, not market data. For a research layer the pair says two things: report a self-excitation or branching ratio against 1 rather than an unbounded persistence claim, and never let a synthetic-run metric stand as evidence.

## 6. Direction inference is the weak link

`2604.24366v2` is the slice's most important negative result. On Polymarket, a continuous tick-level archive of the public WebSocket order-book feed (30.28 billion events over 52 days, 385,198 markets) joined to the authoritative on-chain OrderFilled record, it shows that trade direction inferred from the feed agrees with on-chain ground truth only about 59% of the time (volume-weighted 0.592, panel mean 0.615, market-clustered 95% CI [0.58, 0.65] over a pre-registered 600-market panel), barely above the 50% chance baseline that direction-dependent measures need (p.1, p.12). The mechanism is structural: the feed's change_side field marks which side of the book moved, not which side initiated the trade, so the aggressor sign is not recoverable from the feed alone (p.4). The propagation is the part that matters: on the comparable top-100 subset the effective half-spread changes sign between feed-inferred and on-chain directions on 67% of markets in one 7-day window and 50% in the next non-overlapping window, and Kyle's lambda on 60% and 43% (p.13). Volume-weighting strengthens the spread result to 86% in the first window. Both windows sit in or below the chance band, well short of the roughly 80% accuracy Lee-Ready achieves on Nasdaq (p.13). The other stylised facts are secondary but useful calibration: the median quoted half-spread on the central price decile is about 400 bps and rises to 1,300-1,800 bps for markets below 0.10, the L1 level carries a median 0.136 of cumulative top-10 depth against a 0.10 uniform null, and the median archive-ingestion delay is 41.5 ms with a multi-second 99th percentile (p.7, p.9). The lesson for this repository is that a signed flow statistic must name its direction source, and a signed number built on an inferred sign is not an estimate with error bars - it can be the wrong sign.

## 7. Forced execution: DeFi liquidations as the extreme case

`2009.13235v6` is the first in-depth empirical study of liquidations on a protocol for loanable funds, covering Compound from 7 May 2019 to 6 September 2020 (p.10). The market is concentrated: the top supplier holds 27.4% and the top ten 49% of supplied funds, while the top borrower holds 37.1% and the top ten 59.9% of borrows (p.11). Leverage is manufactured by looping, with 2,141 accounts running leveraging spirals for over 600M USD, roughly half of all funds supplied (p.12). The launch of the COMP governance token on 15 June 2020 changed risk appetite, and afterwards a 3% decline in the DAI price would have made over 10M USD of collateral liquidable (p.13). Liquidation itself is a race: about 60% of the 35M USD of liquidated collateral was taken in the same block in which it became liquidable, rising from 26% in 2019 to 70% in 2020, and 85% within two blocks (p.14). `2106.06389v2` widens the measurement to Aave, Compound, MakerDAO and dYdX, which together held over 85% of the Ethereum lending market at 39.88B USD of total value locked (p.1). Across the full history of the four protocols it counts 28,138 liquidations moving 807.46M USD, run by 2,011 unique liquidator addresses (p.6). The economics are winner-take-most: the most active address ran 2,482 liquidations for 741.75K USD and the most profitable made 5.84M USD in only 112 liquidations (p.6). Competition is visible in fees, with 73.97% of liquidations paying an above-average gas price (p.7), and 623 flash loans totalling 483.83M USD funded liquidations without inventory (p.9). The paper's central design criticism is that fixed-spread mechanisms over-sell collateral: close factors of 50% or 100% liquidate more than rescuing the position requires, which favours liquidators over borrowers (p.7-8). For this repository the useful transfer is the shape of the flow: a forced seller who must move a fixed fraction of available liquidity in a single interval, not a schedule spread over weeks, and a cost that concentrates in a few participants.

## 8. Clocks and timestamps: the tax on every microstructure statistic

`1312.2004v1` contributes one hard operational fact and one classification. It defines the a-b-c increments: waiting times between transactions within a session (a), price increments within a session (b), and price changes between sessions (c) (p.16). Its CME Globex example is the E-mini S&P 500 contract ESM13 across 57 sessions from 1 March to 22 May 2013, 21,364,635 records and 835,401,602 bytes, averaging 374,818 records per session (p.15). The load-bearing point is granularity: records carry time accurate to one second, the session mean is only 4.5 records per second, yet a single second at 11:00:00 on the corn contract carried 806 transactions over a [630.00, 638.75] price range, worth 437.50 USD per contract before about 10.66 USD per round trip in fees (p.15). Truncating to the second maps every sub-second gap below about half a second to a zero a-increment, creating an apparent point mass at zero that no continuous waiting-time density can fit (p.21). The consequence for a daily-bar research layer is severe in the other direction: this repository has no sub-second tape at all, so it cannot compute a waiting-time, intensity or intraday-toxicity statistic that depends on event spacing, and any such number it does publish must state the sampling grid it came from.

## Caveats

Half the slice is thin on data or narrow in scope, and the numbers above inherit those limits. `0903.2428v1` and `1012.0349v4` are reviews with no new dataset; the survey's stylised facts span many venues and years and are cited as vocabulary, not as a measurement. `2510.01814v1` and `2207.09951v1` are theory and simulation respectively - the Santa Fe scalings are derived rather than fitted and are explicitly weaker for large market-order intensity, and the RL market maker's edge exists only inside its own reduced-form Hawkes book. `2308.01112v1` is one market (Tokyo) over nine years and needs very large samples for a single gamma estimate; `1907.06230v2` is six Nasdaq names in one year on one venue; `2507.22712v2` is three days of one Indian index future and is diagnostic rather than predictive; `2509.21244v1` is intraday only and its leverage kernels are acknowledged to operate mainly at the daily scale; `1312.2004v1` is one exchange and an idiosyncratic collection. `2604.24366v2` is a single venue over 52 days, and its central sign-flip rates swing by about 17 percentage points between two 7-day windows, so the headline is window-sensitive even though the methodological conclusion is not. `2009.13235v6` covers one protocol (Compound) to September 2020 and `2106.06389v2` four protocols to April 2021; both are pre-2022 DeFi and speak to a liquidation landscape that has since changed. Finally, several findings have no research-layer landing by construction: queue position, order-type priority and the mechanics of a matching engine are execution-layer facts, and this repository has no matching engine, no order gateway and no sub-second tape, so a "where it lands" consequence can only be the smallest research-layer one - a coverage flag, an unavailable return or a calibration constant in the offline cost model - not a live execution behaviour.

## Papers read in depth

- `0903.2428v1` - Price Impact (2009). Concave transient impact, the square-root metaorder law, the transient kernel beta = (1 - gamma)/2 and the spread-impact identity S = 2*lambda*v^psi; a review with no new data.
- `1012.0349v4` - Limit Order Books (2010/2013). The formal vocabulary (orders, lot and tick resolution parameters, relative prices, priority mechanisms, hidden and iceberg orders); a survey, not a measurement.
- `1312.2004v1` - Optimal Trading Strategies as Measures of Market Disequilibrium (2013). The a-b-c increment classification and the one-second timestamp defect on CME Globex data; one exchange, idiosyncratic in structure.
- `1907.06230v2` - Multi-Level Order-Flow Imbalance in a Limit Order Book (2019). Depth improves imbalance fits and Ridge beats OLS, 65-75% RMSE gain for large-tick and 15-30% for small-tick names; six Nasdaq stocks, one year.
- `2009.13235v6` - Liquidations: DeFi on a Knife-edge (2020/2021). First empirical Compound liquidation study, the same-block race and the 3% DAI de-peg; single protocol, pre-2022.
- `2106.06389v2` - An Empirical Study of DeFi Liquidations: Incentives, Risks, and Instabilities (2021). Four-protocol measurement and the over-liquidation design critique; ends April 2021.
- `2207.09951v1` - Deep Reinforcement Learning for Market Making Under a Hawkes Process-Based Limit Order Book Model (2022). An RL market maker in a weakly consistent Hawkes simulator; entirely synthetic.
- `2308.01112v1` - Quantitative statistical analysis of order-splitting behaviour of individual trading accounts in the Japanese stock market over nine years (2023). Direct verification of gamma = alpha - 1; one market, very large samples required.
- `2507.22712v2` - Order-Flow Filtration and Directional Association with Short-Horizon Returns (2025). Lifecycle filters and a three-step diagnostic ladder; three days, one contract, diagnostic not predictive.
- `2509.21244v1` - Multivariate Quadratic Hawkes Processes - Part II: Non-Parametric Empirical Calibration (2025). Cross-Zumbach, covariance-to-volatility and cross-leverage kernels across futures and stocks; intraday only.
- `2510.01814v1` - Mean-field theory of the Santa Fe model revisited (2025). A systematic BBGKY derivation and closed scalings, plus the correction to Smith et al. 2003; theory with no empirical data.
- `2604.24366v2` - The Anatomy of a Decentralized Prediction Market: Microstructure Evidence from the Polymarket Order Book (2026). The ~59% direction-inference agreement and its propagation to spread and Kyle's lambda; one venue, 52 days, window-sensitive.
