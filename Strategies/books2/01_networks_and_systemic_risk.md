# Financial networks, contagion and systemic risk: the network of direct exposures is a weak standalone channel that switches on only when it meets a common-asset shock, and every network statistic worth publishing is the one read against a heterogeneous null

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers the financial-network and systemic-risk slice of a local harvest of the arXiv q-fin literatures: contagion, cascades, interbank topology, connectedness and correlation networks. The slice is the 322 records whose title or abstract touches interbank networks, contagion, cascades, systemic risk or connectedness; 40 were shortlisted and 12 were read from the PDFs. It is written for a research and analysis layer, not for an execution engine: the landing places are the deterministic calculators under tradingagents/strategies, the agent tools that expose them, the score engines and their gates, and the decision contract. This repository has no interbank exposure graph, so the interbank half of the slice routes to the smallest research-layer consequence that does exist, and says plainly where a finding has none.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from the descriptive topology of national interbank markets, through closed-form cascade thresholds and multiplex layers of maturity and seniority, to market-based networks built from correlations, tail dependence and variance decompositions. Its single most consequential finding for this repository is that the network of direct bilateral exposures is, on its own, a weak contagion channel - the Austrian stress tests put the affected fraction in the low single-digit percent - but it becomes a powerful amplifier once a common asset shock is added, and whether it amplifies is set by the concentration of debt versus credit at the hubs `1306.3704v1` `1410.2549v1`.

The second most consequential finding is methodological and it lands almost everywhere. The same topological statistic can be invisible or predictive depending only on the benchmark it is compared to: Dutch dyadic motif z-scores jump abruptly in 2008 against a homogeneous random graph and show a three-year build-up from 2005Q1 against a degree-preserving configuration model `1302.2063v4`. The repository already owns the machinery for this - a correlation matrix, its shrinkage, a Marchenko-Pastur null band and an eigenspace rotation read - and the slice says to route every correlation-network signal through it.

## The short answer

1. Direct interbank exposures are a weak standalone contagion channel, but they amplify overlapping-portfolio losses: in the Austrian network the interaction multiplies the bankruptcy fraction in a specific overlap regime `1306.3704v1`.
2. Contagion is non-monotone in connectivity; the turning point, the critical degree, is a closed-form function of interest rates, leverage and liquidity, and with realistic parameters it is small, about 5 to 10 `1402.4783v2`.
3. Debt concentration, not link count alone, sets cascade size: the network whose hubs are large debtors cascades to 0.99 against 0.49 when the hubs are large creditors `1410.2549v1`.
4. Topology carries early-warning information only against a heterogeneous null: the Dutch network diverges from its configuration-model expectation from 2005Q1, three years before the 2008 collapse, and shows nothing against a homogeneous random graph until the crisis is already visible `1302.2063v4`.
5. Homogeneous or randomized interbank simulations overstate fragility: randomizing bank sizes and loan weights inflated the average cascade 1.4x to 2.5x above the empirical Italian network `1310.1634v2`.
6. Aggregating loan maturities destroys mesoscale structure: complete maturity aggregation is always rejected for the Russian interbank network, and the optimal granularity lies strictly between aggregation and full separation `1906.08617v1`.
7. Distress can propagate without any default: mark-to-market valuation of interbank claims creates endogenous volatility clustering and a down-market effect, and yields lower solvency probabilities than static clearing `2211.15431v4`.
8. A node's systemic importance depends on the prevailing level of risk: risk-dependent centrality reduces to degree at low risk and to eigenvector centrality at high risk, so rankings can interlace with no change in topology `1907.07908v4`.
9. Correlation built on Pearson coefficients misses the tail: negative-tail local Gaussian correlation is statistically larger than Pearson and the negative-tail network shrinks its average shortest path most in the 2015-2016 Chinese crash, which Pearson does not flag `2510.21165v1`.
10. Anti-correlation is not noise: up to 43% of stock pairs are negatively correlated, the anti-correlation network is scale-free while the positive-correlation network is not, and only market declines move its topology, not rises `2404.00028v2`.
11. A variance decomposition is a weighted, directed network adjacency matrix, total connectedness is its mean degree, and directional connectedness is in- and out-degree `2211.04184v2`.
12. Option-implied good fears and bad fears form two asymmetric networks: aggregate fear connectedness was 57.61% over 2000-2017, Goldman Sachs was the largest transmitter and Bank of New York Mellon the largest receiver, and the good-fear network is stronger than the bad-fear network except in recessions `1810.12022v2`.

## 1. What a financial network is measurable as

The descriptive papers agree on a small set of stylized facts. The Dutch interbank network is built from prudential reports of exposures above 1.5 million euro with maturity up to one year, at quarterly frequency, giving 44 snapshots from 1998Q1 to 2008Q4 with 91 to 102 banks in each (p.2). The Austrian dataset covers 12 quarters from 2006 to 2008 with 825 to 846 banks per quarter (p.4). The Italian e-MID overnight market is reconstructed as 257 daily netted-exposure networks, averaging 128 nodes and 355 links (mean degree 5.5) in 2006 and 70 nodes and 161 links (mean degree 4.5) in 2011 (p.4). The Russian market is unusually granular: 2.4 million individual loan contracts over 57 monthly networks from January 2000 to October 2004, preassigned into eight contiguous maturity classes (p.2).

The distributions are heavy-tailed but not clean power laws. Austrian total assets follow a power law with exponent about 0.74 to 0.75 over roughly three orders of magnitude, liquid assets about 0.67, with a flat small-bank regime below about 60 million euro and a cutoff above about 35 billion euro (p.5). The correlation-based stock networks carry the same shape: after Generalized Pareto fits, the tail-shape parameter of strength centrality is positive and below 1 for almost all anti-correlation windows and negative for all positive-correlation windows, so the anti-correlation network is scale-free in strength and the positive-correlation network is not (p.4). For the Chinese market the minimum pairwise correlation reaches -0.92, 90% of 387 rolling windows have a minimum below -0.5, and the probability of an anti-correlation in a window is as high as 0.43 (p.2).

The market microstructure of these networks is stable enough to be a signal only in a relative sense. Reconstructing a correlation network from 1,542 Shanghai stocks over ten regime periods, the node centrality and entropy readings are dominated by market state rather than by a fixed structure: the smallest entropy of the negative-tail network appears in period seven (2015-2016 turbulence) while the smallest entropy of the Pearson network appears in period eight, so the two disagree about when the system was most fragile (p.6).

## 2. The exposure network is weak alone and an amplifier with a common asset

`1306.3704v1` is the paper that most changes how a stress test should be built. Its Austrian stress tests run every bank as a seed under three mechanisms: counterparty loss, roll-over risk and overlapping portfolios. Under counterparty loss and roll-over risk considered in isolation, the contagion probability is a few percent and the conditional extent of contagion is small, because a prudent bank can cap its exposure to any single counterparty at its own equity (p.12). Once banks share a common asset, the picture changes. If every bank holds a fraction c of a common asset and that asset depreciates, the fraction of banks going under reaches 30% or more at c of about 0.05 (p.14), and adding the interbank network on top produces a discontinuity rather than a smooth increase.

`1410.2549v1` separates connectivity from concentration using directed preferential-attachment networks of 1000 nodes whose degree exponents land between 2.2 and 3.2 (p.6). The aggregate default impact is close across the debt-concentrated, credit-concentrated and symmetric variants, 0.48, 0.46 and 0.43 (p.12), but the aggregate default cascade separates sharply: 0.99 for the debt-concentrated network against 0.79 symmetric and 0.49 for the credit-concentrated one (p.13). The lesson is that the direction in which the hubs are concentrated - large debtors versus large creditors - is a distinct risk factor from raw connectivity.

`1310.1634v2` supplies the null-model discipline. Building the Italian e-MID cascade on empirical size and weight distributions and then on randomized variants, it finds that simulations assuming homogeneity for size of banks and loan contracts dramatically overestimate the fragility of the interbank market, with the average-cascade inflation running 1.4x to 2.5x (p.1, p.9). Network structure matters mainly in fragile markets, and homogeneous stand-ins are not a conservative approximation - they are a wrong one.

## 3. Cascade thresholds in closed form

The most reusable analytical result in the slice is the critical degree of `1402.4783v2`. With a mean-field model of interbank lending parameterised by the external interest rate R, the interbank rate r, the liquidity ratio f and the leverage ratio Lambda, the first critical degree is k*(1) = [ r(1-f) - max(r(1-f) + 2 Lambda - 1, 0) ] / [ (R-1)(1-Lambda) + Lambda ] (p.4). Below that degree an individual shock can propagate to the shocked bank's neighbours; above it, losses are diluted and the neighbours survive (p.4). The critical degrees are strictly decreasing functions of both the liquidity ratio f and the leverage ratio Lambda, so lower liquidity and higher leverage both enhance systemic risk (p.5). The second critical degree is smaller than the first, so failures hardly extend beyond first neighbours.

The empirical calibration matters more than the formula. With realistic parameters the critical degree is about 5 to 10, while the FedFunds market had a mean degree near 15 with almost half of banks below 4 (p.9). This repository has no interbank exposure graph and cannot compute either side of that comparison, so the formula has no landing here beyond recording the channel as absent. The honest statement is that the exposure-network literature is reference material: it fixes what a research layer would have to store (a directed, dated exposure matrix with leverage and liquidity per node) if it were ever to test these thresholds.

## 4. Early warning is a deviation from a heterogeneous null

`1302.2063v4` is the cleanest demonstration that the same statistic can be useless or predictive depending on the benchmark. Size, link count and connectance show no significant change at the onset of the crisis (p.2). Against a directed random graph, the dyadic motif z-scores jump abruptly in 2008 with no preceding build-up: the network can be seen, but not foreseen (p.3, p.4). Against the directed configuration model, which preserves each bank's observed in- and out-degree, the picture inverts: z-scores are stationary and below 2.5 in absolute value for quarters 1 to 28 (1998-2004), then change sign and drift from quarter 29, 2005Q1 (p.5). The transition is a three-year pre-crisis phase, not a jump (p.5).

The earliest precursor is a motif, not a summary statistic. From 2000Q3 to 2004Q4 an excess of unreciprocated three-bank lending loops (triadic motif 9) is strongly over-represented, and when the pre-crisis phase begins that same motif flips to the strongest under-represented pattern (p.5, p.6). A node-level reading of the same idea is `1907.07908v4`: the risk-dependent centrality of node i is the i-th entry of e to the power beta A times the all-ones vector, and its limits are degree centrality as beta goes to zero and eigenvector centrality as beta goes to infinity (p.10, p.11). Between those limits two nodes can swap ranking purely because the external risk level changed, with no topology change (p.1). On an S&P 100 asset network built from daily returns from January 2001 to December 2017, the rankings are highly volatile during 2007-2008 (p.2).

## 5. Multiplexity: maturity and seniority are not details

`1906.08617v1` shows that collapsing maturity layers discards real structure. Fitting a coarse-grained layered stochastic block model to the 57 monthly Russian networks, it finds the optimal maturity granularity always lies strictly between complete aggregation and complete differentiation, so the eight reporting classes are partly redundant but aggregation is always rejected (p.4). The number of statistically significant lending-pattern bins rises with market development, and the short layers below 30 days carry 97% of issued loans while the long layers carry a few percent of issuance but sizeable economic weight in outstanding loans (p.5). Layer non-homogeneity holds for most topology measures, so a single collapsed network or a single extracted layer is an incomplete representation.

`1501.05400v3` makes the same argument for seniority, using a two-layer multiplex network of junior and senior loans and a multilevel threshold-cascade condition. Self-interested banks would prefer all loans senior, but the all-senior network is as vulnerable as the all-junior one; an intermediate mix minimizes the set of network densities for which bankruptcies spread widely (p.5). For uncorrelated Erdos-Renyi networks the optimal seniority ratio is 1.79, that is on the order of 50% to 100% more senior than junior loans (p.5), and it rises as the junior-default threshold falls (p.8). For this repository both papers have no research-layer landing: there is no loan contract record and no seniority layer to preserve.

## 6. Distress without default, and the term structure it bends

`2211.15431v4` changes the accounting rule rather than the topology. Interbank claims are marked to market at the discounted expected payment, so a bank's own balance sheet depends on the market's conditional probability that its counterparties survive to maturity (p.3). This makes the clearing problem forward-backward: today's capital requires future default probabilities, and the solution is a fixed point in the solvency-probability vector (p.3). The economic consequence is that the network generates an endogenous stochastic-volatility term in equity returns, with clustering and a down-market effect - volatility rises when capital falls and the effect is stronger the more stressed the system (p.4). Because of network effects, spikes in volatility hit firms regardless of the riskiness of their own book, so long as their counterparties have a chance of defaulting (p.4). With multiple maturities the model also produces an interbank term structure whose inversion can arise solely from the riskiness of other banks in the system (p.4).

`2608.13979v1` supplies the opposite sign under a different institutional assumption. If interbank debt is long-term and can be diluted by new senior borrowing, a shocked bank can pledge its interbank claims as collateral and raise liquidity; high indebtedness and dense connections then transfer liquidity from healthy to shocked banks and are stabilizing (p.1). Netting, which improves stability when debt is non-dilutable, undermines it when debt is dilutable (p.3). This is a warning against stating a universal sign for connectivity: the sign is a property of the debt contract, not of the graph.

## 7. Market-based networks: correlation, tail dependence and anti-correlation

The papers that land most directly on this repository are the ones that build a network from returns, because the repository already owns a Pearson correlation matrix (`tradingagents/strategies/statistical.py::correlation_matrix`), a shrinkage covariance, a Marchenko-Pastur null band and an eigenspace rotation read. `2510.21165v1` argues the Pearson matrix is the wrong object for tail risk: it is non-Gaussian, non-robust to outliers and blind to nonlinear dependence, whereas the local Gaussian correlation captures local nonlinear dependence and handles heavy tails (p.1). On 1,542 Shanghai stocks over 3,639,462 closing-price records from 2004-04-07 to 2019-12-31, the local Gaussian correlation among negative tails is statistically larger than among positive tails, the negative-tail network has a smaller average shortest path than the Pearson network in almost all ten periods, and its entropy is smaller, meaning worse resilience (p.4, p.6). The Pearson network cannot capture the risk of a crash: the smallest negative-tail entropy appears in the 2015-2016 turbulence while the smallest Pearson entropy appears a period later (p.6).

`2404.00028v2` makes the complementary point for the sign of the correlation. Studying 5,329 Shanghai and Shenzhen stocks over 5,816 trading days and 13,687,721 records from 2000 to 2023, in 387 rolling windows of 26 trading days, it finds the anti-correlation network is scale-free in strength while the positive-correlation network is not, that the anti-correlation assortativity is strongly negative, and that market declines move the anti-correlation topology (clustering up, shortest path up) while market rises do not (p.4, p.6). The asymmetry between decline and rise is itself the finding: collective behavior strengthens on the way down and not on the way up (p.6). A single positive-correlation matrix discards the anti-correlation half of the information.

## 8. Connectedness as a variance decomposition, and good versus bad fears

`2211.04184v2` is the retrospective on the Diebold-Yilmaz program. Its central insight is that a variance decomposition matrix is the adjacency matrix of a weighted, directed network (p.4): total directional connectedness from others to i is the in-degree of node i, total directional connectedness to others from j is the out-degree of node j, and total system-wide connectedness is the mean degree of the network (p.4, Table 1). In high dimensions the tabular presentation fails and network graphics carry the understanding instead (p.5). The construction rests on a VAR approximating model, so no structural mechanics have to be assumed.

`1810.12022v2` applies that machinery to option-implied fears for ten major U.S. banks over 4,528 daily observations from 2000-01-03 to 2017-12-29. It splits implied variance into a call side (good fear) and a put side (bad fear) and builds two directed networks. Aggregate fear connectedness over the whole sample is 57.61%; Goldman Sachs is the largest transmitter (TO connectedness 108.85%) and Bank of New York Mellon the largest receiver (FROM connectedness 70.08%), with the strongest pairwise link Goldman Sachs to Morgan Stanley at 20.29% (p.15, Table 1). Good-fear connectedness is stronger than bad-fear connectedness for almost the entire period, with a correlation of about 90% between the two, but bad-fear connectedness rises during recessions and the asymmetric fear connectedness is a candidate monitoring tool (p.18, p.19). This is a forward-looking, market-based complement to the exposure networks that the rest of the slice studies.

## Caveats

Most of the empirical evidence is single-country and single-period: the Dutch network is 44 quarters to 2008 `1302.2063v4`, the Austrian network is 12 quarters of 2006-2008 `1306.3704v1`, the Russian market is 2000-2004 `1906.08617v1`, e-MID is 2006 and 2011 only `1310.1634v2`, the Chinese bank recurrence network is 2012-2020 `2310.10283v1`, the Shanghai tail-correlation study is 2004-2019 `2510.21165v1`, the A-share anti-correlation study is 2000-2023 `2404.00028v2`, and the option-fear network is ten U.S. banks over 2000-2017 `1810.12022v2`. Three papers in the read set have no data at all and are models: the critical-degree paper `1402.4783v2`, the dynamic distress-contagion paper `2211.15431v4` and the debt-dilution paper `2608.13979v1`; a fourth, the multiplex-seniority paper `1501.05400v3`, is cited here at reference level rather than read in depth. Their results are conditional on their assumptions. Several correlation-network papers are single-author and single-market and their findings are stated at the strength one national market supports, not as laws. The connectedness and centrality results are essentially descriptive and reduced-form; none of them is a costed trading rule, and none accounts for transaction costs, borrow or capacity. Finally, the repository has no interbank stress engine, so the closed-form cascade, maturity-layer and dilution findings have no research-layer landing and are recorded as reference material rather than as producers to build.

## Papers read in depth

- `1306.3704v1` - How interbank lending amplifies overlapping portfolio contagion: A case study of the Austrian banking network (2013). The strongest finding in the slice: direct exposure is weak alone but amplifies a common-asset shock to a 30%-plus bankruptcy fraction at c about 0.05; one country, 12 quarters.
- `1402.4783v2` - Mapping systemic risk: critical degree and failures distribution in financial networks (2014). Closed-form critical degree and fat-tailed failure distribution; a mean-field model with no data, and no landing here.
- `1302.2063v4` - Early-warning signals of topological collapse in interbank networks (2013). Configuration-model divergence from 2005Q1 and the unreciprocated-loop precursor; one network over one crisis, but the null-model lesson is general.
- `1310.1634v2` - Cascades in real interbank markets (2013). Empirical e-MID cascades and null-model comparison showing homogeneous simulations overstate fragility by 1.4x to 2.5x; two years only.
- `1906.08617v1` - Loan maturity aggregation in interbank lending networks obscures mesoscale structure and economic functions (2019). Maturity layers carry economic function and must not be collapsed; one market, 2000-2004.
- `2211.15431v4` - Endogenous distress contagion in a dynamic interbank model (2022). Mark-to-market valuation, endogenous volatility and interbank term structures; theory and small numerical examples.
- `1907.07908v4` - Risk-dependent centrality in economic and financial networks (2019). Centrality as a function of the external risk level, with ranking interlacement; illustration on two networks.
- `2310.10283v1` - Unveiling Early Warning Signals of Systemic Risks in Banks: A Recurrence Network-Based Approach (2023). High-frequency recurrence indicator for bank crises; 16 banks, 12 events at a 0.001 level.
- `2510.21165v1` - The local Gaussian correlation networks among return tails in the Chinese stock market (2025). Negative-tail local Gaussian correlation is more sensitive than Pearson; one market, ten periods.
- `2404.00028v2` - Anti-correlation network among China A-shares (2024). Separates positive from anti-correlation and shows the asymmetry between decline and rise; one market, 24 years.
- `2211.04184v2` - On the Past, Present, and Future of the Diebold-Yilmaz Approach to Dynamic Network Connectedness (2022). The variance-decomposition-as-network statement and its history; a methods retrospective with no new data.
- `1810.12022v2` - Asymmetric Network Connectedness of Fears (2018). Option-implied good and bad fear networks for ten U.S. banks; forward-looking but sample-bound to 2000-2017.
