# ESG, climate and sustainability: the measurable ESG signal is inter-rater disagreement, and this research layer holds no ESG, climate or carbon surface to carry it

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads ten papers from the arXiv harvest on the ESG, climate and sustainability slice: 79 matching records published between 2010 and 2026, 16 of them with a journal reference. The slice runs from econophysics permit allocation and carbon-economy modelling (2011-2012) through transition-risk asset pricing and ESG fund performance (2018-2022) to firm-level climate stress testing and rating-disagreement measurement (2024-2026). It is written for an operator deciding what a research-and-analysis layer should compute, so every finding ends at a calculator module, an agent tool, a config gate, a score engine or an evidence surface - or at an explicit statement that no such surface exists.

## What this covers

The arc of the slice is a shift from treating ESG as a score to treating it as a measurement problem with a named failure mode: aggregate confusion. Early papers allocate emissions permits by maximum entropy and model the carbon economy as a physical system; the recent papers ask what a rating actually measures, whether raw disclosure fields beat the composite, and how far a carbon price propagates through a supply chain. The single most consequential finding for this repository is that the choice of ESG data provider is not a detail: on the identical 200-firm European sample, replacing the CDP climate score with the LSEG environmental pillar score eliminates the flagship-index-membership effect on the disclosure-performance gap while the renewable-energy effect survives, so the detected "greenwashing" is conditional on the rater (`2606.31469v1`, p.1).

The second most consequential finding is a negative one for this project. A repository-wide search finds no ESG, climate or carbon surface at all: no calculator, no tool, no config gate, no score component. The word "esg" appears in exactly one Python file, `tradingagents/strategies/universe_factors.py`, and only in a docstring that records the deliberate absence. The transferable content of this slice therefore lands on adjacent surfaces that do exist - the text-divergence calculator, the news engine's regulatory/industry legs, the book-risk stress machinery, the sector-driver table and the fundamentals tools - and on the honest record that the ESG category itself is an unfilled gap.

## The short answer

1. ESG ratings are measured, not observed, and they disagree structurally: inter-agency correlations frequently fall below 0.60, against roughly 0.99 for credit ratings (`2606.31469v1`, p.2).
2. The determinants of a firm's disclosure-performance gap flip when the rating provider is swapped, so any ESG signal must be tested per provider before it is trusted (`2606.31469v1`, p.1).
3. The gap itself is quantifiable: for 200 large European firms in fiscal 2023 it has standard deviation 1.267 and range -3.22 to +5.28, and materiality runs from roughly 55% index members to roughly 91% TCFD supporters (`2606.31469v1`, p.11, p.12).
4. Aggregated ESG scores throw away the risk signal: selected raw LSEG variables explain Energy-sector log return volatility at %dev 0.51 versus 0.39 for plain volatility, and beat the model built from all ten pre-aggregated category scores (`2508.18679v2`, p.3, p.5).
5. That signal is additive to financial factors: Energy %dev rises from 0.32 (financial factors alone) to 0.51 (selected ESG variables) to 0.62 (combined) (`2508.18679v2`, p.13).
6. Relevant ESG risk variables are size- and sector-specific: environmental factors dominate for large-cap Energy firms while community and shareholder-rights factors dominate for small-cap Energy firms (`2508.18679v2`, p.14, p.15).
7. ESG best-in-class filtering is not alpha: it produced small but negative excess returns and negative information ratios across MSCI World regions, and only 12 of 40 World Developed sector/metric portfolios had positive excess return (`2002.07477v2`, p.5, p.6).
8. Non-linear learning from the same ESG features did produce alpha - +2.76% annualised over Jan 2013 to Mar 2018 - but the learned rules decayed to zero or negative within about a year without retraining (`2002.07477v2`, p.14, p.17).
9. ESG fund protection is crisis-specific: in 2005-2008 the top-ESG tertile of 73 US funds had monthly return -0.32% and Sharpe 0.56, versus 0.30 for the lowest tertile; outside the crisis the lowest-ESG funds won (`1806.09906v2`, p.7).
10. Climate transition risk is a forward-looking, state-dependent shock: anticipation of a technology-driven transition produces a "run on fossil fuel" with accelerated extraction and falling spot prices, and the empirical oil-market response is significant mainly over 2009-2019 (`2410.00902v1`, p.1, p.4).
11. Carbon pricing is small direct and large through contagion: at the EU ETS II cap of 45 EUR/t direct losses are about 1.3% of sales and 1.2% of bank equity, but supply chains amplify them, reaching 5.3% of output under substitution and past 50% of output at 30 EUR/t when a systemic firm fails (`2503.10644v1`, p.4, p.6).
12. News-based ESG scoring imitates one rater and is coarse: a DistilBERT relevance classifier reaches 83% accuracy, but predicting the exact 0-100 provider score as 100 classes reaches 2-3%, and the best regression sits about 12 to 13 rating points away (`2212.11765v1`, p.6, p.7).

## 1. Ratings disagree, and the disagreement is the measurement

The founding problem of this slice is that ESG is a proxy. `2606.31469v1` reports that environmental ratings from different agencies frequently correlate below 0.60, against roughly 0.99 for credit ratings (p.2), and shows that this "aggregate confusion" reappears inside a single estimate: the determinants of greenwashing are conditional on the rating lens applied (p.1). `2508.18679v2` shows why the underlying database is hard to use. The LSEG structure is explicitly hierarchical, with 663 raw variables aggregating into 10 category scores, then 3 pillar scores, then one overall score (p.4), and the provider expanded its collected raw variables from 400 in 2017 to 600 in 2023 largely as a marketing exercise, leaving the panel sparse and the newly added columns unusable for older years (p.5). For the Energy sector alone the processed panel falls from 617 variables and 695 company-years to 255 variables and 422 observations (p.4). The operational consequence is direct: a single "ESG score" field is not a sufficient statistic, and any research layer that stored one column would be storing an editorial decision by a vendor, not a measurement.

## 2. What predicts the talk-walk gap: symbolic versus costly signals

`2606.31469v1` gives the cleanest falsifiable result in the slice. It defines a Disclosure-Performance Gap (DPG) as the within-sector standardised distance between a firm's voluntary CDP disclosure and its realised Scope 1+2 emissions intensity per unit revenue (p.1, p.8). Across the 200 large STOXX Europe 600 firms in Energy, Materials, Industrials and Utilities in fiscal 2023, the gap has standard deviation 1.267 and ranges from -3.22 to +5.28 (p.11). Four predictors survive a six-stage selection over 421 candidate specifications: flagship index membership widens the gap (beta +0.78, p<0.01), TCFD support widens it (beta +0.86, p<0.05, identified off only 19 non-supporters), renewable-energy use narrows it (beta -0.31, p<0.01) and environmental capex narrows it (beta -0.22, p<0.05) (p.13). Bivariate correlations agree (renewable r=-0.33, capex r=-0.19, index r=+0.31, TCFD r=+0.28), and governance and financial controls sit near zero and are pruned by the complexity penalty (p.13). The symbolic signals are saturated - about 55% of the sample are flagship index constituents and about 91% are TCFD supporters - which is exactly why the estimated model reads the gap as decoupling among firms that all claim to be green (p.12). The evidentiary limit is that this is one cross-section, so the coefficients are conditional associations and not treatment effects (p.6). The transferable, rater-free quantity here is the idea of a talk-versus-walk gap between two documents the engine already holds for one firm-period, which is the same construction `text_factors.divergence` performs on filing versus news text.

## 3. Raw ESG variables beat aggregates for risk, and the effect is size-specific

`2508.18679v2` is the methodological core of the slice. Its response variable is the natural logarithm of return volatility, chosen because Box-Cox diagnostics favour the log form: Jarque-Bera residual p-value 0.1389 and R2 0.51 for log volatility, against p=0.0000 and R2 0.39 for raw volatility (p.3). Its framework selects LSEG raw variables inside each of the 10 categories, then across categories, then refits by ridge; for the Energy sector the Variable Selection step keeps 35 of the initial 255 variables with %dev 0.52, and ridge retains that set at %dev 0.51 (p.6). The headline result is that raw variables beat aggregation: models built from selected raw variables in a few categories outperform a model that uses all 10 pre-aggregated category scores, so the aggregation process itself dilutes the most risk-relevant variables (p.5). The second result is that the selected ESG variables carry information the financial factors do not: for Energy, %dev is 0.32 for financial factors, 0.51 for selected ESG variables and 0.62 combined, with the same direction in every sector tested (p.13). The third result is that relevance is not a sector property alone: in every sector the fit is substantially better for large caps than small caps, and in Energy the dominant risk categories are environmental for large caps but social and governance for small caps (p.14, p.15). For a research layer the warning is precise - do not average, do not reduce to one score, and do not assume one factor set per sector.

## 4. ESG screening: filtering is not alpha and learned rules decay

`2002.07477v2` tests both the popular approach and a machine-learning alternative on the MSCI World universe of more than 1,600 companies. ESG best-in-class filtering, which drops the lowest x-quantile of ratings within peer groups, produced small but negative excess returns and negative information ratios across regions and thresholds, with Europe and low thresholds the only partial exception; over August 2009 to March 2018 the World Developed benchmark annualised 10.07% against 9.51% for the 50% best-in-class filter, and only 12 of 40 World Developed sector/metric portfolios had positive excess return, half of them on the governance score alone (p.5, p.6). The learned alternative, a rule-ensemble over granular ESG features, screened to a positive portfolio that beat the benchmark by 2.76% annualised, best-in-class by 2.94% and a negative-score portfolio by 4.77% over January 2013 to March 2018 (p.14). Two limits travel with that: the test window is five years and three months in a strong-equity regime (p.14), and the predictive power of the rules vanished with time - the number of active rules moved between 31 and 73, and the excess return of a frozen rule set shrank to zero and went negative over time (p.17). The practical reading is that ESG data may contain a signal that only non-linear methods extract, but the signal is not durable and must be re-fit.

## 5. ESG fund performance is crisis-conditional

`1806.09906v2` studies 73 US socially responsible mutual funds over 2005-2016, splitting the period into the great recession (2005-2008), the recovery (2009-2012) and the expansion (2013-2016) (p.4). The high-ESG tertile held up better only in the downturn: in 2005-2008 its monthly return was -0.32% with Sharpe 0.56, while the low-ESG tertile returned -0.39% with Sharpe 0.30 (p.7). Outside the crisis the ordering reversed - in 2009-2013 the low-ESG funds had the higher monthly return (1.33% against 1.11%) and in 2013-2016 they again led (0.99% against 0.75%) - and the lowest-ESG funds attracted the largest differential inflows except during the downturn (p.7, p.12). Expense ratios were negative for both risk-adjusted return and flows across the whole period (p.8). The paper is one of the oldest in the read set and the sub-periods are short, but its shape matches the slice's recurring theme: ESG's return premium, where it exists, is a downside-protection property rather than a full-sample alpha.

## 6. Transition risk is a forward-looking, state-dependent shock

`2410.00902v1` builds a dynamic general-equilibrium, production-based asset-pricing model in which the arrival rate of a climate-linked transition shock rises endogenously with temperature (p.16, p.17). Its central mechanism is a sign that depends on the expected nature of the shock: anticipation of a technology-driven transition produces a "run on fossil fuel" with accelerated extraction and falling spot prices, while anticipation of a tax-driven transition produces a "reverse run" with restrained production and elevated prices, and fossil fuel firm prices fall in both cases (p.1). The paper cites the standard stranding benchmark that holding warming below 2C requires leaving 30% of oil and 80% of coal reserves unburned (p.7). The empirical work is an event study plus a structural VAR on the global oil market: sectors with the highest transition-risk exposure show the largest cumulative abnormal returns around transition events, and an increased transition likelihood leads to higher oil production and a lower oil spot price, amplified and statistically significant mainly over 2009-2019 (p.4). The model is stylised and the empirical response is state-dependent, so the robust output is the sign and the state-dependence, not the magnitude (p.4).

## 7. Carbon pricing: small direct exposure, large supply-chain amplification

`2503.10644v1` is the most operationally concrete paper in the slice. Using VAT transaction records it estimates CO2 emissions for all 410,523 Hungarian firms from their oil and gas purchases, expanding coverage from the 119 firms reporting under ETS I to 185,783 firms with positive emissions, which account for 70% of gross output (p.2). It finds 45% of firms directly exposed to carbon pricing, and the emissions distribution is heavy tailed with a power-law exponent near -1.05 (p.2). At the EU ETS II price cap of 45 EUR/t, with cost pass-through, direct real-economy losses are about 1.3% of sales and direct bank equity losses about 1.2% (p.4); system-wide equity losses range from 0.3% to 21% as the price rises from 10 to 1,000 EUR/t (p.4). The headline result is contagion. Once defaults cascade along supply chains, optimistic-substitution losses reach 12.3% of gross output at 200 EUR/t, and a pessimistic no-substitution scenario jumps past 50% of output and 43% of bank equity at a 30 EUR/t price when a systemically central firm fails (p.6). The authors call the estimates upper bounds because firms are assumed not to adapt in advance, and the study is one country (p.7). For a research layer the transferable point is that the relevant exposure is a network property, not a direct-emissions number, which is the same shape as correlation and concentration risk.

## 8. Allocation and price: permits and carbon forecasts

`1108.2305v2` derives an emissions-permit allocation from the Boltzmann distribution, in which a single parameter beta sets how strongly the allocation favours high per-capita emitters (p.5, p.10). Applied to eight countries and a 2008 target of 17,084,135 thousand tonnes of permits (97% of 2007 emissions), the least-squares reference value beta=0.0966 gives China 41% and the US 32% of permits, leaving both as net sellers and six countries as buyers (p.12, p.18). Below beta=0.1164 China receives more permits than the US, and above it the US receives more, so one parameter reassigns responsibility between the largest total emitter and the largest per-capita emitter (p.14). `2212.11787v1` forecasts the EU carbon price with support vector regression on oil, coal, gas, the DAX and Paris emission targets, projecting 114.7 EUR in 2030 under a 2,137,554 kt target and 203.0 EUR under a 1,603,165.5 kt target (p.5, p.10). The forecast is explicitly data-limited: the author states the study has limited training data, limited compute for the parameter search and only two policy targets (p.12). Both papers are useful mainly as warnings about how much a single parameter or a single policy target moves a carbon number.

## 9. Divestment as a social tipping process

`1902.07481v1` models divestment as a coupled social-and-financial dynamic. Its agent-based model joins an adaptive investor network, where beliefs about future carbon policy spread by contagion, to a stock market and a fixed 250 GtCO2 carbon budget (p.5). Six behavioural regimes emerge, and the central result is a tipping point: a socially responsible investor share of only 10% to 20% is sufficient to burst the carbon bubble when social interaction is fast, consistent with a Pareto-principle minority driving change (p.1, p.13). Empirically, more than 1,000 institutions holding about USD 7.93 trillion had pledged to divest by 2017, although fossil fuel stocks are typically only 5-10% of a portfolio (p.2, p.3). The model is stylised - constant fuel price, identical investor wealth, uncorrelated fossil-fuel stock price - and holds the SRI share fixed, so it understates the feedback it argues for (p.13). The operational reading is that a fossil-fuel de-rating is a threshold event, not a linear glide path, which is the same discipline as scenario-testing a sudden correlation breakdown rather than a steady drift.

## 10. News-based ESG scoring and its accuracy ceiling

`2212.11765v1` predicts Refinitiv Asset4 combined ESG scores, built from 186 metrics grouped into 10 themes on a 0-100 scale (p.2), from news for about 3,000 US companies and 3,739,871 articles (p.1). The pipeline classifies articles for ESG relevance with a fine-tuned DistilBERT, reaching 83% accuracy with 85% precision on the relevant class (p.6), derives sentiment, clusters articles into six semantic groups and feeds nine monthly time series per company into a deep model. The task is better framed as regression than classification: exact 100-class accuracy is only 2-3%, while the best model, a deep CNN regression, achieves a mean absolute difference of about 11.97 to 13.04 rating points against the provider score (p.7). Predictions are better for small-cap firms (10.28) than large-cap firms (15.42) (p.8). The paper also cites a Bank of America estimate that S&P 500 companies lost more than USD 600 billion of market value to ESG issues over seven years (p.1). The limit is that the ground truth is one provider's score, so the model learns to imitate a rater rather than to measure sustainability - the same aggregate-confusion trap from section 1, now inside a model.

## 11. The repository holds no ESG, climate or carbon surface

A direct search of `tradingagents/`, `scripts/` and `contracts/` for `esg`, `climate`, `carbon` and `emission` finds no ESG calculator, no ESG tool, no ESG config gate and no ESG score component. The only "esg" hit in the Python tree is a docstring in `tradingagents/strategies/universe_factors.py` that lists ESG among "vendor-blocked survey systems" that are deliberately absent because no reachable source measures them and a proxy would be a fabricated number. The theme registry in `tradingagents/strategies/security_context.py` registers six ids (ai, tariff, china, regulatory, commodity, cyber) and none is a climate or carbon theme, although the priority matrix does give the energy sector a HIGH priority on `regulatory` and `commodity`. The `regulatory` theme in `tradingagents/strategies/news_score.py` is already a lower-better news component fed by `tag_category_read` over the article feed's topics, and `industry_shock` is an existing sector-relative news leg - these are the two surfaces through which climate regulation and carbon-driven sector shocks would actually enter the research layer today, without inventing an ESG engine. This is the honest result of the slice: the category is a genuine gap, and the transferable findings route to the text-divergence calculator, the news engine, the book-risk stress machinery, the sector-driver table and the fundamentals tools.

## Caveats

The read set is not comparable across papers and several results rest on one country, one cross-section or one model. `2606.31469v1` is a single fiscal-year (2023) cross-section of 200 European firms in four carbon-intensive sectors, so it establishes associations and not causal effects. `2503.10644v1` is one country (Hungary), assumes firms do not adapt in advance and reports upper-bound losses. `2002.07477v2` tests its machine-learning screen over only five years and three months (January 2013 to March 2018), a strong-equity regime, and its authors say so. `1806.09906v2` covers 73 US funds over 2005-2016 and its crisis-versus-expansion split rests on three short sub-periods. `2212.11787v1` forecasts a single market (EU carbon) with limited training data and two policy targets, so its 2030 numbers are illustrations, not forecasts. `1108.2305v2` allocates permits for eight countries on 2008 data and defines allocation energy in the simplest possible way, the negative of per-capita emissions. `1902.07481v1` is a stylised agent-based model with a constant fuel price, identical investor wealth and a fixed socially-responsible-investor share. `2508.18679v2` is US sectors only and its ground truth is one provider's data. `2212.11765v1` learns one provider's score as its target. No paper in the read set includes transaction costs except indirectly through fund expense ratios, and several test windows overlap the 2008 crisis and the COVID-19 period, so cross-paper comparisons of magnitude should not be made. Finally, the landing column is deliberately conservative: this repository has no ESG surface, and every row that names a path names an existing module whose job is adjacent to the finding, not a new ESG engine.

## Papers read in depth

- `2606.31469v1` - Same Firms, Different Verdicts: ESG Rating Choice and the Measurement of Greenwashing (2026). Firm-level disclosure-performance gap for 200 STOXX Europe 600 firms and the demonstration that its determinants are conditional on the rating provider; one cross-section, associations not effects.
- `2508.18679v2` - Identifying Risk Variables From Raw ESG Data Using Its Hierarchical Structure (2026). Selects raw LSEG variables for log return volatility, beats aggregated scores and beats financial factors when combined; US sectors only and one provider's data.
- `2002.07477v2` - ESG investments: Filtering versus machine learning approaches (2020). Best-in-class filtering does not pay while a learned non-linear screen does, then decays; short out-of-sample window in one market regime.
- `1806.09906v2` - Fund Characteristics and Performances of Socially Responsible Mutual Funds: Do ESG Ratings Play a Role? (2018). Crisis-conditional ESG fund performance and flows; small sample of 73 US funds, coarse sub-periods.
- `2410.00902v1` - A Run on Fossil Fuel? Climate Change and Transition Risk (2024). General-equilibrium model of transition-risk expectations with an event study and a structural VAR; stylised, and the empirical response is state-dependent.
- `2503.10644v1` - Combined climate stress testing of supply-chain networks and the financial system with nation-wide firm-level emission estimates (2025). Firm-level carbon stress test for all 410,523 Hungarian firms including supply-chain contagion; one country, upper-bound losses.
- `1108.2305v2` - Permit Allocation in Emissions Trading Using the Boltzmann Distribution (2011). Maximum-entropy permit allocation with one fairness parameter; eight countries on 2008 data and a minimal energy definition.
- `2212.11787v1` - Macro carbon price prediction with support vector regression and Paris accord targets (2022). SVR forecast of the EU carbon price to 2030; very limited training data and only two policy targets.
- `1902.07481v1` - Divestment may burst the carbon bubble if investors' beliefs tip to anticipating strong future climate policy (2019). Agent-based divestment model with a 10-20% social tipping threshold; stylised, constant fuel price, identical wealth.
- `2212.11765v1` - Predicting Companies' ESG Ratings from News Articles Using Multivariate Timeseries Analysis (2022). Deep-learning prediction of Asset4 scores from 3.7m articles; it learns one provider's score and exact-class accuracy is near zero.
