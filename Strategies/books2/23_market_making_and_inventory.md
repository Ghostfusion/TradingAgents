# Market making, spreads and inventory: dealer pricing power sets the spread, but the part a research layer can use is the spread and inventory estimator, not the quoting mechanic

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads the general-finance (q-fin.GN) harvest on market making, spreads and inventory: 22 records spanning 2006-2026, 8 of which carry a journal reference. It is written for a research and analysis engine, not an execution engine - a repository of deterministic offline calculators, LangChain tools and LLM analysts that emits a scored research decision. Its job is therefore not to propose a quoting algorithm (this layer has no order gateway) but to decide which spread and inventory statistics are trustworthy enough to feed cost modelling and risk, and which market-making findings should be recorded honestly as having no landing here.

## What this covers

The slice spans three market forms that share one question. First, discretionary dealer markets where a small set of intermediaries price bilateral flow: UK gilt repo (`2603.10690v1`), OTC currency (`1601.00085v2`) and an abstract competitive dealer market (`1807.08278v3`). Second, automated venues where the "market maker" is a formula or a learned policy: a Hawkes-driven limit order book (`2207.09951v1`), a constant-product pool (`2302.05219v2`) and Uniswap v3 positions treated as high-frequency market makers (`2509.02941v1`). Third, the historical and cross-market evidence on what a dealer franchise is worth and how a spread responds to an outside shock (`1910.13882v1`, `2601.08263v3`). The companion brief in the sibling library covers the Avellaneda-Stoikov and Cartea-Jaimungal control lineage from the trading corpus; nothing here restates it.

The single most consequential finding for this repository is a negative one. Almost none of this slice lands on a research-layer surface as a computation: the repo market-power estimates, the learned quoting policy and the pool mechanics are all execution-layer facts, and there is no repo-dealer-network feed, no order gateway and no automated-market-maker module in `tradingagents/strategies/`. What does land is the honesty of the spread estimators that already exist - `tradingagents/strategies/liquidity_risk.py` carries Roll (1984), Corwin-Schultz (2012) and Abdi-Ranaldo (2017) spread proxies that are daily-bar relative reads with stated failure modes, while the cost model in `tradingagents/strategies/backtest_models.py` and `tradingagents/strategies/evaluate.py` is a flat per-trade basis-point charge. The slice says the real cost depends on inventory, on flow regularity and on the market regime, so the correct action is to record those dependencies rather than to publish a single spread or cost number as if it were measured.

## The short answer

1. Dealer market power and the transmission of dealer shocks move gilt yields by 2.5 to 5.3 percentage points in absolute deviation from a spline benchmark: markups and markdowns explain 0.5 to 1.3 points and heterogeneous shock transmission another 2 to 4 (`2603.10690v1`, pp.1, 3).
2. That power acts on prices, not quantities: relationship depth predicts repo volume with high significance, but the estimated spread effect of relationship trading is not statistically significant (`2603.10690v1`, p.25).
3. Quoted width was changed by three observable factors - a conditional-volatility recursion, a trade-count factor and a volume factor - consolidated with equal weights into one rolling spread factor whose 30-day mean and standard deviation set the widen/narrow thresholds (`1601.00085v2`, pp.9-11).
4. Dealer-market price impact is transient, not permanent, because client flow can be externalised to a finite-liquidity open market; the classic permanent-impact result returns only in the limit of no open-market access (`1807.08278v3`, pp.3-4).
5. The liquidity cost scales with the regularity of client flow: order theta for smooth flow, order sqrt(theta) for diffusive flow, the latter depending on the flow's quadratic variation and on the dealers' holding cost (`1807.08278v3`, pp.10-11).
6. A learned quoting policy penalised on the absolute value of inventory, not its square, held a mean absolute position of 0.42 against 1.47 for a symmetric benchmark, at a Sharpe ratio of 1.62 (`2207.09951v1`, p.5).
7. Quoting tolerates fees only up to a measurable threshold: the learned policy survived about 0.6% limit-order fees, a linear-inventory rule turned negative at 0.4% and best-bid-offer quoting at 0.2% (`2207.09951v1`, p.6).
8. Passive liquidity provision is size-selective: on 30-day holds in the twelve largest pools, the fraction of profitable observations was 0.00 for small, 0.05 for medium and 0.49 for large positions in USDC/WETH (`2302.05219v2`, p.6).
9. The return to passive provision decomposes into a non-negative fee effect and a divergence loss that is a deterministic function of the change in the price ratio, so a convex profitability frontier separates profitable from unprofitable allocations (`2302.05219v2`, pp.2-3).
10. High-frequency liquidity-producer dynamics are non-quadratic and regime-dependent: the same pool produced a clear double-well potential at two-month windows and a non-harmonic single well at one-month windows (`2509.02941v1`, pp.5, 8).
11. A cross-market shock can narrow a spread rather than widen it: the 3-month AA nonfinancial commercial paper spread fell about 3.0 basis points on the day of a major DeFi exploit and about 2.4 on the next day, against a joint pre-trend test that fails to reject zero (`2601.08263v3`, p.25).

## 1. Dealer pricing power sets the spread and acts through prices, not quantities

`2603.10690v1` uses proprietary transaction-level data on all gilt-backed repo and reverse-repo trades from January 2016 to January 2022, covering dealer-to-non-dealer and dealer-to-CCP trades (p.8). It recovers a dealer-level markup, (r - c)/r = 1/eta, and a markdown, 1/nu, from a structural demand-and-supply model with granular instrumental variables (pp.14, 21), and measures mispricing as the absolute deviation of each gilt's yield from a daily spline benchmark (p.9). Frictions from markups and markdowns explain 0.5 to 1.3 percentage points of yield deviation, and dispersion across dealers plus network transmission of shocks adds 2 to 4 points, for 2.5 to 5.3 in total, strongest at the long end (pp.1, 3).

Two structural facts deserve mention. There are 15 dealers and 3,380 repo non-dealers in 727 families, forming 2,112 dealer-family dyads (p.10); five dealers explain 40% of all funding yet account for only 8% to 18% of total variance contributions (p.12); and the inter-dealer segment is about 70% of daily volume while under half of over-the-counter trades are overnight (p.11). At the transaction level a 1% higher market power raises the repo spread by 0.039 percentage points, about half its standard deviation, but raises the reverse-repo spread by 0.53 points against a standard deviation of 0.08 (p.25). The operationally important asymmetry is that market power transmits through repo rates only: relationship depth predicts volume with a high positive coefficient, frequency is significant, and the relationship-trading estimates for the spread are not significant (pp.25-26). For a research layer this is a caution against inferring expected slippage from counterparty concentration - the paper's own evidence says concentration moves the price of funding, not the volume of flow.

## 2. A quote-adjustment loop built from three observable factors

`1601.00085v2` is the operational counterpart: a bid-offer adjustment model for a currency market maker that changes the quoted width as conditions change. Three factors enter - a conditional-volatility recursion on log-return innovations sampled at 60-second intervals, a trade-count factor and a volume factor, the last two measured as the log ratio of the latest interval to a 30-day rolling average (pp.9-11). The three are consolidated by a weighted sum with equal weights into a spread factor, whose 30-day rolling mean and standard deviation define the action bands: widen when the factor exceeds half a standard deviation above the mean, narrow when it falls a third of a standard deviation below (p.11).

The test used EUR/USD from 24 July 2013 to 24 October 2013, with the trade price proxied by the mean of high, low, open and close over each minute, and trade count and size simulated from a lognormal so that total volume matches publicly reported figures (p.12). The reported outcome is a USD 513,050 increase in P&L for the window; the spread was widened 47,347 times and narrowed 48,244 times, hitting the upper bound 19,605 times and the lower bound 27,535 times; the volume affected by the widened spread was about USD 444.95 billion and by the narrowed spread 443.19 billion (pp.12-13). The transferable content is not the P&L number - the exercise is one pair over one quarter with simulated flow - but the construction: a spread read assembled from observables, with explicit upper and lower caps and a rolling recalibration window.

## 3. Inventory, internalisation and why dealer-market impact is transient

`1807.08278v3` solves a continuous-time Grossman-Miller intermediation model in which liquidity takers' demand is absorbed by dealers with quadratic inventory costs, who can gradually transfer positions to an exogenous open market with finite liquidity (pp.1-2). The equilibrium price in the dealer market is the expected dividend plus a term proportional to aggregate inventory measured in units of aggregate risk tolerance (pp.3, 9), so the premium clients pay is the compensation dealers require for warehousing. Because client flow can be passed on, price impact is transient rather than permanent, and the classic permanent-impact result is recovered only in the limit of no open-market access (pp.3-4).

The second result matters for cost modelling. For smooth demand the liquidity cost is (M+1)/M times the open-market trading cost, of order theta, and is independent of dealer inventory cost; for diffusive demand it is of order sqrt(theta), depends on the quadratic variation of the flow, and does expose the dealers' holding cost (pp.10-11). Approximating a rough strategy by a smooth one does not avoid the cost of the rough one (p.4). Finally, in the competitive limit of many small clients and dealers the welfare loss from segmentation disappears (p.17), which is a reminder that a per-trade cost calibrated on a concentrated market does not transfer to a fragmented one.

## 4. Learned quoting: absolute-inventory penalties and a measurable fee tolerance

`2207.09951v1` trains a Soft Actor-Critic controller on a weakly consistent, multivariate Hawkes process limit-order-book simulator with eight event types, where the market maker's own limit and market orders feed back into arrival intensities (p.3). The policy observes inventory, the spread and a trend variable and chooses offsets on both sides, rewarded by terminal wealth minus a running penalty on the absolute value of inventory rather than the squared value, which the authors use for a value-at-risk reading (pp.4-5). Against a symmetric best-bid-offer benchmark and the best member of a linear-inventory class that contains the Gueant-Lehalle-Fernandez-Tapia approximations, the learned controller reported mean P&L 13.76 versus 9.87 and 8.21, Sharpe 1.62 versus 1.20 and 1.42, mean absolute position 0.42 versus 1.47 and 0.55, P&L per unit mean absolute position 32.51 versus 6.73 and 14.99, and thinner P&L tails with kurtosis 4.21 versus 6.06 and 10.56 (p.5).

Two transferable findings survive the simulator. First, penalising the absolute value of inventory is a cleaner risk instrument than the convenient squared penalty, because the penalty then reads directly as a position budget. Second, fee tolerance is a measurable property of a strategy: the learned policy tolerated limit-order fees up to about 0.6%, the linear rule turned negative at 0.4% and symmetric quoting at 0.2% (p.6), and the learned policy's mean transaction count fell from 24.54 to 15.0 to 11.28 to 7.85 as the fee rose through 0%, 0.2%, 0.4% and 0.6% (p.6). Under perturbed background intensities the Sharpe ratio fell monotonically from 1.38 to 1.02 to 0.86 (p.6), so even the learned policy is not robust to a change in arrival rates.

## 5. Passive liquidity provision without discretion: a size-selective profitability frontier

`2302.05219v2` formalises the economics of a constant-product market maker and separates the two components of a liquidity provider's return: a non-negative fee-accumulation effect and a divergence (impermanent) loss that is a deterministic function of the change in the price ratio (p.2). Dividing the fee effect by the cost side produces a profitability frontier in the reserve space, a convex set of allocations for which provision beats holding, with price limits derived in closed form for both fee cases (pp.2-3). The empirical part covers every Uniswap V2 transaction: 132,657 pools, 2,371,811 mint events, 1,048,613 burn events and 93,749,446 swap events between 4 May 2020 and the end of 2022 (p.5).

Because a mint and a burn are separate transactions, and a full cycle can require up to five blockchain transactions including ERC-20 approvals, the fixed network cost is large relative to a small position (p.3). The result is an implicit lock-in for small providers: in the twelve largest pools, positions defined by a fee-to-size ratio of 0.10 (small), 0.05 (medium) and 0.01 (large) were profitable on 30-day holds in 0.00 / 0.05 / 0.49 of observations for USDC/WETH, and 180-day and 360-day profitability rose to 0.20 / 0.55 / 0.69 and 0.44 / 0.47 / 0.53 (p.6). This is the clearest statement in the slice that a strategy's edge is conditional on a minimum viable size once a fixed cost is present, a constraint the repository's own cost model can express through its fixed-fee and capacity terms.

## 6. High-frequency liquidity-producer dynamics are non-quadratic and regime-dependent

`2509.02941v1` treats Uniswap v3 pools as a laboratory of high-frequency market making, estimating the drift and diffusion of log price non-parametrically with a Kramers-Moyal expansion and integrating the drift to obtain the potential the price is pulled toward (pp.3-4). Data are three pools on Arbitrum: USDC/WETH for 2024 (about 300k transaction records) and for the first half of 2025, and WBTC/WETH for 2024, with each observation the log price averaged over 15 consecutive transactions at roughly 100-second spacing and log-price tails trimmed at 0.5% top and bottom (pp.3-4).

The finding is that the linear-diffusion default is wrong at intraday horizons. At lags from 10 minutes to 6 hours the estimated drift is strongly non-linear and the implied potential is non-quadratic; for USDC/WETH in 2024 two of three two-month windows produced a clear double-well potential while a one-month window produced a non-harmonic single well (pp.5, 8). Above hourly sampling the potential usually has one global minimum; the authors read a single well as a resilient mean-reverting state and a double well as a metastable state with two competing price regimes, associated with uncertainty or stress (pp.7-8). They also argue that fast hidden drift components self-average out at windows beyond five days, so the estimate is a property of the window as much as of the market (p.10). The practical warning is direct: a spread or liquidity statistic computed on one window can choose its own answer, so the window and the regime are part of the number.

## 7. Dealer franchises in illiquid markets: reciprocity and concentration

`1910.13882v1` is a narrative case study of Michael Milken and the junk-bond market, and it is the slice's only evidence on how a dealer franchise is built in a market with no central venue. The numbers are historical aggregates: Milken's salary and bonus rose from USD 43 million in 1983 to 123 million in 1984 and peaked near 500 million in 1987, while the junk-bond market grew from near zero to more than USD 125 billion (p.2). In 1977 only 6% of roughly 11,000 corporations were investment grade; new junk issuance ran below USD 1.5 billion a year from 1977 to 1981; Drexel issued USD 1.1 billion for MCI in 1983 and took 60% of its 1985 profit from junk bonds at underwriting fees of 3% to 4% (p.7).

The structural claim is the interesting one: the dealer ran on "an intricate network of reciprocal obligation", selling the bonds of one issuer only if the buyer agreed to take another issuer's bonds later, and it committed principal by buying back the bonds it underwrote (pp.7-8). The sentencing judge treated client loyalty as the mechanism through which the defendant profited - by increasing clients' dependence and therefore the edge over competitors (p.12). Concentration is documented with a Herfindahl index for junk bonds (p.9), and the financing it enabled was heavily levered, with debt forming 60% to 90% of the transactions used to acquire majority ownership, though LBOs were only 15% to 25% of all completed mergers (pp.10-12). There is no price or spread data in the paper at all, so it is context, not calibration.

## 8. Cross-market spread responses: segmentation, not contagion, sets the sign

`2601.08263v3` asks whether DeFi operational shocks destabilise a traditional short-term funding price, the spread between 3-month AA nonfinancial commercial paper and the 3-month Treasury bill. The sample is the 50 largest exploit-driven shocks from 2021 to 2024, with event dates mapped to the next US trading day when needed and macro-event days excluded from the stacked panel (pp.12-13). Mean loss per event was USD 88.80 million with a maximum of 625 million, and the spread itself has a mean of 12.34 basis points over 1,005 daily observations (p.6).

The naive contagion prior is a widening; the measured pattern is a narrowing of about 3.0 basis points on the event day and about 2.4 basis points on the next trading day, against a joint pre-trend test that fails to reject zero (F = 0.53, p = 0.714), with placebo empirical p-values of 0.000 and 0.002 for the two days from 500 covariate-matched Monte Carlo draws (pp.25, 29). The authors' mapping is explicit: a segmented market narrows when demand amplification into the prime-eligible segment exceeds the supply pressure loading on the same segment (p.12), and regulatory segmentation under SEC Rule 2a-7, which requires government money market funds to hold at least 99.5% of assets in cash, government securities or fully collateralised repo, makes prime funds the plausible marginal destination (p.4). The mechanism is inferred from pricing and monthly holdings, not identified at the daily frequency. The lesson for a risk engine is that a shock's sign is not fixed by the shock's direction: it depends on which segment the flow is routed into.

## Caveats

Only four of the eight papers contain measured market data on a real venue - `2603.10690v1` (one collateral type in one country, 2016-2022, with model-recovered markups), `2302.05219v2` (Uniswap V2 daily snapshots and virtual positions), `2509.02941v1` (three pools on one chain) and `2601.08263v3` (one aggregate spread series with a mechanism inferred rather than identified) - and all four are single-market and single-period. Everything else is theory, simulation or narrative: `1807.08278v3` is a linear-quadratic equilibrium with no asymmetric information, `2207.09951v1` is entirely inside a Hawkes simulator, `1601.00085v2` simulates its own trade counts and volumes and reports one quarter of one currency pair, and `1910.13882v1` has no price data at all. The AMM results (`2302.05219v2`, `2509.02941v1`) come from one blockchain family and window-dependent estimators, so the double-well shapes are as much a property of the two-month window as of the market. Three findings have genuinely no landing here: dealer-market pricing power and network transmission (this layer has no repo or dealer-network feed), divergence loss and automated-market-maker mechanics (there is no LP module), and outright learned quoting (an execution-layer artefact). Of the two spread surfaces this repository does own, both are off by default - the tool `get_spread_estimate` is gated by `enable_spread_estimator` (default `False`, `tradingagents/default_config.py:1155`) and the capital-flow fold is gated by `enable_orderflow` (default `False`, `tradingagents/default_config.py:922`) - so a reader should not assume a spread or flow read is present unless the gate is on.

## Papers read in depth

- `2603.10690v1` - When David becomes Goliath: Repo dealer-driven bond mispricing (2026). Dealer market power and network shocks priced into gilt yields at 2.5-5.3 pp; one market, one window, model-derived markups.
- `1601.00085v2` - Dynamic Multi-Factor Bid-Offer Adjustment Model (2016). A deployable three-factor quote-adjustment loop with rolling thresholds and caps; simulated flow and one quarter of EUR/USD.
- `1807.08278v3` - Liquidity in Competitive Dealer Markets (2021). Transient dealer-market impact and an order-of-magnitude gap between smooth and rough client flow; stylised equilibrium, no data.
- `2207.09951v1` - Deep Reinforcement Learning for Market Making Under a Hawkes Process-Based Limit Order Book Model (2022). Absolute-inventory penalty and a measurable fee tolerance; simulator-only, assumed fill and jump laws.
- `2302.05219v2` - Decentralized Exchanges: The Profitability Frontier of Constant Product Market Makers (2023). A closed-form profitability frontier plus full Uniswap V2 transaction coverage; virtual positions, no realised P&L.
- `2509.02941v1` - Non-Linear and Meta-Stable Dynamics in Financial Markets: Evidence from High Frequency Crypto Currency Market Makers (2025). Non-parametric evidence that intraday liquidity-producer dynamics are non-quadratic and regime-dependent; three pools, window-dependent shapes.
- `1910.13882v1` - Michael Milken: The Junk Dealer (2019). The franchise-as-network account of dealing in an illiquid market, with historical aggregates; no price or spread data.
- `2601.08263v3` - A Blessing in Disguise? DeFi Exploits and Short-Horizon Responses in U.S. Commercial Paper Spreads (2026). A cross-market spread that narrows after an outside shock, with a segmentation explanation; one aggregate series, mechanism inferred.
