# Return predictability and trading strategies: the cross-section is mostly real and the time series mostly is not, so decay, costs and leakage are the binding constraints

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers the 122-record slice of the local arXiv harvest whose titles or abstracts concern return predictability and trading strategies, spanning 2001 to 2026, 22 of which carry a journal reference. It reads twelve papers in depth at the PDF and asks what is actually predictable in returns, how much a predictability claim is worth once multiple testing, trading costs and turnover are counted, and how the newest family of forecasters, large language models, contaminates its own backtests. It is written for the research-and-analysis layer of this repository, not an execution layer: every take-away lands on a deterministic calculator module under `tradingagents/strategies/`, an agent tool, a score engine, a config gate, a ledger/registry, or the decision contract, and findings that would only be actionable in an order gateway are re-routed to the smallest research-layer consequence that exists.

## What this covers

The slice runs from the oldest question in asset pricing, whether payout ratios forecast returns, to the newest, whether a language model can. The arc is a reversal. The classic time-series result does not survive a broad out-of-sample panel of 48 country-asset combinations (`2209.00121v1`), while the cross-sectional "factor zoo" that was widely suspected of being p-hacked turns out to be mostly genuine: an easy bound puts the false-discovery rate at or below 25 percent and a tighter visual bound at or below 8.5 percent (`2206.15365v10`).

That inversion is the single most consequential finding for this repository. Predictability is abundant enough in the cross-section that the binding constraints move from discovery to decay, crowding, cost and the integrity of the evaluation. The rest of the slice shows what those constraints look like in practice: momentum design and its skewness term structure (`2101.01006v2`), drawdown- and recovery-based ranking rules (`1403.8125v4`), mass-weighted definitions of momentum (`1208.2775v5`), MACD rules that win under half their trades (`2206.12282v1`), regime-switching cointegration in crude oil futures (`2309.00875v3`), a news-driven multi-horizon backtest (`1508.04332v2`), the measured lookahead contamination of large language models (`2512.23847v2`, `2309.17322v1`), the model of AI-driven alpha decay (`2605.23905v1`), and the statistical gap between a binary forecast being right and a payoff actually paying (`1907.11162v3`).

## The short answer

1. Payout-ratio return timing fails a broad out-of-sample test: of 48 country-asset combinations across 16 countries from the 1870s to 2020, none shows consistent in-sample, out-of-sample and utility gains, and the prediction portfolios trade several times more than the prevailing-average benchmark (`2209.00121v1`).
2. The cross-sectional factor zoo is probably mostly true rather than p-hacked: the easy bound puts the false-discovery rate at or below 25 percent and a tighter visual bound at or below 8.5 percent, i.e. at least 91.5 percent of equal-weighted findings are real (`2206.15365v10`).
3. The defence of a signal must be weighting- and factor-specific: after value-weighting and a four-factor adjustment the false-discovery bound rises to 41.7 percent, and the 42 percent is the part liquidity and momentum can explain (`2206.15365v10`).
4. Ten thousand significant signals are not ten thousand independent bets: more than 80 percent of pairwise correlations are below 0.25 in absolute value and roughly 50 principal components explain 75 percent of variance, so combining data-mined predictors yields out-of-sample Sharpe ratios near 1.5 rather than a 100-fold boost (`2206.15365v10`).
5. Momentum strategies are positively skewed by construction, even when the underlying returns are i.i.d., zero-mean and unskewed, and the skewness is hump-shaped in the holding horizon, peaking near 1.1 EMA periods and then decaying as M^-1/2 (`2101.01006v2`).
6. Recovery- and drawdown-based ranking rules beat cumulative return at matched horizons: recovery wins weekly contrarian portfolios in KOSPI 200 and the S&P 500 while cutting the maximum drawdown roughly in half (`1403.8125v4`).
7. Physics-flavoured momentum works only through the mass proxy: volume- and turnover-weighted definitions beat unit-mass cumulative return, while the inverse-volatility mass does not replicate that on the KOSPI and can turn negative (`1208.2775v5`).
8. Indicator-only MACD strategies win under half their trades (0.37 to 0.49), and genetic-algorithm parameters differ for every index and are driven by two trades exceeding twenty times the initial investment (`2206.12282v1`).
9. A hidden-Markov, regime-switching spread model produces the best pairs strategy in crude oil futures, and the forward-looking prediction-interval rule returns 15.18 percent annualised at a Sharpe ratio of 1.18 and a maximum drawdown of -5.73 percent even where pairwise cointegration is not detected (`2309.00875v3`).
10. A news-driven agent-based model backtests long-horizon SPY predictability and still reports Sharpe ratios of 0.97 to 1.23 gross over 2003-2015, and a Sharpe ratio of 1.07 after a 0.80 percent per annum cost drag in live trading, on one market and one strategy family (`1508.04332v2`).
11. LLM lookahead contamination is measurable without access to the training corpus: the model's probability of committing to a direction on a date-only recall query is materially positive throughout the training window and collapses to essentially zero after the December 2023 knowledge cutoff (`2512.23847v2`).
12. The win rate and the payoff are different statistical objects: being calibrated in binary probability space does not make a strategy's payoff well-behaved, because under fat tails the higher moments that drive real profit and loss are explosive (`1907.11162v3`).

## 1. The textbook time-series predictability does not survive the panel

`2209.00121v1` builds annual data for 16 developed countries across long-term government bonds, equities and housing from the 1870s to 2020, using coupon-price, dividend-price and rent-price ratios as predictors. In sample, payout prices predict excess returns in only two bond markets and five equity markets, while rent-price predicts eight housing markets (p.9). Out of sample the record collapses: none of the 16 bond markets is predictable, dividend price predicts only the UK equity market, and only the French and German housing markets are predictable (p.10). Utility gains agree: positive certainty-equivalent-return gains appear in two bond markets, three equity markets and four housing markets, and two of the four housing gains are near zero (p.11).

The decisive detail is turnover. The portfolios built on payout-ratio forecasts trade several times more than the prevailing-average benchmark: in the Italian equity market the dividend-price portfolio turns over about four times as much despite a non-positive CER gain, and the French housing portfolio about four times as much (p.11). Because the paper rebalances annually, the housing transaction costs alone, commission near 6 percent in the US and around 7.7 percent of value in 13 of the 16 countries, swamp the best 0.61 percent CER gain (p.3, p.11).

The paper's constructive half is the Cochrane decomposition: if returns are not predictable then payout growth must be, and it finds dividend growth predictable in 14 countries and rent growth in five, with a VAR simulation that does not reject the joint null of no return predictability and predictable dividend growth (p.12, p.13, p.19). That is the mechanism an analyst should internalise: a persistent valuation ratio is not evidence that the price will move.

## 2. The factor zoo is mostly real, and the weighting decides

`2206.15365v10` asks how many published cross-sectional predictors are false. Its "Easy Bound" needs only the share of signals with |t| greater than 2 and gives FDR no larger than 5 percent divided by that share; because data-mining studies find at least 20 percent of randomly chosen accounting ratios clear |t| greater than 2, the bound is 25 percent in eight of the nine studies examined (p.4). A tighter "Visual Bound" uses the observed frequency of small t-statistics and, on 29,000 data-mined signals of which 9,700 clear |t| greater than 2 and 6,300 sit below 0.5, gives FDR no larger than 8.5 percent, i.e. at least 91.5 percent true (p.9).

The reconciliation matters as much as the level. Studies reporting a false-discovery rate above 45 percent are reinterpreted: Harvey, Liu and Zhu's own dataset implies a bound of at most 12 percent under the same algebra, and the 9 percent figure falls out of their own simulated method-of-moments estimates once null and insignificant are kept as distinct concepts (p.7, p.15, p.16). Where the measurement changes, the bound moves: equal-weighted raw returns give 7.3 to 9.6 percent, but value-weighted returns with a four-factor (FF3 plus momentum) adjustment give FDR no larger than 41.7 percent (p.13). The paper is explicit that large, liquid stocks show weaker predictability and that liquidity and momentum may account for that 42 percent (p.13).

The practical corollary is about portfolio construction, not statistics. Ten thousand significant signals are not ten thousand independent bets: pairwise correlations are mostly below 0.25 in absolute value, roughly 50 principal components explain 75 percent of variance, and combining data-mined predictors yields out-of-sample Sharpe ratios near 1.5 rather than the 100-fold boost an independence assumption would suggest (p.12).

## 3. Momentum is a design problem, and skewness has a horizon term structure

`2101.01006v2` separates the first moment of a trend-following strategy from its third. Even when the risk-adjusted returns are i.i.d. with zero mean and zero skewness, the trading returns of a linear momentum strategy are positively skewed, because the position is largest after the strategy has already made money and the high-variance mixture components carry a positive mean (p.1, p.2). Skewness is therefore a property of the position rule, not of the market.

The term-structure result is the actionable one: the skewness of the M-period trading return rises to a maximum at a period proportional to the trend filter's response time and then decays as M^-1/2 (p.3, p.9). For a single-EMA model the peak skew is roughly 2.1 to 2.4 and occurs near M approximately 1.1N; for a two-EMA model it is about 2.1 near M approximately 1.7 times the sum of the two periods (p.11). A strategy's reported skewness therefore depends on the horizon at which it is measured. The position is scaled inversely to volatility, phi_n / sigma_n, so the risk of the book is held roughly constant (p.4).

Two design warnings follow. A binary plus-or-minus-one transformation of the momentum factor is not optimal and can remove the positive skewness or make it negative (p.4). And the option-like reading is exact: the trading return decomposes into quadratic bets with a few large positive-convexity, long-dated eigenvalues and many small negative-convexity, short-dated ones, which is why trend-following does well when long-horizon volatility is high relative to short-horizon volatility and suffers when it is not (p.15, p.16). The Cornish-Fisher relation P(Y greater than E[Y]) approximately 1/2 minus kappa_3/(6 sqrt(2 pi)) makes the point sharp: a positively skewed strategy can win about 45 percent of its trades and still be worth running (p.3, p.13).

## 4. Ranking rules that beat cumulative return, and the win-rate trap

`1403.8125v4` replaces the cumulative-return sort with sorts on maximum drawdown and the recovery after it, decomposed as cumulative return equals pre-peak return minus drawdown plus recovery over the three sub-periods (p.5). On weekly six-week/six-week contrarian portfolios in KOSPI 200 from January 2003 to December 2012 the recovery portfolio returns 0.1455 percent per week against 0.0731 percent for the benchmark, with standard deviation 1.7567 percent against 2.8417 percent (p.9). On the S&P 500 weekly contrarian the recovery rule returns 0.0872 percent against 0.0420 percent and cuts maximum drawdown from 72.56 percent to 43.99 percent (p.18).

The time-scale split is the finding: drawdown-based rules win at monthly momentum horizons, where the cumulative-return-minus-drawdown portfolio on sector ETFs returns 0.1724 percent per month against 0.1174 percent for cumulative return (p.16), while recovery-based rules win at weekly contrarian horizons (p.9, p.18). All portfolios are overlapping, formed one-sixth at a time and liquidated after the holding period (p.7).

`1208.2775v5` does the same exercise with a physics analogy, defining price momentum as mass times velocity in log-price space and giving eleven candidate definitions, of which cumulative return is the special case with unit mass (p.8, p.9). On the KOSPI 200 the best weekly contrarian criterion returns 0.2607 percent per week against 0.0685 percent for cumulative return with a lower standard deviation (p.14), and on the S&P 500 the inverse-volatility criterion is best (p.16). The mass proxy matters: volume- and turnover-based definitions outperform, whereas the inverse-volatility mass does not reproduce the effect on the KOSPI and can turn negative (p.13, p.14). Transaction costs are not applied in the comparison, so these are gross rankings (p.12).

The counterweight is `1907.11162v3`. It argues that a binary forecast measures the zeroth moment while a real-world payoff needs all moments, and that the two coincide only when the payoff is a Heaviside step function (p.2). Calibration metrics such as the Brier score belong to the thin-tailed class and misrepresent performance under fat tails, which is why a series of binary forecasts converges quickly to a Gaussian even when the underlying payoff is Pareto distributed (p.8, p.9). The operational lesson, stated in the paper's own words, is that "you do not eat forecasts": a strategy can be right more often than not and still lose, because the probability of an event is an expected payoff only under a step payoff (p.2, p.10).

## 5. Regime-switching statistical arbitrage and instrument-specific costs

`2309.00875v3` treats pairs trading as cointegration plus a mean-reverting spread and models the spread as an AR(1) whose parameters switch with a hidden Markov chain, estimated online by a filter-based EM algorithm (p.4, p.6). The data are daily and weekly continuous Brent, WTI and Shanghai crude oil futures, from the Shanghai contract's launch on 26 March 2018 to 30 June 2023, giving 1373 daily and 273 weekly observations, with a training sample ending 1 July 2022 and a one-year test sample (p.13, p.14).

The Johansen trace test finds exactly one cointegrating relationship among the three prices (p.15), and the error-correction coefficients show the adjustment runs through the Shanghai contract: its coefficient is positive and significant at 1 percent while Brent and WTI do not adjust, a unidirectional long-run causality (p.15, p.16). The estimated spread is the Brent price minus 0.6982 times the Shanghai price minus 0.3402 times the WTI price plus a constant, and the preferred number of latent states is two (p.16, p.17). That is the economic reason the recently introduced contract is the profitable leg: it corrects the disequilibrium.

Performance is measured with transaction costs estimated as average daily half-spreads: Brent 5.80 bps, WTI 20.24 bps, Shanghai 53.71 bps, and Dubai 1.52 bps (p.12). At the baseline bandwidth the forward-looking prediction-interval strategy returns 15.18 percent annualised with a Sharpe ratio of 1.18, 12 completed trades and 83.3 percent of positive trading-window returns, with a bootstrapped maximum drawdown of -5.73 percent against -15.54 percent for the S&P 500 proxy and -39.47 percent for the crude-oil ETF (p.21, p.23). Across bandwidth levels the model-based prediction-interval and predicted-increment strategies dominate the backward-looking probability-interval and realised-increment rules, and the White Reality Check repeatedly selects the prediction-interval strategy (p.21, p.22). The magnitudes are consistent with the 6 to 8 percent annual excess returns previously documented for commodity-futures statistical arbitrage (p.22).

## 6. News, language models, and the leakage that flatters them

`1508.04332v2` couples a news-driven agent-based model with empirical news flow and backtests four purely news-based SPY strategies over 1995-2015, reporting out-of-sample results from 2003 (p.34). Mean monthly-compounded returns are 16.6 to 19.0 percent per annum with volatility 13.9 to 15.5 percent and Sharpe ratios 0.97 to 1.23, against 0.39 for SPY and 0.62 for a price-based momentum-reversal benchmark, with average holding periods of 8.6 to 44.6 business days (p.35). The single strategy later run live shows 25.1 percent annual volatility, a 5 percent monthly value-at-risk of -4.7 percent and a Sharpe ratio of 1.07 after an 0.80 percent per annum cost drag (p.37). The model's claim is structural: the market is quasi-efficient intraday and inefficient beyond one day, because the news-price feedback loop is negligible at short timescales and leading-order at long ones (p.30, p.32, p.39).

The LLM papers make that claim expensive to test. `2309.17322v1` compares GPT-3.5 sentiment strategies on original and anonymised headlines over an in-sample window ending September 2021 and an out-of-sample window to December 2022 (p.4). In sample the anonymised headlines do better, 30.97 versus 25.08 basis points per day on the scraped set and 81.64 versus 77.27 on the Thomson Reuters set, with p-values 0.066 and 0.017 (p.9). The original prompt is wrong less often, 17.6 percent against 38.95 percent, but its errors are far larger: it loses 242 basis points when the anonymised prompt stays neutral against 97 basis points in the mirror case (p.10). The strategy also trades systematically larger companies, 35.37 billion against 17.91 billion USD in sample (p.12).

`2512.23847v2` turns that worry into a diagnostic. It defines Lookahead Propensity as the probability the model assigns to "up" or "down" on a date-only recall query that contains no headline at all (p.3, p.10). LAP is materially positive throughout the in-sample period and collapses to essentially zero immediately after the December 2023 training cutoff; across all 7,806 firm-day recall queries in 2024 the maximum is below 10^-4 and not one exceeds 0.5, and the Kodak query for 29 July 2020 returns P(up) of about 0.9999 with no headline supplied (p.3, p.11). In the headline-to-return task the recall-query directional signal alone predicts a 26 basis point higher next-day return per unit move (t of 3.53), and restricted to the top half of LAP the coefficient rises to 0.306 (t of 3.59) while in the bottom half it is statistically zero (p.19, p.20). The interaction between the LLM forecast and LAP is positive and significant in sample and statistically zero after the cutoff (p.5, p.20).

`2605.23905v1` is the slice's forward-looking frame, and it is a model rather than a measurement. It writes the alpha half-life as h(phi) = ln 2 / [theta + delta(phi)] with delta(phi) = N phi rho a / lambda(phi), so that half-life is convex-decreasing in AI adoption phi; at phi of 0.7 and rho of 0.6 the calibration implies an 18-month half-life for a medium-frequency factor against 5 to 7 years pre-AI (p.1, p.3, p.5). Three channels are combined: signal crowding when systems share training data and architectures, performative signal erosion when trading degrades the signal itself, and a Red Queen equilibrium in which aggregate AI investment is positive but net alpha is identically zero (p.3, p.5, p.20).

## 7. Crowding and the endogenous half-life of a signal

The supporting evidence for `2605.23905v1` is calibrated simulation, not observation: SEC Form 13F filings, 99.5 million holdings over 2013-2024, are used to calibrate a portfolio-convergence model that reports a 42 percent rise in simulated pairwise cosine similarity over the sample, and the 2010 Flash Crash is reproduced as a fragility illustration (p.1, p.6). The context it frames is the useful part: 91 percent of asset-management institutions report using or planning to use AI, and algorithmic trading is estimated at 60-80 percent of US equity volume (p.3).

Whether or not the half-life formula is right, the direction agrees with the rest of the slice. `2206.15365v10` already reports that out-of-sample tests that are close to the original tests show decay of 20, 26 and 36 percent (p.19). A signal that is published and widely implementable is a signal whose excess return is being competed away, and the practical question for any research pipeline is not whether a factor is real but how long it stays real at a given capacity.

## Caveats

The evidence is uneven and mostly single-market. `2605.23905v1` has no data at all: its empirical section is a calibrated simulation matched to Form 13F filings, so the 42 percent convergence figure and the 18-month half-life are model outputs, not observations. `2206.15365v10` derives bounds from other papers' summary statistics rather than a new dataset, so its conclusions are about interpretation as much as about evidence, and its headline bound is an equal-weighted, US-listed-stock bound. `2206.12282v1` covers US index constituents from 2015 to 2021, applies no transaction cost and does not consider shorting, so its win rates are gross and long-only. `2309.00875v3` covers three crude oil futures from 2018 to 2023 with a one-year test window and only 12 to 35 completed trades per strategy, so its returns rest on few observations even after bootstrapping. `1403.8125v4` and `1208.2775v5` share overlapping samples (KOSPI 200 from 2003 to 2012, S&P 500 from 1993 to 2012, sector ETFs from 1999 to 2012) and are gross of transaction costs; their ranking rules are not independent evidence. `1508.04332v2` is one market, one instrument family, and its live track is pro forma. `2512.23847v2` covers two applications and one model family (Llama-3.3-70B), and `2309.17322v1` one model version (GPT-3.5), so the leakage magnitudes are model-specific. `2209.00121v1` uses annual data and discusses housing costs rather than modelling them trade by trade. `1907.11162v3` is a mathematical argument with illustrative examples, not a portfolio backtest. Finally, several of these papers use overlapping windows and no costs; treat every ranking as gross until a costed run is produced.

## Papers read in depth

- `2209.00121v1` - 150 Years of Return Predictability Around the World: A Holistic View Across Assets (2022). Shows payout-ratio return timing failing across 48 country-asset combinations and locates the predictability in payout growth instead; limitation: annual data, and housing costs are discussed rather than modelled trade by trade.
- `2206.15365v10` - Most claimed statistical findings in cross-sectional return predictability are likely true (2022). Bounds the false-discovery rate at 8.5-25 percent and reconciles the literature; limitation: bounds are built from published summary statistics, not a new panel, and are equal-weighted.
- `2101.01006v2` - Design and analysis of momentum trading strategies (2021). Derives the skewness term structure of linear and nonlinear momentum rules from the position rule itself; limitation: a synthesis of theory with a light empirical check, not a portfolio backtest.
- `1403.8125v4` - Maximum drawdown, recovery, and momentum (2014). Drawdown and recovery ranking rules beat cumulative return at matched horizons and cut drawdown; limitation: equity universes to 2012, overlapping portfolios, gross of costs.
- `1208.2775v5` - Physical approach to price momentum and its application to momentum strategy (2012). Eleven momentum definitions with a mass proxy, of which cumulative return is the unit-mass special case; limitation: same overlapping samples as the drawdown paper, and transaction costs are not applied.
- `2206.12282v1` - A comparative study of the MACD-base trading strategies: evidence from the US stock market (2022). Documents sub-50 percent win rates and genetic-algorithm overfitting; limitation: no shorting, a bull-market sample, and no transaction costs.
- `2309.00875v3` - A hidden Markov model for statistical arbitrage in international crude oil futures markets (2023). Regime-switching cointegration with online EM and instrument-specific costs beats fixed bands; limitation: three contracts and a one-year out-of-sample window.
- `1508.04332v2` - Forecasting stock market returns over multiple time horizons (2015). A news-driven agent-based model with a live SPY strategy and the intraday-efficient, multi-day-inefficient claim; limitation: one market, one instrument family, and the live track is pro forma.
- `2309.17322v1` - Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated By GPT Sentiment Analysis (2023). Anonymisation shows distraction outweighs leakage in sample; limitation: one model version, headline-level signals, no transaction costs.
- `2512.23847v2` - Detecting Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts (2025). A portable leakage diagnostic via the date-only recall probability and its interaction with the forecast; limitation: two applications and one model family, with no trading-cost analysis.
- `1907.11162v3` - On the Statistical Differences between Binary Forecasts and Real World Payoffs (2019). Separates the zeroth moment a hit rate measures from the higher moments a payoff needs; limitation: a mathematical argument with illustrative examples rather than a backtest.
- `2605.23905v1` - AI-Driven Alpha Decay: Algorithmic Homogenization, Reflexive Signal Erosion, and the Paradox of Intelligent Markets (2026). A model of crowding, performative erosion and a Red Queen equilibrium with a half-life formula; limitation: empirical validation is entirely simulated.
