# Machine learning for finance: a fitted model's claim is bounded by its evaluation design, not its architecture

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers one slice of the local arXiv q-fin harvest: 156 records from 2008 to 2026 whose title or abstract touches machine learning applied to finance, 18 of them carrying a journal reference. It reads twelve of those papers in depth - three credit and default models, two fraud and platform-failure detectors, a matched loss-function experiment, a controlled architecture benchmark, four studies of large language models as measurement instruments or forecasters, and a systematic review of deep learning in asset management. It is written for the TradingAgents research layer, where deterministic calculators feed LangChain tools, LLM analysts emit scored decisions, and every claim is supposed to carry a number; the question each paper is asked is not "does it fit better" but "what is the largest claim this design can support".

## What this covers

The slice's arc is that general-finance machine learning has moved from "can it predict" to "what exactly did we measure". Credit models find small, honestly-bounded gains and show that a simpler model often wins: a graph-attention-plus-LSTM ensemble gains 0.87% AUC and 1.67% F1 over XGBoost on a 12-month mortgage default, and every static network baseline loses to plain XGBoost (`2402.00299v2`, p.20 and p.22). A matched experiment then shows the training objective moves a portfolio's Sharpe ratio more than the model class does: the classification ensemble earns a value-weighted Sharpe of 2.08 against 1.39 for the regression ensemble on identical features (`2108.02283v7`, p.2). A 918-run benchmark shows 99.90% of forecast-error variance is architecture and 0.01% is seed, yet directional accuracy sits at 50% for all 54 model-category-horizon combinations (`2603.16886v1`, p.1).

The single most consequential finding for this repo is about language models, because this repo has LLM analysts emitting measured claims. When 1,946 earnings calls are scored by seven models from seven providers on thirteen measures, the mean cross-provider rank correlation is only 0.52, and a crossed variance-components split attributes 34.4% of score variance to the transcript, 33.4% to the provider, and 32.2% to a provider-by-transcript interaction (`2609.31013v1`, p.3, p.4, p.16). For the typical construct, which foundation model scored the text matters nearly as much as which firm was scored - so a language-model-derived feature is a measurement with a provider term, not a reading of the firm.

## The short answer

1. Small, honestly-measured gains are what credit models deliver: an attention-based dynamic multilayer graph model reaches AUC 0.812 and F1 0.851 against XGBoost's 0.805 and 0.837, a 0.87% and 1.67% improvement, and every static network baseline loses to XGBoost (`2402.00299v2`, p.20 and p.22).
2. Deeper architectures do not reliably beat simpler ones: XGBoost beats every graph baseline (`2402.00299v2`, p.20), a plain CNN fusion beats ConvLSTM, ConvGRU and BERT on credit-rating fusion (`2304.10740v3`, p.28), and ConvGRU beats LSTM in every group (`2304.10740v3`, p.28).
3. Text can dominate numeric inputs, but the benchmark leaked: the text channel scores weighted AUC 0.915 against 0.808 for the best numeric channel, yet the covariate channel included the prior rating (`2304.10740v3`, p.31 and p.32).
4. A probability term structure is learnable from one model: a transformer encoder predicts a 3-month to 3-year default term structure with a 10-fold mean AUC of 0.869, and the fundamental channel contributes 30 percentage points on average, with the pricing channel decaying from 16.2% at three months to 5% at three years (`2111.09902v4`, p.27, p.28 and p.29).
5. Accuracy at a low base rate is not evidence: 99.72% accuracy and 0.987 AUC are reported where fraudulent transactions are about 0.2% of the set, so a constant "not fraud" rule scores about 99.8% (`2502.15822v1`, p.2 and p.4).
6. Define the costly error before choosing the metric: with a false acceptance defined as "predicted to survive but fails", the best platform-failure model reaches AUC 0.969 at false-acceptance 0.065 on six variables, and AUC is flat beyond seven variables while FAR keeps falling (`2311.14577v1`, p.4, p.5 and p.6).
7. The loss function moves portfolio outcomes as much as the model: a matched classification-versus-regression experiment gives a value-weighted Sharpe of 2.08 for classification against 1.39 for regression, and the linear benchmark repeats it, 1.57 against 1.03 (`2108.02283v7`, p.2 and p.10).
8. Architecture choice explains 99.90% of forecast-error variance versus 0.01% for random seed, so three seeds suffice for ranking - yet directional accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from 50% for every model, asset class and horizon (`2603.16886v1`, p.1).
9. Same-model outputs are largely reproducible for simple tasks: binary classification reaches Fleiss' Kappa above 0.93, but five-class classification drops to 55.66% perfect agreement over 50 runs, and 3 to 5 aggregated runs is the practical fix (`2503.16974v4`, p.15 and p.19).
10. Different-provider measures of the same text are not interchangeable: mean pairwise rank correlation is 0.52, sentiment is the only construct above 0.80 at 0.85, and the within-call standard deviation across providers is 0.75 times the total variation (`2609.31013v1`, p.3 and p.16).
11. A language model can recall the outcome instead of reasoning about the input: a date-only recall query yields a Lookahead Propensity that is materially positive in-sample and collapses to below 1e-4 after the training cutoff, but the test identifies the sign of contamination, not its magnitude (`2512.23847v2`, p.3 and p.15).
12. Language-model trading agents follow their prompts more faithfully than they pursue profit, which makes them a controlled experimental substrate and a systemic-correlation risk when many agents share one foundation model (`2504.10789v1`, p.2 and p.3).

## 1. Credit and default: small gains, measured honestly

The general-finance credit literature is where this repo can see what a defensible evaluation looks like, because the outcome is rare, delayed and audited. `2402.00299v2` builds a dynamic multilayer graph from Freddie Mac single-family loan data, one layer for borrower geography and one for the lending company, and passes monthly snapshots through a graph attention layer and an LSTM with a temporal attention mechanism. The double-layer network holds 297,040 training nodes and 108,151,460 edges in the first window (p.19); each window holds six one-month snapshots and predicts 12-month default (p.17 and p.18). The best configuration, GAT-LSTM-ATT on the double-layer network, reaches AUC 0.812 and F1 0.851 versus 0.805 and 0.837 for XGBoost on borrower features (p.22). The honest reading is in the baselines: static GCN and static GAT lose to logistic regression, XGBoost and a plain neural network (p.20), and the authors state that "more complex models do not always yield better performance" (p.20). The limits are one crisis-adjacent origination cohort (2009-2010 training, 2013 holdout) and a single country.

`2111.09902v4` treats default as a multi-label panel-classification problem over US mid-caps from 1990 to 2020, producing a full 3-month to 3-year probability term structure from one transformer encoder. The winning multimodal architecture with differential training reaches AUC 0.847 (p.26) and a stratified 10-fold mean AUC of 0.869 with a standard deviation of 0.011 (p.27). Its Shapley group analysis is the operational part: the fundamental channel contributes 30 percentage points on average, using fundamentals alone gives AUC 0.791 against 0.836 for all channels (p.28), and the pricing channel's contribution decays from 16.2% at three months to 5% at three years (p.29). Table 6 shows that over 52% of the fundamental channel's contribution comes from the most recent observed year (p.29). The limitations are a proprietary, non-shareable dataset and a single market.

`2304.10740v3` fuses earnings-call text with bond, market and financial-ratio streams across 27,854 records and rates companies into eight merged classes. Its best model is a hybrid-concatenation CNN-CNN at weighted AUC 0.932 (p.29), and its out-of-time and out-of-universe tests hold at 0.935 and 0.929 (p.31). The text channel alone reaches weighted AUC 0.915 versus 0.808 for the best numeric channel (p.31 and p.32) - but the covariate channel included the prior rating, so the comparison is not a clean text-versus-numbers test. The same paper finds ConvGRU better than LSTM in all groups and a plain CNN best (p.28), and reports that BERT was truncated at its 512-token limit against transcripts averaging about 4,000 tokens (p.36). One constructive number: the model's weighted AUC rose from 0.927 before COVID-19 to 0.964 during and after it (p.32 and p.33).

## 2. Fraud and platform failure: the base rate decides which metric is honest

`2502.15822v1` proposes a hybrid of gradient boosting and a simplified random forest and reports accuracy 99.72%, precision 94.83%, recall 93.45%, F1 94.14% and AUC 0.987 against 0.942 for a random forest and 0.961 for gradient boosting (p.4). The setup is a 70/15/15 split of a public credit-card fraud set plus desensitised bank data where fraud is "usually about 0.2%" (p.2 and p.3). At that base rate accuracy is close to meaningless, since a constant "not fraud" predictor scores about 99.8%. The paper reports no precision-recall area and no cost-sensitive threshold, and its reference list does not match its in-text citations (p.5), so the headline metrics should be treated as unverified.

`2311.14577v1` is the better template. It predicts failure of 2,438 Chinese peer-to-peer lending platforms from 29 characteristics, defines the costly error explicitly - a false positive is a platform "predicted to survive but actually fails", with the investor potentially losing everything (p.4) - and reports the false acceptance rate beside AUC. Its best model, XGBoost with forward-selected features, achieves AUC 0.969 and FAR 0.065 on six variables (p.6); a five-variable model keeps AUC 0.966 at FAR 0.076 (p.6); backward elimination gives AUC 0.968 at FAR 0.069 on seven variables (p.6). The transferable finding is that AUC is essentially flat once the feature count passes seven while FAR keeps falling slowly (p.5, Figure 2), so the trade-off is between interpretability and tail error, not between accuracy and accuracy. The limits are a single country and a single window (2014 to September 2018).

## 3. The loss function and the target are the model

`2108.02283v7` runs the cleanest experiment in the slice: matched model pairs that differ only in the loss function, on 102 firm characteristics, with an out-of-sample period from July 1983 to December 2024 giving 498 monthly observations (p.4 and p.9). Classification under categorical cross-entropy delivers a value-weighted Sharpe ratio of 2.08 for the stacking ensemble against 1.39 for the matched regression ensemble, and equal-weighted Sharpe ratios of 3.77 against 3.12 (p.2 and p.9). Classification wins in 49 of 50 paired comparisons of Sharpe ratio and factor alpha across weighting schemes (p.9). The mechanism is that cross-entropy trains the model to assign the correct return decile while mean squared error trains it to estimate magnitudes, a harder problem; the linear benchmarks isolate this because logistic regression shares the OLS functional form and still reaches a value-weighted Sharpe of 1.57 against 1.03 and a monthly six-factor alpha of 2.6% against 1.1% (p.3 and p.10). The paper also reports that the soft construction, which uses the full probability vector, beats the argmax-based hard construction at every class count (p.12 and p.13). After 30 basis points per one-way trade and a 50-basis-point annual borrow cost, the classification Sharpe stays at 1.85 while regression falls to 1.09 (p.14). The limit is US equities only.

## 4. Architecture, seed, and the directional-accuracy null

`2603.16886v1` is the cautionary companion. Across 918 runs of nine architectures on twelve instruments in three asset classes, ModernTCN has the best mean rank of 1.333 with a 75% first-place rate over 24 evaluation points and PatchTST is second at 2.000 (p.1). Architecture choice explains 99.90% of raw RMSE variance against 0.01% for seed, so three seeds suffice for ranking (p.1). But directional accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from 50% for every model, category and horizon (p.1), and error grows 2 to 2.5 times from a 4-hour to a 24-hour horizon (p.1). The returns are stationary with excess kurtosis of 15 to 96 (p.11), and every model uses strictly chronological 70/15/15 splits with no shuffling (p.10 and p.11). The operational reading is that MSE-trained price regressors rank prices accurately without being tradeable; the paper's own remedy is to redesign the loss function for direction rather than to add capacity. The limit is hourly bars over 2017-2026 across crypto, forex and index products.

## 5. Language models as measurement instruments: same model, same text, not the same number

`2503.16974v4` runs 50 independent generations per task over 50 days, producing 3,443,400 outputs from roughly 749,340 API calls and more than 84 million pairwise comparisons (p.13, p.15 and p.54). Simple tasks are reproducible: binary classification and sentiment analysis reach Fleiss' Kappa above 0.93 across text types (p.16), with GPT-4o-mini at 0.97 for binary and 0.86 for five-class classification while GPT-3.5-turbo is better than GPT-4o-mini on the five-class task at 0.91 (p.15). Perfect agreement over all 50 runs falls from 96.18% for binary to 55.66% for five classes (p.15). Summary length varies with a mean absolute relative difference near 8.5% between runs (p.16), while the cosine similarity between summaries stays at 0.98 (p.16) - so length is the unstable quantity, not the content. The most useful result for this repo is that the noise does not poison downstream inference: in a simulation over 10 million regressions, 97.35% of length-effect inferences were correct with a 1.28% Type I rate (p.20 and p.21). The practical fix is to aggregate three to five runs (p.19). The limit is a single provider and zero-shot prompting (p.22).

## 6. Cross-provider divergence: the model id is part of the measurement

`2609.31013v1` supplies the cross-provider test the previous paper does not. Seven models from seven providers score 1,946 earnings-call question-and-answer sections from 501 S&P 500 firms across 13 measures, 177,086 score requests in all, 98 (0.06%) unparseable (p.9 and p.12). Mean pairwise Spearman rank correlation across measures is 0.52; sentiment is the only construct with substantial agreement at 0.85, while integrity reaches 0.32 and a rule-based specificity ratio reaches only 0.23 (p.3 and p.16). The average within-call standard deviation across providers is 0.75 times the total cross-call variation, so for the typical construct which model scored the text matters nearly as much as which firm was scored (p.16). A variance-components analysis splits score variation into 34.4% transcript-level, 33.4% provider-level and 32.2% provider-by-transcript (p.4). Reliability at seven providers averages 0.836 for generalizability but only 0.720 for dependability, and only 5 of 13 measures clear 0.80 on the latter (p.25). The paper also finds that provider-specific downstream climate-risk coefficients span -0.4 to +0.2 with only one provider significant (p.28 and p.30). The limitation is a fixed API prompt with no retrieval or reasoning scaffolding (p.38).

## 7. Lookahead, memorisation, and the post-cutoff placebo

`2512.23847v2` turns the leakage worry into a portable diagnostic. It sends the model a date-only recall query containing a firm name, ticker and date and nothing else, and reads the first-token probabilities of "up", "down" and "unknown"; Lookahead Propensity is the total probability on the two directional labels (p.10). LAP is materially positive throughout the in-sample window and collapses to essentially zero after the model's December 2023 training cutoff: across 7,806 post-cutoff firm-day queries the maximum LAP is below 1e-4 (p.11), and across 6,619 post-cutoff earnings firm-quarters the maximum is below 1e-5 (p.11). The worked example is the Kodak loan announcement, where a query with no headline yields P(up) of about 0.9999 on a +318% intraday move (p.3 and p.9). The test is honest about its own reach: it identifies the positive sign of the contamination loading, not its magnitude, requires a documented cutoff and token-level probabilities, and cannot separate reasoning from memorisation in a plain accuracy regression (p.15). The limit is one model and a task-specific prompt.

## 8. Language-model agents as a controlled experimental substrate

`2504.10789v1` implements a continuous double-auction market with a persistent order book, market and limit orders, partial fills, stochastic dividends and a fundamental value, populated by GPT-4o agents that submit structured decisions and natural-language reasoning (p.9 and p.10). The baseline runs 15 to 20 rounds with eight agents, 1,000,000 units of cash and 10,000 shares each, a fundamental value of 28, a dividend of 1.40 plus or minus 1.00 with 50% probability on each branch, and 5% interest per round (p.29). Transaction costs are set to zero to isolate agent behaviour and market structure (p.29), and the systematic analysis varies the price-to-fundamental ratio from 0.1 to 3.5 (p.22). The central behavioural result is that agents follow their system prompt faithfully regardless of profit implications, so an LLM trader is an instruction-follower rather than a profit maximiser (p.2 and p.3). The framework flags correlated behaviour across agents sharing one foundation model as an unquantified systemic-risk concern (p.4 and p.35). The limit is a synthetic zero-cost environment with no live validation.

## 9. Where the field actually is: the survey

`2503.01591v1` screens 934 Scopus articles from 2018 to 2023 and retains 612 with a genuine deep-learning application to financial asset management (p.12). The distribution is lopsided: price forecasting accounts for 449 of 612, portfolio management 98, algorithmic trading 48 and value/factor investing 17 (p.15). Electricity price forecasting alone is 43% of the commodity-price articles (p.15), so the aggregate counts overstate how much of the field is trading-relevant. A naive or random-walk baseline appears in only 4.0% of price-forecast papers, interval forecasts in 6.4% and classification forecasts in 5.1% (p.22), and algorithmic-trading articles report a Sharpe ratio in 62.5% and a maximum drawdown in 39.6% of the 48 (p.19). Filtering to AJG-2024 journals cuts the sample to 165 and pushes naive-baseline use up from 4.0% to 15.6% (p.25 and p.27). The recurring caution is the one this brief keeps meeting: the literature needs robust validation, out-of-sample discipline and interpretability (p.4). The limit is a single database and a keyword filter.

## Caveats

Several papers here have no usable public data: `2111.09902v4` uses proprietary mid-cap data, `2304.10740v3` uses licensed WRDS streams with only a synthetic release, and `2502.15822v1`'s dataset is public but its benchmark reporting is thin and its reference list does not match its citations, so its 99.72% accuracy should not be relied on. The credit and failure papers are single-market and single-period - Freddie Mac loans originated 2009-2010, US mid-caps 1990-2020, Chinese P2P platforms 2014 to September 2018 - so none establishes that the same feature sets transfer across the markets this repo covers; where a finding is only actionable in an execution layer (fill economics, borrow cost), it is routed here to the smallest research-layer consequence that exists and not to a matching engine. The language-model results are snapshots of specific checkpoints: cross-provider divergence is measured on seven cost-efficient API models in 2026, same-model reproducibility on three OpenAI models in 2024-2025, and lookahead on one Llama-3.3-70B with a December 2023 cutoff, so none is a law about future models. Overlapping-window and no-cost limits apply to `2503.16974v4` and `2504.10789v1`, which are measurement and simulation studies rather than tradeable results; the loss-function and architecture experiments (`2108.02283v7`, `2603.16886v1`) carry costs only in the forms the authors chose. This brief also does not restate what `Strategies/books/` covers on supervised learning generally; where that sibling library already treats the architecture question, this brief adds the evaluation-design and measurement findings only.

## Papers read in depth

- `2402.00299v2` - Attention-based Dynamic Multilayer Graph Neural Networks for Loan Default Prediction (2024). Builds borrower networks over geography and lender; the GAT-LSTM-attention model gains under 2% AUC over XGBoost, and the sample is one crisis-adjacent origination cohort.
- `2111.09902v4` - A transformer-based model for default prediction in mid-cap corporate markets (2021, European Journal of Operational Research). Produces a 3-month to 3-year PD term structure from multimodal panel data; strong cross-validated AUC but proprietary data and one market.
- `2304.10740v3` - Multi-Modal Deep Learning for Credit Rating Prediction Using Text and Numerical Data Streams (2023, Applied Soft Computing). Shows text can dominate numeric channels for credit ratings and that a plain CNN beats recurrent and BERT fusions; the covariate channel leaked the prior rating.
- `2502.15822v1` - Financial fraud detection system based on improved random forest and gradient boosting machine (GBM) (2025). Reports near-perfect accuracy and AUC on a 0.2%-fraud set; the metrics are base-rate-blind and the benchmark reporting is not verifiable.
- `2311.14577v1` - Predicting Failure of P2P Lending Platforms through Machine Learning: The Case in China (2023, Finance Research Letters). Best template for cost-asymmetric evaluation: AUC 0.969 at false-acceptance 0.065 on six features; single country and period.
- `2108.02283v7` - Machine Learning Classification and Portfolio Construction: Does the Loss Function Matter? (2021, Financial Analysts Journal). Matched classification-versus-regression experiment isolating the loss function; US equities only.
- `2603.16886v1` - A Controlled Comparison of Deep Learning Architectures for Multi-Horizon Financial Forecasting (2026). Nine architectures, 918 runs, architecture explains 99.90% of error variance while directional accuracy sits at chance; hourly, three asset classes.
- `2503.16974v4` - Assessing Consistency and Reproducibility in the Outputs of Large Language Models (2025). Fifty runs across five finance tasks; same-model reproducibility is high for simple tasks and weaker for complex ones.
- `2609.31013v1` - Same Text, Different Numbers: The Divergence of LLM-Based Measures (2026). Seven providers score the same calls and disagree; measurement-only, so validity is left open.
- `2512.23847v2` - Detecting Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts (2025). Date-only recall query yields a Lookahead Propensity that collapses after the training cutoff; identifies the sign of contamination, not its magnitude.
- `2504.10789v1` - Can Large Language Models Trade? Testing Financial Theories with LLM Agents in Market Simulations (2025). Open double-auction with GPT-4o agents; stylised, zero-cost environment with no live validation.
- `2503.01591v1` - The Role of Deep Learning in Financial Asset Management: A Systematic Review (2025). PRISMA review of 612 articles; single-database census dominated by price forecasting and electricity markets.
