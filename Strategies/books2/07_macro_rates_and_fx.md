# Macro, rates and foreign exchange: the tradable macro signal in this corpus is a scheduled-announcement parallel shift of the whole curve plus a Taylor-rule currency factor, and a single-name equity research layer can consume it only through its regime, sector-duration and rate-convention surfaces.

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers the macro, interest-rate and foreign-exchange slice of a local arXiv harvest: 140 records spanning 2001 to 2026, 30 with a journal reference, 40 screened and 12 read from the PDFs. TradingAgents is a single-name equity research and analysis layer - deterministic calculators in `tradingagents/strategies/*.py`, wired as LangChain tools under `tradingagents/agents/utils/`, driven by LLM analysts, gated by `enable_*` config keys and scored by the score engines - and it holds no bond book and no currency position. The brief is therefore written to answer one operational question: which of these rate and FX findings can change a number, a label or a prompt in this repository, and which cannot.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from the institutional prehistory of the yield curve, through parametric and information-geometric ways of fitting and comparing curves, the high-frequency evidence that central-bank announcements co-jump a term structure in parallel, the recovery of interest rates from listed derivatives where no bond market exists, the conversion of a data release into a currency signal, the asymmetric and regime-dependent response of inflation expectations, and the combination of macro density forecasts under a simplex constraint. Its single most consequential finding for this repository is negative and structural: the one macro object with genuine tradable co-movement - a policy-announcement parallel shift of the curve `1905.01541v1` - has no bond book here to trade it, so it transfers only as an environmental flag on the regime read and as a duration mapping on the sector-driver surface. The repo is single-name equity oriented; the sovereign-curve and FX findings that survive are inputs to its regime, Taylor-rule and sector-duration readings, and the position-level FX and curve trades do not transfer at all.

Selection rule: read the records whose abstracts measure a curve, a rate, an exchange-rate reaction or an inflation-expectation relation with data, plus the two papers that define what a curve is an object of, preferring high-frequency, derivative-based and causal evidence over structural macro models without estimation; also read the two papers whose FX and business-cycle channels are directly checkable against this repository's macro surfaces.

## The short answer

1. A yield curve is an institutional object before it is a mathematical one: it requires standardised instruments, comparable borrowers, several maturities, observable prices and a secondary market, none of which existed for most of credit history `2609.07958v1`.
2. Where no bond market exists, zero-coupon curves can be recovered from listed derivatives: call-put parity on inverse options combined with inverse futures replicates synthetic zero-coupon bonds and yields implied rates `2509.03964v2`.
3. Central-bank announcements are where the curve co-jumps: co-jump activity is about twice as large in US Treasury futures as in European bond futures, and about twice as large at the long end as the short end `1905.01541v1`.
4. The default announcement response is a parallel level shift: roughly 30% of announcements shift the whole curve while only about 1% rotate it, so a slope or rotation shock should be a separate, rare scenario `1905.01541v1`.
5. Co-jumps raise the measured correlation of the curve: on median estimates the total correlation exceeds the continuous-only correlation by 0.036 to 0.065 in the US and 0.035 to 0.079 in Europe, so a jump-free estimate understates curve co-movement `1905.01541v1`.
6. AI-read macro data releases give a cross-sectional currency signal with an annualised Sharpe above 0.7 and an alpha worth 74% of the strategy return at a 48-month lookback, and the paper's four look-ahead tests do not reject genuine reasoning `2608.00761v1`.
7. That signal's mechanism is the Taylor rule: inflation, employment and broad-activity releases dominate, which is the monetary-policy channel this repository already models as a Taylor-rule deviation `2608.00761v1`.
8. Bitcoin falls about 24 bps on a one-standard-deviation inflation surprise (t = 2.44) while no major cryptocurrency reacts to growth news, so the crypto-inflation-hedge trade has the wrong sign `2301.10117v1`.
9. Household inflation expectations respond asymmetrically to news, and the asymmetry became statistically significant in the early 1990s: rising-inflation news moves expectations more than falling-inflation news `2009.11557v1`.
10. The monetary regime does not neutralise the inflation-uncertainty link: in most of six Eastern European economies higher inflation is associated with higher inflation volatility, with regime credibility determining the exceptions `1303.6192v1`.
11. Regularised mixtures of density forecasts beat every individual ECB-SPF inflation forecaster, and the simplex constraint is mandatory because negative or non-summing mixture weights make the density invalid or the optimum undefined `2012.11649v3`.
12. On thin auction data the four-parameter Nelson-Siegel model fits at least as well as the more flexible Svensson model, so extra flexibility is not free `2310.00321v2`.

## 1. What a yield curve is made of, and why this repository cannot have one

The history paper `2609.07958v1` makes the point that a curve is not implied by the existence of credit. Interest-bearing credit is documented from around 2400 BCE (the Cone of Entemena) and the Code of Hammurabi, circa 1750 BCE, fixes benchmark rates of 33 1/3% for grain and 20% for silver, rates that recur for centuries (p.2). Classical Greek rates fall from 16%-18% at the time of Solon to 10%-12% in the fifth century BCE and 6%-10% by the end of the second century BCE, and normal Roman rates in the first century CE run 4%-6% (p.3). Yet the evidence is too heterogeneous to order rates by maturity, because most loans ran under a year and there was no secondary market to reveal the term structure (p.3). The decisive later steps were standardisation and transparency in public finance: the British 3% consols were launched in the mid-eighteenth century and their yields can be followed until 1888, while France's remaining perpetual rentes were redeemed in 1982 and the British consols in 2015 (p.8-9). The paper states six conditions for a curve to exist and concludes that interpolation is the last step, done by mathematics after institutions have made comparable points observable (p.9-10). The Burundi study `2310.00321v2` is the modern version of the same story: domestic public debt multiplied about 34 times between 2000 and 2019, the bond share of that debt rose from 11% to 65%, commercial banks went from holding 0% to 90% of sovereign securities, and the central bank obliged primary dealers to sell at least 10% of primary-market acquisitions onward and to quote within a +/-10% band up to 7 years and +/-15% above (p.4). This repository holds none of that scaffolding; its only rate-convention primitives are the discount-factor and forward-rate helpers in `tradingagents/strategies/rate_utils.py`, and its only curve-fitting entry point is the no-extrapolation interpolator `monotone_fill` in the same module. The honest read is that this repo cannot build a term structure and should not pretend to.

## 2. The curve co-jumps in parallel on policy days

`1905.01541v1` is the strongest evidence in the slice for event-driven rates behaviour and the most consequential finding for this repository's event surfaces. Using one-minute and five-minute CME and Eurex bond futures for 2, 5, 10 and 30 years over 5 January 2007 to 28 December 2017, on 2705 US and 2780 European trading days (p.5, p.8), the authors separate the continuous covariation of the curve from co-jumps using wavelet coefficients and a Hausman-type bootstrap test (p.13-14). The 9-hour US window used carries 83% of all trade volume (p.5). For the 5-year/10-year pair the US market shows significant co-jumps on 444 of 2705 days and the 5-year/30-year pair on 365; for Europe the corresponding counts are 217 of 2780 and 240 (p.14-15). The share of quadratic covariation attributable to co-jumps is 7.11% for the US 5-30 pair and 5.86% for the US 5-10 pair, against 1.58% and 2.31% in Europe (Table 3, p.15), and the paper's summary is that co-jumps matter about twice as much in the US as in Europe and about twice as much at the long end as at the short end (p.15). Co-jumps also inflate the correlation structure: on median estimates the total correlation exceeds the continuous-only correlation by 0.036 to 0.065 in the US and 0.035 to 0.079 in Europe, with the US 5-10 pair at 0.959 total versus 0.894 continuous (Table A.7, p.30). The announcement evidence is the actionable part. Using 103 FOMC and 119 ECB announcement days (p.7, p.9; one later regression reports 113 ECB days), the probability of a co-jump rises sharply in the 30-minute window after an FOMC release, and the effect is weaker for the ECB (p.19-20). For the representative 2-year/10-year pair, 32 of the 103 FOMC days show significant co-jumps; 31 of those are positive level shifts and exactly one is negative, the 18 March 2008 rate cut (p.25). On the 2602 non-announcement US days there are only 8 such days for that pair, and only 3 days on which the whole four-contract curve shifts without news (p.25-26). Across both markets, roughly 30% of announcements are accompanied by a level shift of the whole curve and only about 1% are interpreted as a change in central-bank preferences, so the default event model is a parallel shift and rotation is the rare case (p.4, p.26). This is covariation evidence, not a tradable edge, and it lands here only as an environmental flag: the repo's `regime_score` engine (`tradingagents/strategies/regime_score.py`, legs `market_trend` and `vix_term_structure`) and its event calendar are where a policy-window flag belongs.

## 3. Where no bond market exists, recover rates from derivatives

`2509.03964v2` confronts a currency with a deep derivatives market and no fixed-income market. The authors use call-put parity on inverse options, combined with inverse futures, to replicate the payoff of a zero-coupon bond (p.17-18), and because the ratio of the call-put difference to moneyness is not constant across strikes they estimate both zero-coupon prices by a joint weighted least-squares objective with a closed-form two-parameter solution (p.19-20), after trimming with RANSAC on `Put_ask - Call_bid` against `Call_ask - Put_bid` and dropping any date-maturity pair whose fitted slope deviates from -1 (p.20-21). The magnitudes matter. Using Deribit data at hourly frequency from January 2022 to December 2024 (p.20), BTC implied rates at a 90-day tenor average 0.0007 (0.07%) with a minimum of -0.1011; at 180 days they average 0.0004; at 360 days -0.0001 (Table 3, p.24). ETH implied rates are negative on average at all three tenors, around -0.0014 to -0.0023 (Table 4, p.24), which the authors flag as puzzling given an Ethereum staking yield near 2% (p.24). The reference-currency curve behaved very differently: Deribit USD rates were very low or negative in the FTX and Terra turmoil of late 2022, rose above 20% at points in 2024, and then stabilised between 10% and 15% (p.24). On 5 November 2024 the USD curve recovered from BTC options ran from about 9.15% to 10.12%, the one from ETH options from about 8.10% to 8.73% (p.23), and the BTC-versus-ETH USD spread averages about 1% and shrinks with tenor (Table 5, p.27). This repository already computes a put-call parity check: `parity_violation` in `tradingagents/strategies/options_surface.py`. A parity-implied rate is the smallest useful extension, but it must be labelled analysis-only because the repo has no curve to trade.

## 4. Fitting and comparing curves: parsimony beats flexibility

Two papers give tools rather than findings. `1111.3757v1` shows that with positive interest rates the discount function is a complementary distribution function, so minus its derivative with respect to maturity is a probability density (p.6-7). The space of admissible term structures is therefore the convex space of densities on the positive real line (p.6-7), and two curves can be compared by Bhattacharyya spherical distance or by the Fisher-Rao metric, with the whole curve evolution representable as a measure-valued process (p.12). Maximum entropy subject to observed moments gives an explicit interpolation - if only the mean is known, the maximum-entropy density is exponential and the rate is the reciprocal of the mean (p.13) - which is a defensible default when only one point is known. The paper also warns that moments may not exist: the flat simple-yield density has none, and for a compounding frequency kappa the moments exist only up to order kappa minus one (p.13), so any moment-based curve comparison needs an existence guard. `2310.00321v2` tests the two standard parametric forms on the Burundi auction data. From 112 raw auction dates with maturities from 13 weeks to eight years the authors reduce to 30 dates each carrying seven securities - three bills and four bonds - giving 210 securities (p.11). Fitting by differential evolution and comparing errors, Nelson-Siegel has a median error of 0.7129 on the 67-day imputed dataset against 0.7274 for Svensson, and the difference is significant on both the original data (t-test p = 2.678e-3) and the imputed data (Wilcoxon rank-sum p = 3.867e-7) (Table 3, Table 6, p.15-16). The mean Nelson-Siegel error is stable at about 0.84 across both datasets (p.16-17), and the authors conclude that Svensson is over-parameterised for an illiquid market (p.18). If this repo ever fits a curve it should start from the four-parameter Nelson-Siegel form, but one African sovereign market is one market and not a law.

## 5. Turning a macro release into a currency signal, and why this repository cannot trade it

`2608.00761v1` is the paper this repository should study most closely for what a macro-calendar input can be. The author feeds the realised value, previous figure and consensus forecast of each economic data release to GPT-4o, with the release date deliberately excluded, and asks for a direction (STRENGTHEN, WEAKEN, INSIGNIFICANT OR UNCERTAIN) (p.3, p.9). The calendar covers 544 unique indicators and 174,820 data points from January 1996 to October 2024, with nine G-10 exchange rates and one-month forwards (p.8). The resulting AIFX index is the net balance of positive and negative signals over a lookback window (p.10-11). Sorting the currencies and going long the top two and short the bottom two produces annualised Sharpe ratios above 0.7, strongest for 36- to 60-month lookbacks (p.2, p.12), and at a 48-month lookback the strategy's alpha after controlling for dollar, dollar carry, carry, momentum and value accounts for 74% of its average return (p.13). The look-ahead work is what makes the result usable rather than decorative: asked to guess the release year from the same inputs the model is right only 5.6% of the time on average within each year (p.5), a difference-in-differences comparison of GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 across the 2021 knowledge-cutoff boundary finds no significant divergence (p.21), and a pure hindsight portfolio built from what the model remembers of monthly returns has a Sharpe of 0.91, skewness of 2.52 and excess kurtosis of 18.61, yet regressing the AIFX strategy on it gives a significant positive alpha, a negative beta and information ratios of 0.75 to 0.85 (p.22-23). The paper attributes the mechanism to the Taylor rule: inflation, employment and broad-activity releases dominate, and the signal is driven more by positive news than negative (p.6). The limits are real: the result is gross of costs and turnover, one vendor's calendar, and the currency position itself has no landing in this single-name equity repository. What transfers is the input format - realised, previous and consensus with a publication timestamp - and the mechanism, which is exactly the Taylor-rule deviation this repo already computes in `tradingagents/strategies/cycle_tilt.py` (`taylor_rule`, `taylor_deviation`, `macro_stance`).

## 6. Inflation surprises hit assets with different signs

`2301.10117v1` studies the reaction of six major cryptocurrencies to macroeconomic news announcements in a 30-minute window from ten minutes before to twenty minutes after the release (p.9). The sample runs from January 2013 to May 2021 with 99-101 observations per announcement type (p.6). No cryptocurrency reacts significantly to growth news, with responses under 2 bps per standard deviation (p.9). Bitcoin loses 24 bps per one-standard-deviation inflation surprise, with t = 2.44, and the regression explains 2.5% of announcement-window return variation; Bitcoin Cash loses 18 bps (t = -2.218) (p.10, Table 6). The mechanism is not the interest-rate channel: the 5-year Treasury yield rises significantly on both growth (t = 10.1) and inflation (t = 7.5) surprises (Table 8, p.11), yet the relation between Bitcoin returns and the rate change is insignificant (t = 0.888 for the inflation window) (Table 9, p.11). The author's preferred explanation is a consumption-savings shift: higher expected inflation lowers savings and net inflows to risk assets. This repository reads crypto only through `get_crypto_prices` in `tradingagents/agents/utils/market_position_tools.py`; there is no crypto strategy module, so the transferable content is the sign convention - crypto is short inflation surprises - not a hedge position. `2009.11557v1` supplies the expectations side from the Michigan Survey of Consumers, January 1978 to February 2016, 208,777 individual records (p.9). It finds a structural break in median inflation expectations in September 1983 (p.10); median expectations fall from 6% to 3% across the break and the cross-sectional variance of expectations falls from 34.3 to 15.2 (p.11, note 7). Hearing news of higher inflation raises expectations by 1.03 percentage points in the first sub-sample and 0.46 in the second, while hearing news of contractionary monetary policy raises them by 0.49 in the first sub-sample, which is the signalling interpretation of a Taylor rule rather than the transmission one (p.13-14, Table 1). The asymmetry is the operational point: news of lower inflation moves expectations by only -0.22 in the second sub-sample, and the asymmetry z-score becomes significant (2.72) only after 1991, while the monetary-policy asymmetry appears after 1999 (p.20-21). Under the zero lower bound from June 2008, inflation news has a larger effect (1.22) and the monetary-policy effect runs through consumer sentiment (p.25-26). Any sentiment or survey feature in this repo should be fitted with a regime break rather than one constant coefficient.

## 7. Combining macro density forecasts without overconfidence

`2012.11649v3` is the slice's answer to the question of what to do with many inflation-related forecasts. It combines density forecasts from the ECB Survey of Professional Forecasters, 83 quarterly surveys from 1999Q1 to 2019Q3, screening 103 unique forecasters down to 18 and adding a uniform forecaster for 19 in total (p.21, p.23). The key constraint is that mixture weights must satisfy the unit simplex - non-negative and summing to one - because negative mixture weights can drive the mixture density negative and without sum-to-one the log-score optimum is undefined (p.7-8). With that constraint plus ridge or entropy regularisation, every regularised mixture beats every individual forecaster in the survey pool, including the ex post best one: the simplex estimator scores -1.88, the best 4-average -1.87, the ex post best individual forecaster -2.02 and the median forecaster -2.17 (Table 3, p.23), so the best 4-average is about 15% better than the median forecaster and about 7% better than the ex post best (p.23). The substantive regime result is that after the Great Recession the optimal regularisation moves probability mass from the centre to the tails, correcting overconfidence, while before the recession it moved mass upward in location (p.2, p.24-25). This repository already declares equal-weight forecast combination and proper scoring rules - `equal_weight_combination` in `tradingagents/strategies/forecast_registry.py` and `crps`/`score_forecast` in `tradingagents/strategies/forecast_scores.py` - and the paper says the simplex-constrained equal-weight mixture is a strong and tuning-free baseline, which is what that repo benchmark already is.

## 8. Regime does not neutralise the inflation-uncertainty link

`1303.6192v1` checks whether the monetary regime changes the inflation-uncertainty relationship, using monthly data from January 2000 to July 2011 for three currency-board economies (Bulgaria, Estonia, Lithuania) and three inflation-targeting economies (Czech Republic, Poland, Romania) (p.5). Inflation standard deviations are higher under currency boards - 3.34, 2.85 and 3.33 - than under inflation targeting - 1.83, 1.58 and 7.71 for the outlier Romania (Table 1, p.6). The EGARCH asymmetry parameter is positive and significant for Bulgaria (0.615), the Czech Republic (0.523) and Romania (0.116), supporting the view that higher inflation is more volatile there, while the sign is negative for Estonia (-0.17) and Poland (-0.081), which the authors attribute to regime credibility (Table 3, p.7-8). The paper's conclusion is that the empirical results do not allow a judgement on which regime is better and that EU accession is a likely reason the differences numbed (p.9). The operational transfer is a caution, not a coefficient: do not assume that a policy regime makes macro factor exposures stable, and test exposure stability per regime on `tradingagents/strategies/regime_performance.py` (`regime_conditioned_performance`, `regime_stability_check`).

## 9. What transfers here and what does not

This repository is single-name equity oriented and holds no bond or FX book, so the transfer has to be stated case by case. The announcement-window parallel shift `1905.01541v1` transfers as an environmental flag on the regime read and as the duration mapping already declared in `tradingagents/strategies/sector_drivers.py`, where XLK, XLU, XLRE and XLC are inversely driven by the 10-year Treasury and XLF positively by the 10-year/2-year spread; it does not transfer as a curve trade. The derivative-implied rate `2509.03964v2` transfers as a parity-implied rate beside `parity_violation`; it does not transfer as a funding-curve risk input because there is no curve to mark. The FX signal `2608.00761v1` transfers as a macro-calendar input and a Taylor-rule mechanism; the currency position does not transfer at all. The crypto sign `2301.10117v1` transfers as a stated convention on the one crypto read that exists. The curve-fitting and density-comparison tools `2310.00321v2` and `1111.3757v1` transfer as primitives in `rate_utils.py` with moment-existence guards; they do not transfer as a production curve. The density-combination result `2012.11649v3` transfers directly because the repo already publishes an equal-weight benchmark and proper scoring rules. The single-country FX-equity relation in `2207.06963v1` - over 243 daily returns from April 2016 to March 2017, Nifty return on USD/INR return gives a coefficient of -0.894 (t = -5.44, R-squared 0.109) with the demonetisation dummy significant in the GARCH variance equation (Table 3, Table 5) - is one country over one year and is not transferable as a beta; it only justifies registering a dollar-index driver, which `sector_drivers.py` already does.

## Caveats

Every finding in this slice is narrow in time and place and none of it reports net-of-cost performance for an instrument this repository can hold. The co-jump study covers 2007 to 2017 in two futures venues and measures covariation, not tradable edge `1905.01541v1`. The FX result is one vendor's calendar, gross of costs and turnover, and is a currency position the repo cannot take `2608.00761v1`. The crypto-news result is six assets over 2013 to 2021 `2301.10117v1`; the inflation-expectation result is US households only `2009.11557v1`; the regime comparison is six small open economies over eleven years `1303.6192v1`; the implicit-rate result is one venue with heavy RANSAC trimming `2509.03964v2`; the parametric-curve comparison is a single frontier market with imputed maturities `2310.00321v2`; the density-combination result is survey data with ex post optimal penalties `2012.11649v3`; the information-geometry paper is a mathematical construction with no empirical calibration `1111.3757v1`; the FX-equity relation is one country over one year with a GARCH model that reports no cost or out-of-sample test `2207.06963v1`; and the business-cycle paper `2403.04104v1`, while the most rigorous causal study in the slice, is about US mortgage credit and housing employment from 1999 to 2010 and does not identify a rate or FX mechanism. Several papers are single-country or single-period and none is a law. Two hard gaps matter for this repo: it has no bond book, so the sovereign-curve findings land only as environmental flags or arithmetic primitives, and it has no FX or crypto strategy module, so the FX and crypto findings land only as input formats or sign conventions. Where a row above says a finding is analysis-only, that is the honest ceiling of the research layer, not an execution limitation.

## Papers read in depth

- `2608.00761v1` - AI and Exchange Rate Predictability (2026). Turns macro data releases into a directional currency signal via an LLM, with unusually thorough look-ahead tests; no cost analysis, one vendor's calendar, and no landing for the currency position in an equity repo.
- `1905.01541v1` - Co-jumping of Treasury Yield Curve Rates (2019). Wavelet separation of co-jumps in US and European bond futures tied to FOMC and ECB announcement days; 2007-2017 only, two venues, and covariation rather than tradable edge.
- `2609.07958v1` - Historical Reflections on Interest Rates and the Emergence of the Yield Curve (2026). Explains the institutional preconditions for a curve and that interpolation comes last; a narrative essay with no estimates.
- `2509.03964v2` - Cryptocurrencies and Interest Rates: Inferring Yield Curves in a Bondless Market (2025). Recovers zero-coupon curves from inverse options and futures via call-put parity; single venue and heavy trimming.
- `2310.00321v2` - Modeling the yield curve of Burundian bond market by parametric models (2023). Compares Nelson-Siegel and Svensson on auction data; small sample with imputed maturities and one market.
- `2301.10117v1` - Bitcoin Does Not Hedge Inflation (2023). Event-study evidence that Bitcoin loses value on inflation surprises through a consumption-savings channel; six assets and a five-year window.
- `1303.6192v1` - Inflation Uncertainty, Output Growth Uncertainty and Macroeconomic Performance (2013). EGARCH comparison of currency-board and inflation-targeting regimes; six economies, 2000-2011, no settled ranking.
- `2009.11557v1` - A note on the impact of news on US household inflation expectations (2020). Michigan Survey evidence on asymmetric and regime-shifted news effects; US households only.
- `2012.11649v3` - On the Aggregation of Probability Assessments (2020). Regularised mixtures of ECB-SPF inflation density forecasts with the mandatory simplex constraint; survey data only and ex post optimal penalties.
- `1111.3757v1` - Interest Rates and Information Geometry (2011). Reframes the discount function as a density and gives curve distances and maximum-entropy interpolation; no empirical calibration and moments may not exist.
