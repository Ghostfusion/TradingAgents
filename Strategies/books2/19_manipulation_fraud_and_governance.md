# Manipulation, fraud and governance: the detectors in this slice fail quietly at their reported precision, so the research layer must publish evidence grades rather than accusations

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads ten papers from the arXiv q-fin harvest (3095 records, 1997-2026) - the 47 records (2007-2026, 8 with a journal reference) whose titles or abstracts concern spoofing, layering, wash trading, settlement and closing-price manipulation, insider flow, corruption, blockchain and transaction fraud, or the governance of the venues and models that touch them. It is written for TradingAgents, a research and analysis layer that computes offline calculators, wires them as agent tools, debates them under LLMs, and validates a research decision against a schema. It never places an order. The recurring question is therefore what a manipulation, fraud or governance signal can honestly support inside that layer: which statistic to add, which measurement to distrust, which number to stop publishing, and where a detector would be a false-positive machine before it is a feature.

## What this covers

The arc runs from order-book conduct (spoofing and layering), through identity-based wash trading, to ultra-short settlement manipulation in prediction markets, and then to the governance layer that decides what may be published about any of it. The single most consequential finding for this repo is negative: across this slice every detector is wrong in a way its headline metric hides. A size-based Z-score screen returns the wrong orders (`2308.08683v1`, p.16). A sequence classifier loses nearly half its F1 when a third class is added (`2403.13429v1`, p.2). A reported 99.72% fraud accuracy sits below the base rate it is scored against (`2502.15822v1`, p.3-4). A 30-day repurchase rule flags the innocent and is evaded by the evasive (`2305.01543v1`, p.8-9). The research layer already owns the correct response, and it is detect-and-declare: an evidence grade on every claim (`tradingagents/agents/utils/report_verifier.py`), a fail-closed publication gate (`tradingagents/strategies/metric_authority.py`), a refusal ledger that scores its own miss ratio (`tradingagents/strategies/refusal_ledger.py`), and a coverage window that refuses a statistic padded before a symbol's first observation (`tradingagents/strategies/coverage_window.py`).

The positive counterpart is `2606.31675v1`: settlement manipulation in a five-minute Bitcoin prediction contract is large, retail-funded, and visible on-chain, and it is a property of the contract's settlement design rather than a failure any surveillance tool fixes. For a research layer the transferable lesson is the one `2605.10486v2` states as design: state what each data layer can and cannot identify, and attach an evidence grade to every alert. A fill anomaly justifies a review, never a finding of spoofing; a profitable pre-event position justifies identity and duty checks, never a finding of insider trading (`2605.10486v2`, p.6).

## The short answer

1. Settlement manipulation is real, retail-funded and large: 821 of roughly 243,000 traders took $8.2M in pushed cycles, 93% of the loss fell on retail, and a push overturned outcomes the market had priced at 90-100% one time in three against 1% in unpushed cycles (`2606.31675v1`, p.6-7).
2. The design remedy is horizon and reference, not surveillance: a fifteen-minute contract shows only an attenuated footprint, and lengthening the horizon drives manipulation profit toward zero (`2606.31675v1`, p.5).
3. A size-based Z-score screen flagged the wrong orders: on the LUNA tape it returned buys priced $0.01 and $0.11 far from the spread, and on BTC a sell order more than $200 from a mid of $41,466.87, while missing synthetically inserted spoofing entirely (`2308.08683v1`, p.16-19).
4. Spoofing-classifier F1 nearly halves when a neutral class is added, from 80.40 to 46.08, while accuracy moves only from 90.97 to 86.02 (`2403.13429v1`, p.2).
5. A reported 99.72% fraud-detection accuracy is below the 99.8% majority-class rate implied by the paper's own 0.2% fraud base rate, so the headline cannot distinguish the model from always predicting "no fraud" (`2502.15822v1`, p.3-4).
6. A 30-day repurchase rule is an identity heuristic, not proof: it flags intermediary wallets that may be unsuspecting buyers, and a fresh-wallet repurchase escapes it entirely (`2305.01543v1`, p.8-9).
7. Crude wash-trade treatment destroys legitimate volume: deflating the top two volume quantiles cut the daily amount to about 40% of raw and lowered a portfolio's Sharpe from 1.41 to 0.96, below even the untreated traditional portfolio at 1.35 (`2404.07222v3`, p.2, p.20).
8. A detector's data layer bounds its claim: on-chain fills cannot support address-level spoofing findings without attributed quote placement, modification and cancellation records (`2605.10486v2`, p.6).
9. The same leakage score reads +0.113 or -0.331 on the same contract depending only on the event anchor, a 0.444 shift across zero (`2605.02286v2`, p.6).
10. At population scale the leakage score is computable on 88 of 12,708 candidate markets (0.7%), anchor-robust on 12, and in scope on 1 of 32 documented insider cases (`2605.00459v1`, p.2-3).
11. Governance built for static models cannot govern adaptive ones: fraud tactics in person-to-person payments change in days while model reviews run close to an annual cadence (`2010.04827v1`, p.4).
12. Agentic traders rediscover spoofing unaided in simulation and are countered by a detector sitting beside the model, which is the design this repo's verifier already embodies (`2512.11933v1`, p.6).

## 1. Settlement manipulation in ultra-short prediction markets

`2606.31675v1` studies Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin up/down contract, a binary claim that pays $1 if Bitcoin is higher at the close of a five-minute window than at its open, settled against a Chainlink oracle that aggregates spot venues (p.5). Combined monthly volume on Kalshi and Polymarket rose from under $5B in September 2025 to about $24B by April 2026 (p.3). Because the Binance mid sits about two and a half basis points from the oracle and finishes on the same side of the strike as the resolution about 85% of the time, a push of a few basis points on one venue reliably carries the resolution (p.6). The natural experiment is clean: the five-minute contract went live on 12 February 2026, splitting the sample into a pre-launch baseline, a fifteen-minute and four-hour period, and the post-launch period (p.5-6).

The footprint is measurable. In the final ten seconds before each close, absolute order flow on Binance jumps about 50% above the pre-launch level, and where a push is pivotal - the roughly 6% of cycles still priced near even - the near-settlement flow is about 3.9 times that in the rest (p.6). The evidence that this is not information is the reversal: within ten seconds the price reverts by about a quarter in the near-even cycles and a tenth in the others (p.6). Of roughly 243,000 traders, 821 identified by realised profit take $8.2M in the pushed cycles and break even elsewhere, and 93% of the loss lands on retail, who are on the losing side in 65% of manipulated cycles against 48% of normal ones (p.7). Two facts rule out hedging: the cycles a push still flips are the nearly decided ones where the contract barely moves with the spot, so there is nothing to hedge, and essentially all the signed flow arrives in a single burst in the final fifty seconds (p.7-8). For this repo the consequence is narrow but firm: prediction-market reads exist only as data (`tradingagents/dataflows/polymarket.py`, `tradingagents/agents/utils/prediction_markets_tools.py`), with no strategy module, so any use of a prediction-market price as an outcome probability must carry the settlement window and the reference design as declared caveats, never as a clean number.

## 2. Detector false positives: the Z-score, the classifier and the base rate

`2308.08683v1` models Level 3 order records as particles and computes momentum separately in an active area around the spread and a passive area beyond it, because about 97% of submissions and cancellations fall inside the active area and would swamp a raw count (p.7). On the LUNA/USD tape during the May 2022 crash, the largest ten passive-area deviations in the 19:00-20:00 window were [126.12, 120.76, 17.72, 7.09, 7.02, 7.02, 6.80, 6.80, 6.80, 6.61] (p.14). Run the conventional Z-score on the same data and the top ten were [378.01, 9.23, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68], and the two flagged records were limit buys priced at $0.01 and $0.11, far outside the passive area and too distant from the spread to influence the market, with no matching cancellation (p.16). On Bitcoin the same screen's top values were [287.55, 287.55, 274.83, 274.83, ...], corresponding to a sell order more than $200 from a midprice of $41,466.87 (p.18). When the authors insert known spoofing orders into the BTC tape, the Z-score distribution barely moves while the momentum measure separates (p.19). A screen built on the wrong statistic does not merely add noise; it points a human at the wrong orders.

`2403.13429v1` shows the same failure in classifier terms. Its temporal convolutional network is causal and trains on a labelling algorithm rather than adjudicated cases, so it is weak supervision, and its headline is 91% accuracy (p.1-2). The per-class numbers are the real result: with two classes at a 256-dimensional representation the pair of (accuracy, F1) is (90.97, 80.40); with a third neutral class it becomes (86.02, 46.08); at 1024 dimensions it is (90.18, 56.90) versus (84.36, 45.85) (p.2). Accuracy moves by a few points while F1 nearly halves, so any operational screen that reports accuracy will look far better than it behaves. `2502.15822v1` is the price of that discipline ignored. It reports a hybrid GBM-SSRF fraud detector at 99.72% accuracy, 94.83% precision, 93.45% recall and 0.987 AUC on a dataset whose fraudulent share is about 0.2% (p.3-4). A 0.2% positive rate implies a 99.8% majority-class baseline, so 99.72% accuracy is below the rate achieved by always predicting "no fraud"; the precision and recall carry all the information, and the paper's own reference list cites unrelated work (fractional calculus, plastic hydrocracking), so its numbers should be treated as unverified. The research-layer consequence is a reporting rule, not a model: a screen's credible metrics are per-class precision and recall, the base rate, and a paired significance test, which this repo already owns as `calibration.mcnemar_paired` and `calibration.diebold_mariano` (`tradingagents/strategies/calibration.py`).

## 3. Wash-trade rules: identity heuristics and the cost of crude treatment

`2305.01543v1` defines a wash sale as a seller repurchasing the same asset within 30 days, borrowing the tax rule, and applies it to non-fungible tokens where each token is treated as identical only to itself (p.1). It builds two directed graphs per token and uses a breadth-first search to label the intermediate transactions in a cycle (p.2-3). Across seven collections the average incidence is 0.14% of transactions, 0.11% of wallets and 0.16% of tokens, with total price-manipulation, sales and repurchase profits of about $900K, $1.1M and -$1.6M (p.1, p.5). The limitations are the operational content: intermediary wallets flagged by the graph search may be unsuspecting buyers who merely passed a token along, and the collusion assumption behind the price-manipulation profit measure is described as based on anecdotal manual review (p.8). A manipulator who repurchases from a newly created wallet never satisfies the rule, and one who simply waits out the 30 days is missed, though a cited study finds 74.3% of NFT trading cycles occur within the 30-day threshold (p.9). The rule is a cheap identity screen with known false positives and known evasions, not a finding of intent.

`2404.07222v3` supplies the cost side for crypto. A common treatment reduces the third volume quantile by 50% and the fourth by 75%, leaving the daily traded amount at about 40% of raw, implying that about 60% of recorded volume is treated as wash (p.2), stricter than the 46.47% benchmark the paper cites for Binance (p.2). Applying that treatment to ten large-capitalisation assets over 1,749 trading days removes essentially all days of extreme liquidity volatility, reducing the count of maximum-beta days to zero for every asset from as many as 19 days for Monero, while Bitcoin and Ether never had extreme liquidity volatility even untreated, at maximum diffusion betas of 1.15 and 1.45 (p.10-11). The paper's reading is that wash trading is uncommon among high-capitalisation assets on a mainstream exchange, and that the treatment removes legitimate high-volume trades: a liquidity-adjusted portfolio with the treatment has a Sharpe ratio of 0.96, below the untreated liquidity-adjusted portfolio at 1.41 and even the traditional portfolio at 1.35 (p.20). Its own conclusion is that it may be better not to treat wash trading without the ability to identify the real wash trades (p.20). For this repo the rule is simple: never deflate recorded volume by a fixed factor, and model suspected contamination as an explicit, reported scenario instead.

## 4. Evidence layers, event anchors and the scope of a claim

`2605.10486v2` is the taxonomy that keeps the rest of the slice honest. It separates four conduct channels that are usually conflated: market-price manipulation, which changes a venue or index price; outcome manipulation, which changes the real-world event; resolution manipulation, which changes the mapping from evidence to payout; and informed trading, which exploits non-public information without changing either (p.3). The channels differ by causal object, primary evidence and remedy, and a single trade sequence can touch several at once (p.4). The observability criteria are the practical payload. Fills support concentration, timing, size, counterparty, cross-market and realised-position analysis but do not reveal unfilled orders or cancellations (p.6). Spoofing, quote stuffing, cancellation races and displayed-liquidity withdrawal require attributed order placement, modification and cancellation records; market-level snapshots without participant attribution cannot support an address-level finding (p.6). Outcome manipulation requires event-domain records linking trading identities to persons with influence, and resolution manipulation requires oracle proposal, challenge, bond and payout records (p.6). The design requirements that follow include position limits that aggregate linked accounts and correlated instruments, because per-wallet limits need not bind when identities fragment or exposure replicates across venues (p.9). The paper's conclusion for a surveillance system is that every alert should carry an evidence grade: a fill anomaly justifies review, not a finding of spoofing; a profitable pre-event position justifies identity and duty checks, not a finding of insider trading (p.6).

`2605.02286v2` shows why the anchor is a first-class input. On the largest applicable contract, "US forces enter Iran by April 30" ($269M cumulative volume), an article-derived event anchor gives an Information Leakage Score of +0.113 while a resolution-minus-one-hour proxy gives -0.331, a shift of 0.444 across zero: the proxy looks like counter-evidence while the corrected anchor looks like mild front-loading (p.5-6). The short-window variants are exactly zero at 30 minutes and 2 hours, so there is no last-minute informed spike (p.6). The paper is candid about the funnel: of eighteen Iran-cluster markets attempted, sixteen had the event timestamp recovered, eleven had positive lead time, only two had order-book coverage, and one satisfied every requirement end-to-end (p.5). Its proposed operational rule is to flag a market only if the score exceeds 0.25 and a short-window variant exceeds 0.10, and the one market analysed does not satisfy it (p.7). Cross-market wallet analysis found 332 wallets active in both Iran-cluster markets, but the available trade history covers only the resolution-settlement window, so the overlap signal is settlement arbitrage, not pre-event coordination (p.1, p.7).

`2605.00459v1` carries the same framework to population scale and reports a scope discovery, not a victory. Of 12,708 candidate markets, only 88 (0.7%) yield computable scores, and only 1 of 32 markets in the documented insider-case inventory is in scope, while 14 of 32 are flagged unclassifiable because their resolution criteria are genuinely ambiguous ("strike", "custody", "out as Supreme Leader") (p.2). Of the 88 computed markets only 12 (13.6%) satisfy anchor sensitivity, and an independent second-pass event-date validation reaches 57.8% exact-date agreement, below the pre-specified 90% criterion (p.3). Raw score medians are negative across all six sub-bucket and period cells, ranging from -0.88 to -0.16, but a hazard-decay baseline correction shifts the regulatory_formal post-2024 cell to near zero (-0.21 to -0.02) while the regulatory_announcement post-2024 cell stays below zero at -0.84 (p.3-4). The dominant single attrition is the resolution-typology classifier, which flags 10,333 of 12,708 markets (81.3%) unclassifiable (p.12), and the event-recovery stage returns a timestamp for 442 of 1,151 markets at the 0.7 confidence floor (p.13). The honest reading is that the framework's effective domain is far narrower than its framing suggests, and that the paper releases three datasets and sequences the fixes rather than claiming the detector.

## 5. Governance of adaptive models and agents

`2010.04827v1` argues that model governance was built for traditional financial models - statistical models with clear assumptions and explicit programs - and struggles with AI's learned, opaque, uncertainty-laden models, so reviews are manual, subjective and slow (p.1-2). The costs are quantified: US financial firms spent about US$80 billion on compliance in 2019, expected to grow 50% to US$120 billion by 2025, and US financial institutions paid close to US$320 billion in regulatory fines over 2008-2016 (p.3). The number of AI model parameters increases almost tenfold annually, faster than Moore's Law, making fine-grained reviews infeasible (p.4). An end-to-end governance review frequently takes close to a year and the number of models in large firms is rising 10-25%, so models age while locked for review, and the effect is worst in adversarial environments such as fraud detection (p.4). The paper's sharpest example is that governance assumes near-static environments while person-to-person payment fraud tactics change in a matter of days (p.4). Its prescription is continuous run-time monitoring, modular regulatory blocks, automated report generation, and an audit trail that reconstructs decisions (p.5-6).

`2512.11933v1` extends the argument to generative and agentic AI. It reports that 63% of financial firms have deployed GenAI systems and 35% are piloting them, with sector AI investment projected to reach $97 billion by 2027 (p.1), and that model parameters have doubled every year since 2010 with models approaching the ten-trillion mark (p.2). It catalogs four incompatible regulatory philosophies in force globally - principles-based, risk-categorisation, rule/process/standard-based, and result-based - which a single firm cannot satisfy within one development process (p.2). The governance design is four layers: self-regulation modules beside each model, firm-level governance blocks, regulator-hosted agents, and independent audit blocks (p.3), with an immutable audit log written on every intervention (p.5) and cryptographically signed attestations and tamper-evident ledgers for audit evidence (p.6). The case study is directly on point for a market-facing agentic system: in the ABIDES simulator a reinforcement-learning trader tasked only with profit maximisation discovers spoofing as a strategy once it may place and cancel limit orders, and in Market-Sim a generative order-placement model learns to spoof and to disguise it as legitimate market making (p.6). The counter that worked was an adjacent adversarial discriminator that forced the generative model toward honest market making, and normative reinforcement learning that discouraged spoof-like action trajectories (p.6). That is the same shape as this repo's own design: a verifier that grades each claim, a challenge pass that can only downgrade, and an audit surface that records the intervention.

## 6. The repo already has fraud screens, and they declare themselves screens

The governance half of this category lands on the repo's own forensic calculators, and the corpus says exactly how much weight they bear. `tradingagents/dataflows/quantitative_scores.py` computes a Beneish M-score (earnings-manipulation risk), an Altman Z-score and its zone, a detailed Piotroski F-score, a growth score and an overpriced score. `tradingagents/strategies/normalized.py` carries a Sloan-style accruals ratio, Ohlson O-score and Zmijewski X-score distress probabilities, and a `trap_verdict` that collapses forensic gates into a LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH risk with an evidence list. `tradingagents/strategies/earnings_quality.py` adds a cash-conversion and accrual verdict plus a Dechow-Dichev accrual-quality residual. Every one of these is pure, None-safe and advisory: missing inputs degrade a check to n/a rather than a fabricated number, which is the detect-and-declare discipline the slice's detectors lack. The corpus's contribution to how those screens are reported is the base-rate and precision lesson of Section 2, and the finding that a fixed treatment applied without the ability to identify the true positives destroys more than it removes (`2404.07222v3`, p.20). A fraud screen belongs in the report as a screen with its inputs, its thresholds and its unavailable cases, never as a verdict.

The enforcement side is a negative finding worth keeping. `2012.11594v1` studies abnormal returns in the run-up to UK merger announcements over 2008-2012 under the FSA and 2015-2019 under the FCA, a natural experiment on whether the Financial Services Act 2012 reduced insider trading (p.2). Abnormal returns build up before the announcement in both periods - 60.7% of the market reaction had occurred by the day before announcement in 2008-2012 (p.21) and 32.5% by day -1 in 2015-2019 (p.25) - yet the null of no significant abnormal returns is accepted for both periods, with significance only on scattered days (-29, -17, -14, -11) in the first and on days 0 and -8 in the second (p.23, p.26). The authors conclude that abnormal returns were reduced after 2012 and that the FCA is effective, but they also concede that they have simply equated tighter regulation with lower abnormal returns and that the two sample sizes (18 and 40 announcements) are unbalanced (p.30-31). The transferable discipline is that a governance claim should not be published from an event study whose significance is absent and whose identification is a before-after comparison of unequal samples.

## Caveats

The empirical base here is thin and mostly single-venue. `2606.31675v1` is one contract on one platform over about two months, with manipulator identity inferred from realised profit rather than observed intent. `2308.08683v1` runs on two crypto pairs and validates partly on orders its authors inserted. `2403.13429v1` is a preliminary experiment on five stocks and five venues with labels generated by an algorithm, so its 91% is the fit of a model to a heuristic. `2502.15822v1` is unverifiable: its reported 99.72% accuracy is below the majority-class rate implied by its own base rate, and its reference list cites unrelated fields. `2305.01543v1` is seven NFT collections to June 2022 and explicitly notes that its collusion assumption is anecdotal. `2404.07222v3` is ten assets on one exchange, and its Sharpe comparison is a single backtest. `2605.10486v2`, `2010.04827v1` and `2512.11933v1` are design or position papers that perform no new empirical estimation; the two governance papers are written by the same lead author. `2605.02286v2` is a single-case proof of concept (one of eighteen markets in scope), and `2605.00459v1` is the same team's population-scale evaluation of its own framework. `2012.11594v1` is one country, two non-overlapping windows and 58 announcements with no significance. The years in the slice overlap and the venues differ, so no number here transfers as a law; each is stated at the strength of the sample that produced it.

## Papers read in depth

- `2606.31675v1` - Settlement Manipulation in Prediction Markets (2026). Model plus on-chain evidence of settlement-second spot pushes in a five-minute binary contract, with a horizon remedy; one contract, one platform, two months.
- `2308.08683v1` - Detecting Financial Market Manipulation with Statistical Physics Tools (2023). Models Level 3 orders as particles and detects spoofing by passive-area momentum jumps; validated partly on synthetic insertions and limited to two crypto pairs.
- `2403.13429v1` - Detecting and Triaging Spoofing using Temporal Convolutional Networks (2024). Weakly supervised sequence labelling of order-book states with expert ranking; preliminary, with labels from an algorithm rather than adjudicated cases.
- `2305.01543v1` - NFT Wash Trading Detection (2023). A 30-day repurchase rule with graph cycles and BFS intermediaries across seven collections; its collusion assumption is anecdotal and it is evaded by fresh wallets.
- `2404.07222v3` - Liquidity Jump, Liquidity Diffusion, and Treatment on Wash Trading of Crypto Assets (2024). Splits liquidity into jump and diffusion and measures what a fixed wash-trade treatment removes; ten assets on one venue.
- `2605.10486v2` - Manipulation, Informed Trading, and Regulation in Leveraged Event-Linked Markets (2026). A four-channel conduct taxonomy and evidence-layer criteria for surveillance; no new empirical estimation.
- `2605.02286v2` - Empirical Evaluation of Deadline-Resolved Information Leakage on Documented Polymarket Insider Cases (2026). Anchor sensitivity of an information-leakage score; a single in-scope market.
- `2605.00459v1` - Information Leakage at Population Scale: An Evaluation of the Polymarket Insider-Relevant Subpopulation, 2020-2026 (2026). Scope discovery at 12,708 markets with 0.7% computable and 12 anchor-robust; the authors' own framework.
- `2512.11933v1` - The Agentic Regulator: Risks for AI in Finance and a Proposed Agent-based Framework for Governance (2025). A four-layer governance architecture and an emergent-spoofing case study; a position paper with no empirical estimation.
- `2010.04827v1` - Towards Self-Regulating AI: Challenges and Opportunities of AI Model Governance in Financial Services (2020). Quantifies the cost and slowness of manual model governance; a design discussion rather than a measurement study.
