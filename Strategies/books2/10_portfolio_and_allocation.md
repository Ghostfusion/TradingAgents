# Portfolio construction and allocation: the allocation inputs this repo already shrinks are still its weakest link, because every construction rule is a design choice whose ranking moves with the test assets, the estimator and the rebalancing rule

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers the general-finance evidence on how portfolio weights are actually built and how fragile the inputs are. The slice is 112 records matching the portfolio-construction and allocation topics in the local arXiv harvest, spanning 2000-2026, 15 of them carrying a journal reference; from 40 candidates screened, 12 were read at the PDF with page-anchored notes. It is written for the operator of TradingAgents, a research and analysis layer whose allocation path already ships covariance shrinkage (`tradingagents/strategies/covariance_models.py`), a pairwise correlation penalty (`tradingagents/strategies/portfolio.py`), and cross-sectional neutralisation with quantile helpers (`tradingagents/strategies/cross_section.py`); the job of this brief is to name what that path does not yet have, and to route every finding to the smallest research-layer consequence that already exists.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from "the inputs are wrong" to "the measurement is wrong" to "the test itself is a design choice". It starts with estimator error: a covariance matrix that is merely badly conditioned pushes the mean-variance weight vector away from the expected-return direction, and the size of that rotation is bounded by the condition number alone `2107.06194v5` (p.4); Markowitz variance itself is only the zero-volume-fluctuation approximation to a market-based variance that can be far larger or far smaller `2507.21824v1` (p.10). It then moves to views and factors: Black-Litterman cannot express subjective confidence at all `2406.01199v1` (p.9), a skew-normal likelihood cuts portfolio volatility at a fixed target return `2310.12333v1` (p.24), and a factor model's ranking is not invariant to how the test assets are built or how their weights are managed after formation `2606.19550v1` (p.23). It closes on the cross-section and on breadth: published predictors largely survive replication with only a 10-15% shrinkage correction `2209.13623v3` (p.1), while the classical "how many names" answer is far too optimistic once returns are regime-switching and fat-tailed `2204.13398v1` (p.16). Three of the twelve papers were not in the same corpus's execution-layer extraction and are specific to this repo's shape: a continuous compliance score that re-orders a screened universe without earning a return premium `2512.22858v2`, a regime-conditioned allocator that maps state variables straight to weights `2211.00871v1`, and an agentic strategic-asset-allocation pipeline whose own authors state it cannot be cleanly backtested `2604.02279v2`.

The single most consequential finding for this repo is that the allocation path is already ahead on the two things the literature warns about most loudly, and still silent on a third. Shrinkage exists (`covariance_models.ledoit_wolf_shrink`, `ewma_covariance`), a correlation penalty exists (`portfolio.correlation_penalty`), neutralisation and quantile helpers exist (`cross_section.neutralize_book`, `quantile_split`, `industry_neutral_z`), and a covariance spectrum with a null band exists (`covariance_models.spectral_null_band`, `panel_spectrum`). What is missing is any treatment of the second dimension of variance: every tail number in `book_risk.py` is a return-based statistic, so a book whose market-based variance is inflated by trade-volume dispersion is reported as if volumes were constant `2507.21824v1` (p.11); and every factor comparison this repo could run is run on one construction, when `2606.19550v1` shows a single rebalancing-rule switch moves the leader from FF3 to FF5 and FF6 (p.23, p.20). Two smaller gaps follow from the same source: `portfolio_optimizer.black_litterman_weights` has no explicit confidence parameter, only the un-tunable tau that `2406.01199v1` shows cannot be a confidence dial (p.9), and no research-layer surface tests mean-variance spanning at the asset level, which `2403.17127v1` argues is where the conclusion is decided (p.11).

## The short answer

1. Factor-model rankings are construction-dependent: under daily constant weighting FF3 has the smallest mean absolute pricing error in six of eight design cells, while under buy-and-hold FF5 and FF6 lead most cells and q5's errors reach 490.3 bp in the UNIF-EW cell `2606.19550v1` (p.23, p.20).
2. The mean-variance weight vector's alignment with expected returns is bounded only by covariance conditioning: cos(phi) >= 2 sqrt(kappa)/(kappa + 1), so an ill-conditioned Sigma rotates the bets away from the forecast no matter how good the forecast is `2107.06194v5` (p.4).
3. Markowitz variance is exactly the case of zero trade-volume variation; with positive volume dispersion the true market-based variance can be far below or above it, and no conditioning of a return covariance can see that gap `2507.21824v1` (p.10, p.11).
4. Diversification read off one unconditional Gaussian covariance is far too optimistic: a two-regime Markov-modulated NIG fit to Nasdaq 100 daily data needs 94 (87) components to explain 95% (90)% of the variance of a 100-asset universe `2204.13398v1` (p.16).
5. Black-Litterman cannot express subjective confidence; tau lies in [0,1] and is the error bars of the drift estimate, whereas a Wasserstein-barycenter update supplies a tunable t that rewards confident correct views and punishes confident wrong ones `2406.01199v1` (p.9, p.21).
6. Skew-aware views lower risk at a fixed target: at a 1.25% monthly target the skew-normal BL optimum has 3.552% volatility against 4.950% for the Gaussian version, with symmetry rejected on 217 monthly returns of 13 US equities (2 ln LR = 33.212, p = 0.0015) `2310.12333v1` (p.24, p.22).
7. Published cross-sectional predictors mostly survive: 183 of 207 have t > 2.0 and 26 have t > 6.0, and the empirical-Bayes shrinkage correction accounts for only 10-15% of in-sample mean returns, with a false discovery rate under 10% `2209.13623v3` (p.8, p.1).
8. Tail-targeted allocation beats volatility targeting: a tau = 0.1 quantile policy earns an annualised Sharpe ratio of 0.94 with a 21.28% maximum drawdown and a 5% CVaR of -1.13%, against 0.87, 21.39% and -1.36% for the unmanaged benchmark `2510.19271v2` (p.18).
9. A continuous compliance score re-orders a screened universe without paying a return premium: tightening the minimum score from 0.50 to 0.90 cuts holdings from 671 to 461 and effective names from 61.1 to 42.7, yet the score earns no significant cross-sectional coefficient `2512.22858v2` (p.18, p.2).
10. Correlated LLM agents need a governance layer, not just a better model: an agentic SAA pipeline runs 21 construction methods, has them peer-review and Borda-vote, and states that the pipeline cannot be cleanly backtested because the LLM already encodes the past `2604.02279v2` (p.3, p.24).
11. Regime-conditioned weights are learnable directly from state variables: an ANN mapping four state variables straight to weights lifts the monthly Sharpe ratio to 0.09 and the CVaR ratio to 0.34, against 0.06, 0.07 and 0.08 (and 0.17, 0.20, 0.22) for two moment-estimation benchmarks and a linear policy rule `2211.00871v1` (p.15).
12. Spanning must be tested at the asset level; with 306 test assets and 102 benchmark assets over 250 periods the classical F-tests cannot even be applied, and the rejection comes from variance reduction inside the domestic global-minimum-variance portfolio rather than from a Sharpe-ratio gain `2403.17127v1` (p.11, p.1).

## 1. Estimator error, conditioning and the alpha-weight angle

The cleanest statement of why unconstrained mean-variance is fragile is geometric. Write the frontier portfolio as theta* proportional to Sigma^{-1} alpha, where alpha is the expected-return vector and Sigma the covariance. The angle phi between theta* and alpha measures how far the optimizer has rotated the bets away from the return forecast; the larger this angle, the more the portfolio is a property of the estimator rather than of the forecast. `2107.06194v5` extends the Golts-Jones result by deriving the bound through the Kantorovich inequality, (v'v)^2 / ((v'Tv)(v'T^{-1}v)) >= 4 rho_1 rho_n / (rho_1 + rho_n)^2, which gives cos(phi) >= 2 sqrt(kappa)/(kappa + 1) with kappa the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of Sigma (p.4). The practical reading is precise: it is not covariance inversion per se that is the error maximiser, it is the inversion of a badly conditioned covariance, because that is what pushes the direction of the optimal portfolio away from the direction of expected returns. The paper then extends the bound to gearing-constrained optimisations and finds it weakened, not removed, by the constraint: the gearing-constrained solution can be written as theta* = (g0 - w) theta_0 + w theta_alpha, with theta_0 the global minimum-variance portfolio and theta_alpha the optimal risky portfolio, and the overall angle decomposes into an angle from theta_0 and an angle from theta_alpha (p.8, p.9). The consequence the authors draw is that one should minimise the angle between the optimal risky portfolio and alpha, because improving conditioning can move the minimum-variance leg away from alpha even as it pulls the risky leg towards it. The limitation is that this is theory plus a Gaussian simulation: there is no market data, no costs and no out-of-sample portfolio, so the claim is about mechanism and direction, not about how much Sharpe ratio conditioning is worth.

## 2. What Markowitz variance leaves out: trade volumes and the variance budget

Markowitz variance Theta_M = sum over j,k of theta_jk X_j X_k is the textbook quadratic form in fixed portfolio weights. `2507.21824v1` re-derives it as a special case: it is the portfolio variance when the volumes of the consecutive trades with every security are assumed constant, that is when the coefficient of variation c of trade volume is zero (p.2, p.9). The paper defines a market-based variance whose second-order Taylor expansion in c is Theta_M - 2 a sqrt(Theta_M) R c + [R^2 - Theta_M] c^2, where R is the mean gross return and a is a scaled covariance between trade values and volumes (p.10). Three limiting cases matter. When return fluctuations are large so that the zero-volume coefficient of variation y0 is near 1, Markowitz variance sits near its maximum R^2 and a positive value-volume covariance lets the volume term cut the true variance dramatically, so Markowitz overvalues risk (p.11). When return fluctuations are small so that y0 is much less than c, the volume term can raise the true variance towards R^2 from a much smaller Markowitz value, so Markowitz undervalues risk (p.11). When the value-volume covariance is zero the variance grows monotonically from y0^2 R^2 to R^2 as c goes from 0 to 1 (p.11). The limits are severe: it is a pure analytical exercise with no calibration, no data and no portfolio, so the correct use is as a checklist of a missing hazard rather than as a correction formula. Its value for a research layer is that it names a variance component a return-covariance estimator structurally cannot contain, which is the kind of gap worth recording beside a tail budget rather than inside it.

## 3. Views and priors: what a Bayesian or geometric update can and cannot express

Black-Litterman mixes a prior drift pi = gamma Sigma w* with views V ~ N(Pm, Omega_v) and returns a Gaussian posterior drift. `2406.01199v1` turns this into a single-asset gedankenexperiment: the posterior drift is (sigma_V^2 mu + tau sigma_R^2 nu_V) / (sigma_V^2 + tau sigma_R^2), and because tau is confined to [0,1] for the prior to remain a reasonable estimate of the drift, the posterior can never equal the view distribution even at full confidence, while pushing tau to infinity would imply the noise in expected returns exceeds the noise in returns (p.8, p.9). The paper concludes that tau parameterises the error bars of the estimate, not the investor's subjective confidence, and that the Meucci variant has no tunable parameter at all, so it cannot interpolate either. Its alternative is a generalised Wasserstein barycenter that minimises a Lagrangian combining a distance from the prior and a distance from the views, with weight lambda = t/(1-t) and t in [0,1] naming the subjective confidence directly (p.9, p.19). On simulated 50-asset paths of fifteen years of daily data the geometric methods beat the benchmark and both Black-Litterman variants when views are correct and confidence is high, are statistically indistinguishable from them when views are ambiguous, and underperform when views are incorrect and confidence is high, which is the desired penalty (p.17, p.21). On real data, 50 randomly drawn S&P 500 constituents over a walk-forward quarterly rebalance, the geometric model with views taken from the minimum-volatility portfolio beats the benchmark and the linear model (p.24). The limitations are that the sharpest claims rest on synthetic data, the real-data test uses one market and equal-weight priors, and no costs are charged.

The third-moment extension moves in a different direction. `2310.12333v1` replaces the Gaussian likelihood with a hidden-truncation skew-normal and shows the posterior drift is again skew-normal, so the standard mean-variance objective no longer applies and Simaan's two-component variance, spherical plus non-spherical, is needed (p.9, p.10). The optimal portfolio is then found by minimising the spherical variance at a given mean and a given non-spherical variance. On 217 monthly log-returns of 13 US equities from December 2004 to November 2022, a likelihood-ratio test rejects the symmetric skew parameter with log-likelihood 3652.19 normal against 3668.80 skew-normal, 2 ln LR = 33.212, p = 0.0015 (p.14, p.22). At a 1.25% monthly target with zero non-spherical variance the skew-normal optimum has 3.552% volatility against 4.950% for the classical BL, and the portfolios become more negatively skewed as the target return rises, which the authors read as investors trading negative skewness for higher expected return (p.24). The limitations are that the views are hand-specified, the universe is 13 large US names, and there are no trading costs, so this demonstrates that the third moment changes the frontier rather than that it is worth the estimation cost.

## 4. Factor models and the construction of the test

`2606.19550v1` asks whether a factor model's measured performance is a property of the model or of the test assets, and finds the latter matters a great deal. It builds characteristic-unsorted random portfolios from a daily CRSP universe, 1967 to 2024, that keeps 77.6% of common stocks but 99.7% of aggregate market equity (p.9). It varies stock selection (market-equity-proportional versus uniform, nine selection ratios), initial weights (equal, value, square-root market-equity, random), breadth and the weight-management rule, holding the selected stocks and initial weights fixed and changing only the rebalancing rule for the cleanest comparison. The spanning exercise separates the two objectives cleanly: at daily frequency adding q5's factors to FF6 raises the annualised maximum Sharpe ratio from 1.311 to 2.205, while adding FF6's factors to q5 raises it from 2.077 to 2.205, so q5 has the broader opportunity set, with the expected-growth factor alone contributing an annualised alpha of 864.4 bp against FF6 (p.14, p.15). That is a different question from pricing errors. On Fama-French characteristic sorts FF6 has the smallest mean absolute error at 121.2 bp against q5's 187.6 bp, but on industry portfolios the CAPM has the smallest mean absolute error while q5 has the lowest share of significant alphas, which the author calls home-field bias (p.16, p.17). Under buy-and-hold, FF5 and FF6 lead most of the eight selection-weighting cells and q5 is never best, with its worst cell at 110.3 bp; under daily constant weighting FF3 has the smallest mean absolute error in six of eight cells and q5's errors exceed 200 bp in several cells and reach 490.3 bp in UNIF-EW (p.20, p.23). The factor-ablation grid shows the marginal contribution of q5's profitability and expected-growth factors changes sign with the test-asset family and the weight rule, with q5 leaving larger errors than q4 in 62 of 64 cells across the full selection-ratio grid (p.28, p.23). The limitation is that this is US CRSP equity data, with overlapping random portfolios that make the share-of-significant-alphas numbers descriptive rather than independent tests, and no transaction costs are charged for the daily rebalancing that drives the worst cells.

`2403.17127v1` connects to this from the testing side. Its high-dimensional spanning test avoids estimating a precision matrix by testing each asset's moment condition with a batch-mean Student-t statistic and combining p-values with the Cauchy combination test, which stays valid under arbitrary dependence; a randomly-weighted batch-mean step fixes the size distortion that appears when the number of benchmark assets grows (p.5, p.13). The empirical panel is 306 test assets and 102 benchmark assets over 250 periods, a configuration in which the classical GRS or Huberman-Kandel tests cannot be applied because N exceeds T - K (p.11). The conclusion is that the rejection of mean-variance spanning comes from the potential to reduce variance inside the domestic global-minimum-variance portfolio rather than from a Sharpe-ratio gain, and that running spanning tests on ad-hoc portfolios instead of assets introduces aggregation bias and can change the answer (p.1, p.3). The limitation is that it is one equity panel using blue-chip indices from three regions, so the finding is about the validity of the test and the source of the rejection, not about a tradable improvement.

## 5. Diversification, breadth and the cross-section

Two papers attack the diversification orthodoxy. `2204.13398v1` revisits the "how many stocks" question with a Markov-modulated Normal-Inverse-Gaussian model calibrated to Nasdaq 100 daily data from 4 January 2000 to 12 November 2021, 5483 observations (p.7). The two regimes differ sharply: the NIG scale parameter is about 2.5 times larger and the volatility parameter more than seven times higher in the second regime, with persistence probabilities of 71.6% for regime 1 and 28.3% for regime 2 (p.13, p.14). Regime classification is good, with a Regime Classification Measure of 8.63, below the threshold of 10 used as the bar for an adequate two-regime fit (p.15). The punchline contradicts the classical rule: 94 principal components are needed to explain at least 95% and 87 for 90% of the variability of a 100-asset universe, far more than the 8 to 25 names often quoted, because a single unconditional covariance understates how much of the risk is common once regimes and fat tails are allowed (p.16). The limits are that it is one index, the component count comes from an illustrative random-portfolio construction, and the paper offers no out-of-sample portfolio of its own.

`2512.22858v2` shows the same pattern in a screening layer, where the boundary itself is arbitrary. It builds a Continuous Shariah Compliance Index on [0,1] that embeds the published thresholds of six standards into one piecewise score with a comfort region and an outer bound per dimension, aggregated multiplicatively (p.11, p.12). On a survivorship-free CRSP-Compustat panel of US equities from 1999 to 2024 with a six-month accounting lag, mean CSCI is 0.36 and 19% of firm-months sit at zero, while among permissible-sector firms the mean is 0.44 and 24% of observations are at 0.99 or above, so the continuous score carries variation the binary labels suppress (p.15, p.2). Tightening the minimum threshold from 0.50 to 0.90 cuts the number of holdings from 671 to 461 and the effective number of stocks from 61.1 to 42.7, while average compliance rises from 0.918 to 0.995 and annualised Sharpe ratios stay in a narrow band from 0.885 to 0.947 (p.18). The September 2023 DJIM and S&P methodology change provides an external check: firms that became newly compliant after the cash and receivables screens were dropped have an average CSCI of 0.18, against 0.81 for firms compliant under both regimes, so the continuous score measures compliance quality the simplified binary rule no longer distinguishes (p.2, p.16). The negative finding is the important one: CSCI earns no reliable cross-sectional return premium once standard characteristics are controlled for, so it is a portfolio-design axis and an eligibility measure rather than an alpha signal (p.2). The limitation is US equities under one family of religious screens with 25 bp round-trip costs assumed.

`2209.13623v3` is the counterweight that keeps this from becoming fatalism about the cross-section. Reviewing three independent meta-studies it reports four stylised facts: almost all findings replicate, predictability persists out of sample, empirical t-statistics are far above 2.0, and predictors are weakly correlated (p.3). On the Chen-Zimmermann dataset of 207 predictors from 140 papers, 183 have t > 2.0, 121 exceed 3.0, 74 exceed 4.0 and 26 exceed 6.0 (p.8). McLean and Pontiff's 97 replicated predictors lose only 26% of their in-sample return immediately after the sample ends, and the mean, median and modal pairwise correlation is near zero with 60 principal components needed to span 90% of variance (p.6, p.9). The empirical-Bayes correction is small: published t-statistics are overstated by about 10%, the simple model gives an upper bound on the false discovery rate of 4.8%, and the three independent meta-studies agree that shrinkage is 10-15% of in-sample mean returns with a false discovery rate under 10% (p.14, p.15, p.1). The paper also warns about the misreading: with a 1% false-discovery-rate hurdle 81% of the findings that fall below it are true predictors with an average expected return 1.6 standard errors from zero, so raising the hurdle is conservatism rather than evidence of publication bias (p.20). The limitation is that the whole review is the US cross-sectional anomaly literature and the out-of-sample windows are about four years.

## 6. Measurement, tail-targeting and the agentic layer

Drawdown-aware allocation gets a concrete out-of-sample test in `2510.19271v2`. The framework replaces expected utility with recursive quantile preferences indexed by tau, solved by a quantile actor-critic on eleven daily Fama-French and industry factors, with a five-year estimation window and a two-year out-of-sample block, transaction costs charged on turnover, and state variables standardised using only past data (p.4, p.16). The estimated policies form an ordered frontier. The tau = 0.1 policy has the lowest annualised volatility at 7.59%, the least negative 5% CVaR at -1.13%, the shallowest maximum drawdown at 21.28% and the highest Sharpe ratio at 0.94, while tau = 0.9 earns the highest mean at 9.46% with a 33.34% drawdown and a Sharpe ratio of 0.79; the unmanaged benchmark sits at 7.96% mean, 21.39% drawdown and 0.87 (p.18). Relative to a market-volatility-managed benchmark all three quantile policies earn positive annualised alpha, 2.77, 2.47 and 2.16, with betas below one that increase monotonically in tau, so the low-tau policy behaves as a defensive variant of volatility management rather than an unrelated strategy (p.19). The mechanism the authors document is selective cross-sectional de-risking: the estimated slopes linking active factor weights to trailing expected shortfall are negative and monotone in tau, so the downside-focused policy cuts exposure hardest to the factors whose left tails deteriorate most (p.5). The gain is concentrated in states where volatility is high and the cross-sectional dispersion of downside tails is large, where the good-tail-minus-bad-tail payoff is 1.04 percentage points higher than in low-dispersion states (p.2). The limitation is long-only factor portfolios in one market, with daily data used because the object of interest is a conditional quantile.

`2211.00871v1` is the regime-conditioning paper, and its design is the opposite of a two-step estimator. Instead of modelling the conditional return distribution and then choosing a portfolio, it trains an artificial neural network that maps four state variables, the default spread, the dividend yield, the term spread and an S&P 500 trend, directly to the weights that maximise a chosen performance ratio, using a sigmoid output so non-negativity and the budget constraint are automatic and no distributional assumption is imposed (p.8, p.9). The universe is the S&P 500 index and a thirty-year US government bond index, 8609 daily observations from January 1986 to December 2018, with 396 monthly state observations and rolling 156-month training windows followed by 60-month test windows (p.12, p.13). Across the 1999-2018 out-of-sample period the monthly Sharpe ratio is 0.09 and the CVaR ratio 0.34, against 0.06 and 0.17 for the VAR benchmark, 0.07 and 0.20 for the factor benchmark, and 0.08 and 0.22 for the linear policy rule; the improvement is largest for the CVaR and MAD ratios, roughly 50% (p.15). The interpretation study finds that trend is the most and term spread the least influential state variable on the Sharpe ratio, but that the direction of the trend and dividend-yield effect flips between turbulent and post-crisis sub-periods, which the authors read as a warning against relying on any single state variable (p.5). The limitations are a two-asset universe, no costs, and a black-box mapping whose explanation rests on sensitivity methods rather than on a structural model.

`2604.02279v2` is included because this repo is itself an agentic pipeline, and the paper is the honest account of what that architecture can and cannot claim. Its pipeline runs 21 portfolio-construction agents across five categories, from heuristics and return-optimised methods to risk-structured, non-traditional and agentic methods, then subjects them to a structured review in which a chief-risk-officer agent scores each candidate and each construction agent referees two peers, one from its own category and one from another, with 42 reviews released simultaneously (p.10, p.11). Voting is a modified Borda count with 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 points for a top-five ranking and -2 for a bottom flag, blended with a quantitative composite at a regime-dependent weight, and a diversity constraint requires the shortlist to span at least three of the five families (p.13). In the March 2026 illustrative run the vote ranks maximum diversification first, Black-Litterman second and risk parity third, while the CIO agent, scoring six dimensions, weights the ensemble towards centrality and assigns the largest single weight, 11.1%, to market-cap weighting, which ranked nineteenth, and only 3.1% to the top-ranked method; the final portfolio has an expected return of 6.87%, a volatility of 7.54% and an effective number of assets of 11.2 (p.19, p.20, p.23). The paper is explicit that this is one realisation of a stochastic system at one point in time, not evidence of outperformance, and that the more favourable drawdown of -25.6% against -34.3% for a 60/40 benchmark is largely mechanical because the portfolio holds less equity and carries an 8.1% cash sleeve (p.3, p.23). The limitation is the one that matters for any LLM-driven research layer: LLMs are trained on internet-scale corpora that encode historical market data, so any historical out-of-sample evaluation is contaminated by look-ahead bias, and even at temperature zero with a pinned model version and seed, identical prompts can yield different outputs across batch sizes and hardware (p.24). The paper's mitigations, retrieval-augmented evidence restricted to point-in-time information, data homogenisation and entity neutering, are described as reducing obvious memory cues without removing information encoded in the parameters (p.24, p.25).

## Caveats

Most of this slice is narrower than it sounds. Nine of the twelve papers are US or single-market equity studies: `2606.19550v1`, `2209.13623v3`, `2512.22858v2` and `2310.12333v1` use US data, `2204.13398v1` is the Nasdaq 100 alone, `2403.17127v1` is one blue-chip panel across three regions, `2211.00871v1` is the S&P 500 plus one bond index, `2510.19271v2` is long-only US factor portfolios, and `2406.01199v1` real-data test is one set of S&P 500 constituents. Three are model-only with no market data at all: `2107.06194v5` proves an angle bound and illustrates it with a Gaussian simulation, `2507.21824v1` is a Taylor expansion with no calibration, and `2604.02279v2` is explicitly one realisation of a stochastic system that its authors state cannot be cleanly backtested. Transaction costs are modelled in only three places (`2512.22858v2` at 25 bp round-trip, `2510.19271v2` on turnover, and `2604.02279v2` not at all in its illustrative run), and `2606.19550v1` charges none for the daily rebalancing that produces its worst cells, so its construction-dependence magnitudes should be read as gross. Several empirical samples overlap in time and assets, so their findings are not independent draws. Finally, the repo already ships the two mitigations the literature most insists on, shrinkage and neutralisation, so the honest reading of this slice is that its marginal value here is the named gaps (volume-based variance, a tunable view confidence, a third-moment objective, an asset-level spanning check, and a leakage protocol for the agentic layer) rather than the basics.

## Papers read in depth

- `2606.19550v1` - Which Portfolios? The Construction Dependence of Factor Model Performance (2026). Factor-model rankings move with stock selection, initial weights and the rebalancing rule; US CRSP only, overlapping portfolios, no costs.
- `2107.06194v5` - Geometric insights into robust portfolio construction (2021). Bounds the alpha-weight angle by the condition number and extends it to gearing constraints; theory and simulation only.
- `2507.21824v1` - Markowitz Variance May Vastly Undervalue or Overestimate Portfolio Variance and Risks (2025). Recasts Markowitz variance as the zero-volume-fluctuation case; purely analytical with no calibration.
- `2403.17127v1` - High-Dimensional Mean-Variance Spanning Tests (2024). Asset-level spanning test robust to serial dependence; the rejection comes from domestic global-minimum-variance variance reduction, on one equity panel.
- `2406.01199v1` - A Geometric Approach To Asset Allocation With Investor Views (2024). A Wasserstein-barycenter update interpolates prior and views and rewards confident correct views; the sharpest results are synthetic.
- `2310.12333v1` - Black-Litterman Asset Allocation under Hidden Truncation Distribution (2023). Skew-normal views cut volatility at a fixed target return; 13 US names, hand-specified views, no costs.
- `2209.13623v3` - Publication Bias in Asset Pricing Research (2022). Shrinkage of 10-15% and a false discovery rate under 10%; one literature (US equity anomalies), four-year out-of-sample windows.
- `2510.19271v2` - Managing Portfolios Across the Return Distribution (2025). Quantile-targeted policies form an ordered drawdown-versus-upside frontier with alpha over volatility management; long-only US factor portfolios.
- `2204.13398v1` - Portfolio Diversification Revisited (2022). A regime-switching Levy calibration needs 94 components for 95% of variance; one index, illustrative construction, no portfolio of its own.
- `2604.02279v2` - The Self Driving Portfolio: Agentic Architecture for Institutional Asset Management (2026). A 21-method agentic SAA pipeline with peer review and Borda voting; a single illustrative run, and its authors state the pipeline cannot be cleanly backtested.
- `2512.22858v2` - From Binary Screens to Continuous Compliance: A Shariah Screening Measure for Portfolio Design (2026). A continuous compliance score that re-orders a screened universe with no return premium; US equities, one screen family, 25 bp costs.
- `2211.00871v1` - State-dependent Asset Allocation Using Neural Networks (2021). An ANN maps state variables directly to weights and lifts Sharpe and CVaR ratios over three benchmarks; two assets, no costs, black-box mapping.
