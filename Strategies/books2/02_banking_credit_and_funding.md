# Banking, credit and funding liquidity: funding is the residual of settlement, credit risk is not stationary, and the leverage rule is part of the price mechanism

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from the arXiv q-fin harvest on banking, credit and funding liquidity, and states what their evidence changes about how a research and analysis layer should measure credit. The slice is 223 records spanning 2000-2026, 59 of them carrying a journal reference; forty were screened and twelve were read at the PDF. It is written for TradingAgents, which clears nothing and executes nothing: it computes deterministic credit and fundamental reads in `tradingagents/strategies/*.py`, exposes them as LangChain tools under `tradingagents/agents/utils/*_tools.py`, drives them from the fundamentals analyst's prompt, and scores the result in the fundamental score engine. Every take-away below lands, or explicitly does not land, on that layer. The question is not how an engine should fund a position; it is which credit number this layer should stop publishing, which measurement it should distrust, and which comparison it should fix.

## What this covers

The slice runs from payment-flow accounting to balance-sheet fragility, and it is unusually consistent about three things. First, funding is a residual of settlement and not an independent price series: `1202.3182v1` shows overnight lending almost exactly offsets the payment imbalance, and `1702.08774v1` shows the potential supply of bank credit is bounded only by how the reserve base is defined. Second, credit risk is not stationary and is not decided where people expect: `2211.09176v3` shows default hazards converge across risk bands as a loan seasons, and `2506.03344v1` shows who runs is decided by deposit composition and was not hedged. Third, the leverage rule is itself part of the price mechanism: `1403.1637v1` and `1507.04136v1` show a capital floor can force selling into a decline and that the best policy is not a constant. The single most consequential finding for this repo is a negative one: `2409.18443v1` finds the August 2023 US sovereign rating downgrade produced no significant abnormal equity return, so the credit layer must not manufacture a rating-to-equity event path that the evidence does not support. The brief's practical value is that the layer already computes three credit reads (`tradingagents/strategies/credit_spread.py`) and a family of distress and quality screens (`tradingagents/strategies/normalized.py`, `tradingagents/strategies/earnings_quality.py`, `tradingagents/strategies/ratios.py`), and this slice says exactly which of their assumptions the evidence breaks.

## The short answer

1. Overnight lending is the residual of payment imbalance, not an independent market: loan and nonloan imbalances correlate -0.93, -0.88, -0.95 and -0.36 across four days (`1202.3182v1` p.8).
2. Loans are a tiny part of the flow system but carry real money: under 1% of transactions by number and up to 9% by value, with 897 overnight loans in four days at a mean implied rate within 0.01% of the 6.25% target (`1202.3182v1` p.5).
3. The payment network is stable and the loan network is not: about 80% of nonloan flows persist day to day and carry more than 96% of value, while only about 50% of loan flows persist and they carry about 65% (`1202.3182v1` p.9, p.10).
4. Banks did not hedge deposit run risk before 2023: uninsured deposits fell 1.9 percentage points on average, from 27% to 25%, and the fall was largest where initial uninsured and brokered funding was highest (`2506.03344v1` p.5, p.8).
5. Only about 1,200 of 4,551 banks report interest-rate derivative positions at all, and option use has no significant relation to the change in uninsured deposits (`2506.03344v1` p.5, p.7).
6. A sovereign rating downgrade did not move equities: the Fitch US downgrade of 1 August 2023 left cumulative abnormal returns of -0.058, -0.055 and +0.097 with p-values 0.299, 0.167 and 0.301 across three mega-caps (`2409.18443v1` p.13, p.14).
7. Consumer credit risk is not stationary over the life of a loan: default hazards converge across risk bands after about one year and fully by 42-48 months, even though the loans stay deeply underwater (`2211.09176v3` p.5).
8. Default is rare at the top of the risk scale and common at the bottom: 52% of deep-subprime loans defaulted against 4% of super-prime, over 58,118 loans (`2211.09176v3` p.12, p.14).
9. Leverage rules are part of the price mechanism: a 16% capital floor forces selling into a decline and can turn an absorbable shock into a banking catastrophe (`1403.1637v1` p.15, p.17).
10. The best leverage policy is not a constant: a procyclical Basel-style rule is optimal when noise is high and the bank is small, and constant leverage is closer to optimal in the opposite limit (`1507.04136v1` p.7).
11. Whether credit is bounded depends on the reserve-base definition: once interbank claims are admitted to the base, the potential supply of bank credit becomes unbound (`1702.08774v1` p.13).
12. Aggregating loan maturities destroys real structure: the three sub-30-day layers hold 97% of issued loans, yet the statistically optimal granularity always sits between collapsing and separating the layers (`1906.08617v1` p.5, p.4).

## 1. Funding is the residual of settlement, not a price series

`1202.3182v1` reconstructs every interbank transaction settled on a real-time gross settlement basis in Australia's RITS system over five consecutive weekdays from 19 February 2007, with 55 participating banks and 2,970 possible directed flows (p.3, p.9). Overnight loans are extracted by matching reversing transaction pairs above A$200,000 whose implied rate is within 0.5% of the known 6.25% target rate; the authors estimate contamination below 2%, at most five misidentifications per day (p.4). They identify 897 overnight loans over four days (p.5); loans are less than 1% of transactions by number and up to 9% by value (p.5), and the mean implied rate is within one basis point of the target with a standard deviation of about 0.07% (p.5). The decisive result is the imbalance relation: the Pearson correlation between loan and nonloan imbalances is -0.93, -0.88, -0.95 and -0.36 from Monday to Thursday (p.8), and loan-imbalance size correlates with nonloan turnover at 0.74, 0.75, 0.66 and 0.77 (p.8). The reading is causal in the paper's own words: nonloan flows create the imbalance, then loan flows compensate it, and the loan flows are largely unrelated to the specific counterparties that caused the imbalance (p.13). For a research layer this is a warning against publishing a standalone funding-rate or funding-volume factor. There is no funding module in this repo and no settlement model behind it, so any "funding" series it printed would be an exogenous guess at exactly the quantity the paper shows is an accounting residual.

The size of the funding flow is small relative to the flow it clears. Overnight loans are 9.12%, 4.45%, 6.84% and 7.04% of daily transaction value across the four days, and two-day loans number 27, 67 and 24 with total values of A$1.3, A$2.2 and A$1.4 billion, or 1.5%, 1.0% and 0.9% of the day's value (p.5, p.6). Net nonloan flows average about 470 per day, of which roughly 110 consist of a single transaction and 50 of two transactions (p.11). The counterparties are concentrated: the big four banks held 65% of total resident assets in February 2007, each above A$225 billion against A$93 billion for the next largest retail bank, and the big four plus one investment bank held 56% of cash and liquid assets (p.6, p.7). The evidence is one quiescent week in one country and relies on knowing the target rate; it is not a general law, but it is the cleanest available demonstration that funding is endogenous.

## 2. Deposit composition decides who runs

`2506.03344v1` uses FDIC Call Reports for 4,551 to 4,553 banks and asks whether banks positioned their balance sheets ahead of the 2022-2023 hiking cycle. Uninsured deposits, computed as (DEP-DEPINS)/DEP, fell on average by 1.9 percentage points from Q4 2021 to Q3 2023, with the average bank going from 27% uninsured to 25% (p.5, p.6). The cross-sectional regression coefficient on the initial uninsured share is -0.218 with a standard error of 0.012 in the base specification and -0.312 in the specification with derivatives, so the banks that ran hardest were the banks that started most exposed; the brokered-deposit coefficient is -0.108 and -0.241 (p.8). Interest-rate option positions bought and sold have coefficients of 0.009 and 0.005 with standard errors of 0.049 and 0.046, so the study cannot reject zero, and the authors' headline conclusion is that they find no evidence banks proactively managed run risk (p.7, p.8). The dispersion is the story: the change in uninsured deposits has a standard deviation of 0.076, a minimum of -0.544 and a maximum of 0.680, so some banks lost more than half of their uninsured funding while others gained (p.6).

The sample constraint is itself the finding. Deposit data exist for about 4,550 banks but interest-rate derivative data for only about 1,200, and adding the derivative variables cuts the regression sample to 1,123 observations while lifting R-squared from 0.196 to 0.313 (p.5, p.8). The paper has only notional derivative positions, not net positions, so it cannot tell whether a bank is net long or short, and it cannot see the option type (p.7). That is a measurement caution the layer should carry directly: a notional derivative line in a balance sheet is not evidence of a hedge. The natural place to enforce it is the fundamentals analyst's tool surface, where `get_balance_sheet` (`tradingagents/agents/utils/fundamental_data_tools.py`) feeds the deposit and borrowing lines, and where the prompt's basis-discipline and period-tagging rules already require the basis of every input to be stated. For a bank name, the uninsured and brokered deposit shares are the run-risk inputs the evidence supports; derivative notional is not.

## 3. Credit risk is not stationary, and a sovereign rating is not an equity signal

Two of the twelve read papers attack the assumption that a credit statistic is a constant. `2211.09176v3` takes loan-level performance data from SEC EDGAR for four 2017-vintage consumer auto ABS trusts, starting from 55,000, 67,797, 80,636 and 72,515 loans (p.9) and filtering to 72- and 73-month used-car loans with no co-borrower, stated-not-verified income, no subvention and less than 18 months of age at first report, plus an integrity check that removes 2,630 loans or 4.3% of the filtered population (p.10). Risk bands are defined by original APR, with super-prime at 0-5%, prime at 5-10%, near-prime at 10-15%, subprime at 15-20% and deep subprime at 20% or more (p.11). The filtered 58,118 loans are 37% deep subprime and 37% subprime, with 4% super-prime, and 37% defaulted overall, ranging from 52% of deep subprime to 4% of super-prime; 23% are censored (p.12, p.14). Estimating a discrete-time competing-risks cause-specific hazard, the authors find that conditional default risk converges across bands beginning after about one year, and that loans as different as subprime and super-prime behave alike after 42-48 months of payments (p.5). The consumer-finance corollary is that borrowers who improve are slow to refinance, leaving $11-61 per month, or $193-1,616 in total, for the riskiest borrowers and $13-56 per month, or $160-2,327, for near-prime and prime borrowers (p.6). The loans are dispersed across states and manufacturers, with the top five state concentrations at Texas 13%, Florida 12%, California 9%, Georgia 7% and North Carolina 4%, and the top five makes at Nissan 13%, Chevrolet 10%, Ford 7%, Toyota 7% and Hyundai 7% (p.12).

This is the sharpest available correction to how this repo reads a distress flag. The layer's implied-probability-of-default helpers in `tradingagents/strategies/credit_spread.py` - `hazard_from_spread`, `merton_distance_to_default` and `default_probability` - all rest on a constant hazard: `default_probability` literally returns 1 - exp(-lambda t). The auto-loan result is that the hazard is not constant across loan age, so a lifetime PD quoted from those helpers is an age-independent number the data contradict. The distress screens are exposed to the same error from the other side: `trap_verdict`, `ohlson_o_score` and `zmijewski_score` in `tradingagents/strategies/normalized.py` collapse a point-in-time filing into a LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH verdict with no seasoning term, so a seasoned loan or a name whose accounting risk has decayed is flagged as though the flag were permanent. Neither change is an execution-layer change; both are documentation and basis changes to the research layer's own numbers.

The second non-stationarity finding is negative. `2409.18443v1` runs a market-model event study around the 1 August 2023 Fitch downgrade of the United States from AAA to AA+, on the three most-capitalised NASDAQ names (Apple, Microsoft, Amazon) plus the S&P 500 as the market factor, with a 250-day estimation window from 2 November 2022 to 10 July 2023 and an 11-day event window [-5, +5] and a 10-day buffer (p.4, p.6). The estimated betas are 1.3233, 1.3402 and 1.6323 with R-squared 0.70, 0.56 and 0.52 (p.9, p.10). The cumulative abnormal returns are -0.058 for Apple, -0.055 for Microsoft and +0.097 for Amazon, with t-values of -1.0952, -1.5024 and 1.0980 against critical values of about 2.23, and p-values of 0.2991, 0.1672 and 0.3007 (p.13, p.14). The null of no significant effect is not rejected. The paper is candid about its own limits: an 11-day window, three names, one level of significance, and no control for co-incident news (p.15, p.16). The honest reading is not "downgrades never matter" but "this single event did not move these equities, and the study is too small to claim more". For this repo the consequence is precise: `credit_stress_level` in `tradingagents/strategies/credit_spread.py` is an option-adjusted-spread read of the credit cycle, and it should stay that. A sovereign rating action should not be wired into the credit score or the regime score as an equity event.

## 4. Leverage rules are part of the price mechanism

`1403.1637v1` models two consolidated sectors, banks that are risk neutral subject to a probability-of-insolvency constraint and risk-averse outside investors, trading a fixed supply of risky assets under common beliefs. The benchmark example sets the insolvency probability to 1%, the investors' currency share to 10%, the CRRA coefficient to 15 and the clearing price to $0.87, with banks holding 439 bonds, $168 of reserves, $62 of equity and $486 of deposits, and investors holding 611 bonds, $1,072 of equity, $54 of currency and $486 of deposits (p.8). The asymmetry is the result: a positive belief shock has a unique clearing price, while a negative shock can have several because bank demand rises as the price falls, and a sufficiently strong negative shock leaves only the insolvency equilibrium, through a fold or cusp catastrophe (p.13, p.14). Two results matter for measurement. First, successive positive and negative shocks of equal magnitude do not cancel: a large positive shock that builds leverage, followed by a return to the initial belief, can push the system across the multiple-equilibrium region into certain insolvency (p.15). Second, a capital requirement of 16% binds the banks' bond purchases, which prevents the crisis in that example but also forces a larger price decline in a falling market (p.17). The paper's own conclusion is that this supports graduated or countercyclical constraints rather than a fixed floor (p.15, p.17).

`1507.04136v1` generalises the policy question with an agent-based model of a leveraged bank, an unleveraged fundamentalist fund and a passive outside lender, where the leverage target is a power function of perceived risk, the target equalling the sum of current and baseline variance raised to a cyclicality parameter b in the interval [-0.5, 0.5]; negative b is procyclical and positive b countercyclical (p.11, p.12). Its central claim is that the optimum is not a constant: a procyclical Basel II-style policy is optimal when exogenous noise is high, volatility is strongly clustered, the bank is small and leverage is low, while in the opposite limit constant leverage is closer to optimal (p.7). A crude calibration puts the model in the locally unstable region, where a slowly building price bubble followed by a crash has a period of roughly 10-15 years in the deterministic case and about 10 in the stochastic case (p.18). The authors flag the calibration as approximate and the empirical figure as anecdotal (p.4, p.17). Neither paper has an execution-layer counterpart in this repo, and neither touches the research layer's calculators. What they change is how the layer should label a leverage read: any single-name or single-sector leverage number is point-in-time and static, and the evidence says a static leverage reading is not a stress reading. The closest surfaces are the risk score engine (`risk_score`, `category_score` in `tradingagents/strategies/risk_score.py`) and the debt rows inside `compute_ratios` in `tradingagents/strategies/ratios.py`, both of which report leverage as if it were a level rather than a response function.

## 5. The reserve base and the maturity layer are what limit lending

`1702.08774v1` formalises the boundary case that decides whether bank credit is bounded at all. In its model each bank's balance sheet separates currency reserves, retail loans, interbank lending, equity and central-bank assistance (p.6). The narrow reserve base is currency only, which reproduces the textbook fractional-reserve bound; when interbank claims are admitted to the base, a broader monetary system lets banks settle interbank payments through mutual credit, and the potential supply of bank credit becomes unbound (p.13). The pooling coordination parameter runs from 0, complete and perfect pooling, to 1, no interbank credit at all (p.17); the abstract states plainly that coordination mismatch on interbank credit may trigger systemic crises (p.1). The model is illustrative and uncalibrated, with no defaults and no empirical estimation, so its numbers are parameter choices rather than findings. Its lesson for this layer is a modelling one: any claim that credit supply has a ceiling is a claim about a reserve-base definition. There is no balance-sheet or reserve-base model in this repo, so `no research-layer landing` is the honest cell for the supply-ceiling finding, and the caveats record it.

`1906.08617v1` shows what happens when the maturity dimension is thrown away. It uses the complete panel of Russian domestic unsecured interbank loan contracts, 2.4 million loans aggregated into 57 monthly networks from January 2000 to October 2004, with each loan tagged into one of eight Central Bank of Russia maturity classes (p.2). The three short layers below 30 days contain 97% of all issued loans (p.5), which is exactly the argument for collapsing the network, yet the layered stochastic block model rejects that argument: complete aggregation is never the optimal granularity, and the optimum always lies between full separation and full aggregation (p.4, p.11). The number of statistically distinct lending patterns rises with market development, with the boundary between the early phase and the emerging-maturity phase at about month 35 (p.11). The economic reading is that important banks in the short bins both lend and borrow equal amounts, indicative of intermediation, while in the long bins they act as sources or sinks, indicative of financing (p.13). The repo has no instrument or tenor model, so this is a second `no research-layer landing`: the correct consequence is a prohibition, not a feature. Do not collapse distinct credit tenors into one synthetic series, and do not treat income from a short-maturity position and a long-maturity position as the same quantity.

## 6. Aggregation destroys the structure that matters

`1407.5429v1` and `0901.2377v3` attack the same problem from the graph side, and `1309.2130v4` from the distribution side. `0901.2377v3` builds a weighted bipartite graph of banks and quoted firms for each year from 1980 to 2005, with about 229 commercial banks and 2,330 firms (p.3, p.4), and defines a fragility score as the right eigenvector of the matrix product of the two normalised weight matrices (p.8). The spectrum satisfies 0 < lambda <= 1, with lambda = 1 the trivial constant mode, and the sum of the eigenvalues equal to the trace of the product (p.9). Comparing against rewired random bipartite graphs, only the largest few non-trivial eigenvalues are distinguishable from noise; in the 2005 data only the second and third are significant (p.10, p.11). The time series of the normalised eigenvalues shows a peak in the late 1980s, an abrupt drop in 1990, and further peaks around 1992 and 1997 (p.11, p.12), and the eigenvector spikes correspond to banks that failed or were merged, concentrated in the middle and north regions and, from 2003 to 2005, in Okinawa (p.13). The top of the size distribution is where the risk sits: in 2005, 1.25% of domestic firms are the large firms and they account for nearly half of all loans (p.2, p.3), and the rank correlation between a bank's lending volume and its number of relationships is 0.825 (p.6). This layer's concentration surfaces - `ownership_hhi` and `revenue_concentration` in `tradingagents/strategies/liquidity_risk.py` - compute on per-name shares, not on the relation graph, so they would miss the fragility that lives in the eigenvector of the credit relationship matrix.

`1309.2130v4` is the strongest warning about the tails of a dataset. Financial firms are about 30% of the Forbes Global 2000 by count and about 30% of sales, profits and market value over 2003-2012, yet they hold 70% of total assets in the 2004 list, rising to 87% in 2013 (p.1). The largest firm in 2013 is Fannie Mae at $3.2 trillion and the twelfth is Royal Bank of Scotland at $2.13 trillion, comparable to UK GDP of $2.4 trillion (p.1). Zipf's law holds only from the twentieth-largest company downward, and if it held at the top Fannie Mae would be ten times RBS, at $21.3 trillion (p.2). The authors define a shadow-banking index as the summed gap between the fitted power-law size and the actual size over the 1,000 largest firms (p.5), and they note that the missing mass need not be hidden assets: special-purpose vehicles redistribute assets from the top tail into the bulk of the distribution, and that is itself a form of off-balance-sheet shifting (p.7). The paper is explicit that its index depends on the fitted exponent in the middle of the distribution and on the assumption that proportional random growth would otherwise hold at the top (p.5, p.6). The two research-layer consequences are both measurement hygiene: a panel statistic should travel with the coverage count of the names actually present (`aggregate_quality` and `panel_statistic` in `tradingagents/strategies/data_quality.py`), and the fundamental score engine should cap a sub-score when coverage is thin, which is exactly what `_subscore` and `_cap_floor` in `tradingagents/strategies/fundamental_score.py` already do. Off-balance-sheet redistribution is the one finding that lands on the accounting-audit side: the reported-versus-cash divergence checks in `earnings_quality_verdict` and `dechow_dichev_aq` in `tradingagents/strategies/earnings_quality.py`, and the Sloan-style `accruals_ratio` in `tradingagents/strategies/normalized.py`, are the closest audit this layer has of reported assets against cash reality.

## 7. Behavioural credit: the debt trap and the refinance that never comes

`2306.01749v2` uses Open Banking transaction data for 1,815 UK payday-loan customers observed over 2017-2018, comprising 126,188 raw transactions across about 70 financial institutions, to model weekly borrowing intensity with a two-state hidden Markov model (p.1, p.6). The regimes separate almost entirely on the baseline intensity: intercepts of about -3.62 and -0.99, implying roughly 0.03 and 0.37 payday loans per week, an approximately fourteen-fold difference (p.15, p.16). Both regimes are highly persistent: the stay probability is about 0.992 per week in the low-intensity state and about 0.969 in the high-intensity state, implying expected durations of about 125 weeks and about 32 weeks respectively (p.19). Defining a trigger as 12 consecutive weeks in the high-intensity state, 36.4% of borrowers experience at least one such event, and post-threshold high-intensity weeks average 17.8% of borrower-week observations (p.20). The paper's point is not the size of the effect but the shape of it: a debt trap requires only that a high-intensity regime persists for months, not that it is absorbing, and a single-regime average compresses two distinct behavioural relationships into one compromise pattern (p.17, p.19). The layer's nearest analogue is the regime score engine (`regime_score` in `tradingagents/strategies/regime_score.py`), which already prefers a label over a mean; the lesson is to keep that preference and not to average across states that behave differently. The refinance finding of `2211.09176v3` (p.6) is the matching behavioural fact on the consumer-credit side: an improving borrower who does not refinance leaves a measurable amount on the table, so a point-in-time distress flag can be stale in the improving direction as well as the decaying direction.

## Caveats

Four of the twelve papers contain no data at all: `1403.1637v1`, `1507.04136v1` and `1702.08774v1` are models whose numbers are illustrative parameters, and `1712.09978v1` is a legal argument whose quantities are doctrinaire. The empirical papers are narrow by construction: `1202.3182v1` is one quiescent week in February 2007 in one country and relies on knowing the target rate; `1906.08617v1` is one country over four years and uses issuance rather than exposure networks; `0901.2377v3` is the Japanese bank-firm dataset over 1980-2005 and excludes interbank credit; `1407.5429v1` (not read here but cited in the slice) shares that dataset; `2506.03344v1` is a single hiking cycle with notional-only derivative data; `2211.09176v3` is one asset class in two vintages; `2306.01749v2` is UK consumer credit, not bank credit; `2409.18443v1` is a three-name, 11-day event study explicitly underpowered and uncontrolled for co-incident news. The slice also disagrees with itself and with the layer's built-in assumptions: the leverage models say a fixed capital rule can amplify a decline, while the published framework treats a leverage threshold as a safety feature; the auto-loan paper says the default hazard is not constant, while `default_probability` assumes it is. Three findings have no research-layer landing at all - the settlement-residual funding result, the loan-maturity aggregation result and the endogenous-reserve-base result - because TradingAgents has no settlement, instrument-tenor or balance-sheet model; these are recorded as prohibitions on what the layer must not publish rather than as features to build. Finally, `1712.09978v1` is read here for its definitional point that maturity, not just the lender count, decides whether an instrument can be a deposit substitute, but it is a Philippine tax-law paper and its recommendation (a one-year maturity limit) is not a general finding.

## Papers read in depth

- `1202.3182v1` - Loan and nonloan flows in the Australian interbank network (2012). Reconstructs an RTGS week and shows overnight lending is the residual of payment imbalance; one week, one country, target rate assumed known.
- `1702.08774v1` - Interbank Credit and the Money Manufacturing Process (2017). Agent-based model showing interbank credit adds a money aggregate and can make credit supply unbound; no empirical calibration and no defaults.
- `2506.03344v1` - Hedging Deposit Run Risk Prior to the 2023 Regional Banking Crisis (2025). Cross-section of 4,551 banks showing run risk tracked funding composition and was unhedged; notional-only derivatives and one period.
- `1906.08617v1` - Loan maturity aggregation in interbank lending networks obscures mesoscale structure and economic functions (2019). Layered block model on 2.4 million Russian loans; aggregation always rejected; issuance network, one country.
- `0901.2377v3` - Structure and temporal change of the credit network between banks and large firms in Japan (2009). Eigenvalue fragility scores on a weighted bipartite graph; interbank credit excluded and surviving firms only.
- `1403.1637v1` - Inside Money, Procyclical Leverage, and Banking Catastrophes (2014). Shows asymmetric response to shocks and that a capital floor can force selling; stylised two-sector model with illustrative parameters.
- `1507.04136v1` - Taming the Basel Leverage Cycle (2015). Agent-based leverage-cycle model with a cyclicality parameter; the optimal policy depends on bank size and noise; roughly calibrated, single asset.
- `2211.09176v3` - On the Convergence of Credit Risk in Current Consumer Automobile Loans (2022). Competing-risks hazards show credit risk converges with loan age; one asset class, two vintages, left-truncated sample.
- `2409.18443v1` - Investigating the Impact of Sovereign Credit Rating Downgrade on the US Equity Market (2024). Event study finding no significant abnormal returns around the 2023 Fitch downgrade; three names, 11-day window, underpowered.
- `1309.2130v4` - The Interrupted Power Law and The Size of Shadow Banking (2013). Derives a shadow-banking index from missing top-tail mass; relies on a fitted exponent and cannot identify entities.
- `2306.01749v2` - Detecting Consumers' Financial Vulnerability using Open Banking Data: Evidence from UK Payday Loans (2023). Two-state HMM shows persistent high-intensity borrowing regimes; UK consumer credit, 1,815 borrowers, two years.
- `1712.09978v1` - Why Long-Term Debt Instruments Cannot Be Deposit Substitutes (2017). Legal argument that maturity, not just lender count, decides deposit-substitute status; no data, single jurisdiction.
