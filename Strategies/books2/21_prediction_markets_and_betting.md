# Prediction markets and betting: a market-implied probability beats the public forecaster on one venue, but its tape cannot tell you who traded, so cite it as a calibrated prior and never as a direction-dependent signal.

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief covers how probabilistic claims are priced and calibrated, what the vig and the odds mechanics of betting markets put under any edge, what the on-chain order book of a decentralized prediction market actually looks like, and what these venues teach about illiquid, event-driven instruments. It is written for a research and analysis engine: deterministic calculators, LangChain analyst tools, score engines, and an evidence/report surface. The slice is the 24 arXiv records that match prediction markets and betting, spanning 2009 to 2026, of which exactly 1 carries a journal reference; eight records were read in depth from the local PDFs and every number below carries the page marker where it was read.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from 2009 design proposals for prediction markets, through a 2012 theory result on how Kelly bettors make a market aggregate beliefs, through 2017-2019 evidence on how badly people and sportsbooks price a known edge, to a 2026 cluster of papers on Polymarket and Kalshi that finally has tick-level and on-chain data. It is a small and thin slice: one journal-anchored record, four single-venue 2026 papers, and a behavioural experiment. Its single most consequential finding for this repo is a measurement one, because it decides whether any microstructure number computed from a decentralized prediction market can be trusted: the public order-book feed of a decentralized central limit order book does not identify which side initiated a trade, so trade direction inferred from it agrees with the on-chain record only about 59% of the time, which is barely above chance and enough to flip the sign of the effective spread and of Kyle's lambda on most markets (`2604.24366v2`). Everything the repo might otherwise compute from that feed - spread, impact, flow imbalance - is downstream of a channel it cannot observe.

## The short answer

1. A prediction-market price is a probability you can trade, and on one liquid venue it beat the best public forecaster: Kalshi's market-implied high temperature had 9.8% lower RMSE than the best single public product at the end of the first trading hour and 11.4% lower at the final bulletin, pooled over 7,590 city-days in seven U.S. cities `2609.23969v1`.
2. The same market beat an out-of-sample weighted combination of the entire public stack by 3.1% at the opening hour and 4.3% in the evening, though the 3.0% final-bulletin gap is significant only at 10%; the public forecast travels about four times further toward the market than the market travels toward it `2609.23969v1`.
3. The venue tape is not clean: on Polymarket the public order-book feed recovers the trade aggressor only about 59% of the time (panel mean 0.615, 95% CI [0.58, 0.65]), and that sign error flips the effective half-spread on 67% of the comparable top-100 markets (50% in a second window) and Kyle's lambda on 60% (43%) `2604.24366v2`.
4. The quoted spread is a function of the price level, not a constant: a median about 400 bps of mid in the central [0.4, 0.6] range and 1,300-1,800 bps below 0.10, with an 1,818 bps median in the lowest decile `2604.24366v2`.
5. Depth is layered rather than top-of-book (the top level carries a median 0.136 of cumulative top-10 depth) and self-counterparty wash has a 0.97% median with a 22.2% maximum `2604.24366v2`.
6. Ultra-short asset-price contracts are manipulable at settlement: after Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin contract launched, final-ten-second Binance order flow spiked about 50%, 821 wallets took $8.2M (93% of it from retail), and a 90-100% favorite was reversed 34% of the time under a push `2606.31675v1`.
7. Lengthening the contract horizon is the design remedy: the same push signature is much attenuated at fifteen minutes than at five `2606.31675v1`.
8. Any leverage above one on a binary claim carried to the adverse payoff leaves terminal equity of -x*p0*(1 - 1/L), independent of the pre-resolution mark path, and three of five pre-specified control tests failed on a seven-day Polymarket replay `2605.10400v2`.
9. The vig is the floor: a spread bet pays 91 cents on the dollar (a 9-cent vigorish), random spread betting returned about -4.4%, and the reported positive ROI needs a bootstrap interval and a Bonferroni correction to be believed `1910.08858v2`.
10. Even a known 60/40 edge is hard to harvest: of 61 finance-trained subjects given a 60% heads coin, only 21% reached the payout cap (against 95% simulated) and 28% went bust, with an average bet of 15% of bankroll against a 20% Kelly fraction `1701.01427v1`.
11. If every trader is a Kelly bettor the market price is a wealth-weighted average of beliefs with worst-case log regret bounded by ln(1/w_i), and fractional Kelly is exactly full Kelly with a belief blended between the agent's and the market's `1201.6655v1`.
12. Informed flow must be labelled before it can be detected: the Information Leakage Score is interpretable only for event-resolved markets with an article-derived news timestamp and an anchor-sensitivity check, and the documented insider cases are systematically deadline-resolved and fall outside that scope `2605.00493v2`.

## 1. Calibration: when the price beats the forecaster

The cleanest calibration test in the slice races a prediction market against the professional forecasting stack. On Kalshi's daily high-temperature ladders for seven U.S. cities - markets first listed in August 2021 for New York and Chicago, later for Miami, Austin, Philadelphia, Denver and Los Angeles - the panel has a total of 7,590 city-days through August 12, 2026, each contract settling on the National Weather Service's daily climate report for a named station (`2609.23969v1`, p.4, p.5). The market-implied mean read off the ladder of mutually exclusive contracts had a root-mean-square error of 2.44 degrees F at the end of the market's first hour of trading against 2.70 for the National Blend of Models, a 9.8% advantage, and 1.88 against 2.12 by the NBM's final bulletin on the target morning, an 11.4% advantage (`2609.23969v1`, p.7). The market also beat an out-of-sample, day-by-day refit weighted combination of the entire public stack - NDFD, LAMP, GFS MOS, HRRR and ECMWF - by 3.1% at the opening hour and 4.3% in the evening; by the final bulletin the 3.0% gap is significant only at 10% (`2609.23969v1`, p.7). The design controls are stated: a liquidity screen (at least three quoted intervals, spread no wider than 50 cents, midpoints within +/-30% of 1) retains 86% of observations, and without it the advantage over the NBM is 8.0%, 9.0% and 8.0% at the three anchors (`2609.23969v1`, p.11).

Two robustness details matter if such a market is to be used as a signal. The headline uses the implied mean of the ladder, which is sensitive to how the open tails are modelled (geometric decay with ratio 0.55), but the implied median, which does not depend on the tails, agrees with the mean to within 0.05 degrees F pooled and moves the three gaps by less than two percentage points (`2609.23969v1`, p.3, p.11). And the public stack closes on the market by leaning on real-time observations: LAMP, the observation-conditioned product, drives most of the combination's improvement, and if LAMP is dropped the market's lead rises to 3.3%, 4.7% and 6.4% at the three anchors (`2609.23969v1`, p.7). The advantage is largest where forecasting is hardest - in Miami, where the NBM's preceding-day RMSE is 1.6 degrees F, the market does not lead, while in the other six cities (NBM RMSE 2.6-3.5) it does at every anchor - and each additional degree of NBM error adds about six percentage points to the market's advantage (R-squared 0.22) (`2609.23969v1`, p.7).

Why aggregation should work at all is the 2012 theory result, and it is the bridge between the empirical race and any engine that wants to use the price as a prior. If every participant bets the Kelly fraction f = (p - p_m)/(1 - p_m) against the market price p_m, the competitive-equilibrium price is the wealth-weighted average of the participants' beliefs (`1201.6655v1`, p.2, Theorem 1), and wealth is redistributed across rounds exactly as a Bayesian update would redistribute posterior probability over which participant is right (`1201.6655v1`, p.3). The market's worst-case log loss exceeds the best participant's by at most ln(1/w_i) for that participant's starting wealth w_i, a bound that holds against adversarially chosen outcome sequences (`1201.6655v1`, p.3, Theorem 3). In simulation, an economy of 100 Kelly agents whose individual beliefs stay fixed produces a price that tracks the observed frequency as if updating a Beta distribution, with the wealth distribution fitting Beta(10+1, 5+1) after 10 successes in 15 trials (`1201.6655v1`, p.5). The practical caveat is that this is an equilibrium of price-takers; it says nothing about a market in which participants respond strategically to the price itself, which is why the paper treats the market price as a prior to be blended, not as truth.

## 2. The vig, the odds, and the favourite-longshot geometry

Betting markets price a known edge less generously than a financial venue would, and the gap is the vig. A spread bet is engineered to have even odds and pays 91 cents on the dollar, so the bookmaker keeps a 9-cent vigorish (`1910.08858v2`, p.3). Across 16 Las Vegas sportsbooks, random spread betting on NFL, NBA, NCAAF, NCAAB and WNBA games returned a mean of about -4.4%, which is the vig showing up as expected loss, while random moneyline betting returned between -6.4% and 0.8% (`1910.08858v2`, p.5, p.15). The paper's positive result is that a non-parametric win-probability model, plus an epsilon band that refuses to bet extreme spreads and an expected-value threshold, produced positive returns in all five sports: 16.57% for the NFL and 17.01% for the WNBA under the simple average model, and 8.04% (NCAAF) to 13.02% (NCAAB) under the weighted model (`1910.08858v2`, p.16). Every one of those ROI confidence intervals excludes zero and the paper rejects the joint null across five sports with a Bonferroni correction at alpha = 0.05 (`1910.08858v2`, p.10, p.11).

That result is also a warning about hyperparameter search, and this repo has the machinery to answer it. The reported returns are the best over an epsilon grid and an expected-value-threshold grid, and the authors are explicit that they pick these on the same data that produced the returns, then lean on 10,000-iteration bootstrap percentile and high-density intervals plus a multiple-testing correction to argue the optimal ROI and optimal epsilon are jointly positive across five sports (`1910.08858v2`, p.10, p.11). Even with that discipline the numbers are gross of market impact and assume independent one-dollar bets with no compounding and no Kelly sizing (`1910.08858v2`, p.11). The sample deliberately excludes the early 2000s, 2008 and 2018 because those are the years in which the S&P 500 had a negative return, so the market-neutral comparison is a choice, not a feature of the data (`1910.08858v2`, p.16).

The same favourite-longshot geometry shows up on-chain, and its magnitude is the operative fact. On Polymarket's 600-market pre-registered panel, the median quoted half-spread in basis points of mid is about 400 in the central [0.4, 0.6] price range and climbs to 1,300-1,800 bps for markets trading below 0.10, with the lowest decile showing a median of 1,818 bps and an upper quartile of 4,000 (`2604.24366v2`, p.7). The pattern is asymmetric - the low-probability side is wider than the high-probability side - and the authors read the order-of-magnitude gap over the racetrack premium (a few percent of stake) as a liquidity-provision constraint on a bounded-upside binary contract rather than pure misperception, because they cannot separate maker inventory risk from a behavioural premium with the data they collect (`2604.24366v2`, p.7). Two supporting facts sharpen the venue picture: the top level of the book carries a median 0.136 of cumulative top-10 depth with a median Kullback-Leibler divergence from uniform of 0.087, so the folk view that prediction-market books are top-of-book does not hold, and 57% of markets have a top-level share within a factor of two of uniform (`2604.24366v2`, p.7). Maker liquidity is diverse (volume-weighted Herfindahl median 0.031, about 32 effective makers, with a p90 of 0.119) and self-counterparty wash, measured as a direct lower bound, has a 0.97% median, a 10.6% p99 and a 22.2% maximum (`2604.24366v2`, p.9, p.10).

## 3. The measurement trap: trade direction and the measures built on it

The load-bearing methodological result of the slice is that the standard source of trade direction is unavailable on a decentralized CLOB, and the repo's own microstructure calculators inherit the consequence. Six trade-based measures - effective spread, realized spread, Roll, Abdi-Ranaldo, Kyle's lambda and Amihud - all take the form E[sign_t * f(price, mid, size)], and each needs an aggressor sign per trade (`2604.24366v2`, p.4). On Polymarket the public feed's change_side field marks which side of the book moved, not which side initiated the trade (`2604.24366v2`, p.4). Matching feed-inferred buckets against on-chain OrderFilled events at 5-second and exact-price granularity across four disjoint 7-day windows gives a panel-mean sign agreement of 0.615 with a market-clustered bootstrap 95% CI of [0.58, 0.65], a volume-weighted agreement of 0.592, and per-window medians of 0.586, 0.591, 0.618 and 0.645 (`2604.24366v2`, p.12). The authors benchmark this against the equity case: Lee-Ready correctly classifies about 81% of Nasdaq trades, so the feed-driven rate is roughly 22 percentage points below the same algorithm's equity accuracy (`2604.24366v2`, p.4).

The propagation is what makes this decisive rather than merely noisy. Among 24 top-100 markets with both inference paths non-empty in one 7-day window, 16 (67%) have feed-inferred and on-chain effective half-spreads of opposite sign, and 15 of 25 (60%) have Kyle's lambda of opposite sign (`2604.24366v2`, p.13). Weighting by on-chain dollar volume raises the effective-spread sign-flip rate from 67% to 86%, because the single largest market in the window accounts for two-thirds of top-100 volume and is one of the markets that flips; restricting to markets with at least 100 inferred trades raises it to 73% (`2604.24366v2`, p.13). In the next non-overlapping 7-day window the unweighted rates fall to 50% and 43%, both with Wilson intervals containing the 50% chance baseline, which the authors read as the flips being window-specific but the methodological conclusion not being (`2604.24366v2`, p.13). The decomposition confirms the diagnosis: on the top-100 stratum the Glosten-Harris decomposition converges on 97 of 100 markets and returns a median effective half-spread of -0.0003 probability points with a median transitory component of 0.00001 and a median adverse-selection component of 0.0, which the authors attribute to the removal of sign errors rather than to a frictionless venue (`2604.24366v2`, p.11). The implication for any engine is blunt: a direction-dependent statistic computed from the public feed is not attenuated, it can have the wrong sign, so the direction must come from an authoritative trade record or the statistic must not be published.

## 4. Settlement manipulation and the non-portability of leverage

The strongest negative result is that an asset-price prediction contract settles on a price its holders can move. Combined monthly volume on Kalshi and Polymarket rose from under $5B in September 2025 to about $24B by April 2026, and the fastest-growing contracts are binary claims on an asset price with horizons shrinking to five minutes (`2606.31675v1`, p.3). Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin up/down contract launched on 12 February 2026, after the fifteen-minute contract on 9 October 2025 and the four-hour contract on 15 October 2025, which gives a staggered natural experiment (`2606.31675v1`, p.5). The Binance mid sits about 2.5 bps from the Chainlink oracle and finishes on the same side of the strike as the resolution about 85% of the time, so a push of a few basis points can carry the resolution (`2606.31675v1`, p.6). In the final ten seconds before each close, trading on Binance spikes: the magnitude of net order flow jumps about 50% above the pre-launch level, and the spike is sharpest where a push is pivotal - in the roughly 6% of cycles whose contract price is near-even just before the close, the near-settlement flow jump is about 3.9 times that in the rest (`2606.31675v1`, p.6). The reversal is the proof it is not information: within ten seconds the price reverts by about a quarter in the near-even cycles and a tenth in the others (`2606.31675v1`, p.6).

The wealth transfer is measured directly because every trade is on-chain. Classifying the top decile of relative final-ten-second flow - about 1,600 cycles - as manipulated, a push against the favoured side flipped the winner 65% of the time in near-even cycles against 41% in ordinary trading; and even when the market gave one side a 90-to-100% chance, a push reversed the outcome 34% of the time against 1% in cycles with no push (`2606.31675v1`, p.7). Just 821 wallets fit the profit-based identification - about one in three hundred of the roughly 243,000 who traded the contract - and they take $8.2M in the pushed cycles while breaking even in the rest; setting aside the market makers, who end each cycle flat, 93% of the loss falls on retail, and those retail accounts are on the losing side in 65% of manipulated cycles against 48% of normal ones (`2606.31675v1`, p.7). The model behind the evidence is a sequential-trading game in the Glosten-Milgrom style with two venues, in which the manipulator's uninformed order pools with the informed trader's and therefore lowers the adverse selection the spot maker faces; that is why the liquidity response is asymmetric - near-even closes add depth on Binance, exactly where the model predicts an uninformed push should lower spot adverse selection, while Polymarket depth falls as binary-market makers face direct exposure to a flipping settlement (`2606.31675v1`, p.4, p.6). The remedy the model and the data support is one lever: a longer horizon dilutes the manipulator's pivotality, which the fifteen-minute and four-hour contracts bear out (`2606.31675v1`, p.5).

The instrument-design lesson is an identity rather than a calibration. For a synthetic long of x units entered at price p0 with collateral x*p0/L, terminal equity when the claim pays zero is -x*p0*(1 - 1/L) for any L > 1, independent of the pre-resolution mark path, because mark-to-market gains and the final jump telescope to the entry-to-terminal payoff (`2605.10400v2`, p.7, Proposition 1); a symmetric result holds for a short carried to a payout of one (`2605.10400v2`, p.7). The boundary-funding trilemma is separate: no funding rule with a finite transfer cap can impose a uniform lower bound proportional to relative basis arbitrarily close to zero or one, while an uncapped relative-basis correction becomes unbounded and violates payer-solvency or participation (`2605.10400v2`, p.8, Proposition 3). The paper is careful that the problem is terminal maturity and market closure rather than the label "perpetual": a leveraged cash-settled dated future has the same adverse terminal identity at expiry (`2605.10400v2`, p.3, p.8). The empirical layer is a fixed-path mechanical replay, not an equilibrium, and it is deliberately negative. On a seven-day Polymarket window (21-27 April 2026, 61,087 stratified candidates, 13,298 passing adequacy gates, 51.1% sports and 77.9% of the three named classes), two structural diagnostics pass - pooled boundary-depth ratio 1.72 against a 1.5 floor, and a median absolute terminal-hour jump of 0.50 against a 0.10 floor - but three of five pre-specified materiality tests fail: dynamic margin and leverage compression raised liquidation incidence at L = 5 by about 6% rather than cutting it 30%, pooled drawdown fell 5.1% against a 50% floor, and a staged halt cut final-hour liquidations 80.4% mechanically while leaving terminal account-shortfall incidence 2.4% worse (`2605.10400v2`, p.6, p.10, p.11, p.12). Descriptive microstructure from the same run fills in the boundary: median half-spread is about 0.0055 in the low boundary region, 0.2700 in the mid region and 0.00525 in the high boundary region, and final-24-hour activity relative to an earlier baseline is 24.6x in crypto, 2.5x in sports, 0.9x in other and 0.68x in politics (`2605.10400v2`, p.16, p.6).

## 5. Kelly, sizing, and the human failure mode

The sizing result that this repo's calculators already anticipate is the lab evidence, and it is the most honest thing in the slice about discipline. In a coin-flipping experiment with a simulated coin biased 60/40 to heads, a $25 stake and a $250 cap, the Kelly fraction is 20% of the account (`1701.01427v1`, p.3). Of 61 finance-trained subjects, only 21% reached the maximum payout, against a simulated 95% for any constant fraction from 10% to 20%, 28% went bust, and the average bet size was 15% of the bankroll with erratic sizing; 18 of the 61 bet their entire bankroll on a single flip, raising ruin probability to 40-60%, and 41 subjects (67%) bet on tails at some point, with tail bets correlated with a preceding run of heads even though 75% of respondents said they believed the 60/40 statement (`1701.01427v1`, p.3, p.4). Only 5 of the 61 had ever heard of the Kelly criterion, two of whom still played badly, and the subjects did not converge: suboptimal betting of similar magnitude persisted throughout the session (`1701.01427v1`, p.3). The uncapped version of the same game has an expected value of $3,220,637 over 300 flips, for which a log-utility player should still only be willing to pay about $10,000, because the high expected value is carried by unlikely large outcomes and the median outcome is far below the mean (`1701.01427v1`, p.5).

The theory the subjects could have used is precise enough to wire into a sizer. Kelly betting is identical to maximizing expected log utility, and the optimal action is to trade q = f*w/p_m shares where f = (p - p_m)/(1 - p_m) (`1201.6655v1`, p.2). Fractional Kelly is not a separate heuristic: f-fractional Kelly is exactly full Kelly with revised belief p' = f*p + (1-f)*p_m, a weighted average of the agent's own belief and the market's, which has a Bayesian justification if the agent has a Beta prior and believes the market has seen t' trials (`1201.6655v1`, p.3, Equation 3). The paper also notes the no-trade tension: in a rational-expectations equilibrium the market price is at least as informative as any agent's belief, so a rational agent would set the fraction to zero; fractional Kelly is instead framed as an experts algorithm over two experts, yourself and the market, with an initial weight of 0.5 implying at most half of all wealth is lost relative to the better of the two (`1201.6655v1`, p.6). Set against the experiment, the practical reading is that the stated confidence and the realized win rate are separate quantities, and a sizer that consumes confidence without grading it is repeating the subjects' error at machine speed.

## 6. Leakage, insider flow, and the labelling problem

The 2026 detection literature agrees that informed trading on prediction markets is quantitatively significant but is almost entirely post-hoc, which makes the labelling problem the binding one for any real-time measure. Two prior studies anchor the scale: a composite statistical screen over about 210,000 wallet-market pairs estimated roughly $143M of aggregate anomalous profit on Polymarket, and an independent on-chain measurement documented about $40M of arbitrage and quasi-arbitrage profit (`2605.00493v2`, p.2, p.7). The ForesightFlow framework proposes an Information Leakage Score (ILS), the fraction of a market's total information move that occurred before the first public mention of the resolution-relevant event, defined as the pre-news drift p(T_news) - p(T_open) over the total move p(T_resolve) - p(T_open), with a 5-cent total-move threshold below which the score is uninformative (`2605.00493v2`, p.13, Definition 1). Its three scope conditions are an edge-effect condition on the opening price, a trivial-resolution threshold, and an anchor-sensitivity condition requiring the recovered value to be robust across anchor specifications, and the score is only interpretable for event-resolved markets (`2605.00493v2`, p.3, p.14).

The pilot is where the finding turns negative, and the negative findings are the most transferable part. On 725 event-resolved markets plus a matched control population, a resolution-anchored proxy for the news timestamp, T_news = T_resolve - 24h, does not separate event-resolved markets from the control, high ILS on high-consensus markets reflects a formula edge effect, and the proxy-based ILS distribution is not robust to the anchor choice (`2605.00493v2`, p.3, p.10). The recovery of article-derived news timestamps via the UMA proposer evidence URL fails by construction for the population where ILS is most interesting, because UMA-resolved markets are dominated by automated data feeds and the admin-resolved markets that include the political, regulatory and corporate contracts have no on-chain evidence URL at all (`2605.00493v2`, p.10). The resolution typology that follows is the structural finding: the classifier splits resolved markets into event-resolved, deadline-resolved ("will event X occur by date Y", which resolves NO when the event fails to occur) and surprise-resolved, and the deadline class has a 100% NO-resolution rate by construction; across 11,200 resolved markets above the $50K volume cutoff, 10.2% are event-resolved, 10.9% deadline-resolved and 78.8% unclassifiable (`2605.00493v2`, p.15, p.16). The documented insider cases - the February 2026 U.S.-Israeli strike, the 2026 U.S.-Iran cluster, the Google "Year in Search" release, the Bitcoin ETF SEC approval - are systematically deadline-resolved, so the original score's scope excludes exactly the population in which informed trading has been documented (`2605.00493v2`, p.2, p.15). A second structural point is that volume is not a proxy for informed activity: the October 2024 Iran-strike market resolved at a peak volume of about $148,000 yet is among the most concretely documented cases, while the 2024 U.S. presidential top-line markets resolved at $2.57B (`2605.00493v2`, p.13). The framework connects the score to the Murphy decomposition of the Brier score and adapts PIN, VPIN and Kyle's lambda to the discrete bounded-price setting, but the paper is explicit that wallet-level features rather than ILS alone are required to identify informed flow (`2605.00493v2`, p.3, p.8).

## Caveats

The evidence base is uneven and this slice is thin. Two papers have no primary market data of their own: `1201.6655v1` is a theory-and-simulation result about Kelly bettors, and `2605.00493v2` is a methodology preprint whose pilot reports negative findings and whose headline empirical evaluation is deferred to a companion paper. `1701.01427v1` is a 61-subject lab experiment, and `1910.08858v2` is a 1990-2017 sample of Las Vegas lines that deliberately excludes several years. The 2026 papers are each single-venue and short-window: `2604.24366v2` is 52 days of one order-book feed plus a cross-sectional snapshot, `2606.31675v1` is one contract on one platform over two months, `2605.10400v2` is one seven-day window and is sports-heavy (51.1% sports of the full sample, 77.9% of the three named classes, on a mechanical fixed-path replay rather than an equilibrium), and `2609.23969v1` is one exchange and one variable, temperature. The microstructure sign-agreement result is window-specific - the effective-spread flip rate fell from 67% to 50% in a second non-overlapping window - so it bounds what a feed-derived measure can claim, not a fixed error rate. All ROI figures are gross of market impact and assume independent one-dollar bets with no compounding and no Kelly sizing; the weather and manipulation results are net of nothing. The slice disagrees with itself on the value of the market price: one paper shows it beating the professional stack, another shows it being pushed at settlement and lagging the fundamental, and both are correct for their venue and horizon. Finally, the corpus is small and thin on journal-anchored work: exactly 1 of the 24 records carries a journal reference, and several of the strongest results are preprints from a single research group, so nothing here should be treated as a law.

## Papers read in depth

- `2609.23969v1` - Prediction Markets Beat the Weather Forecast on Tomorrow's High Temperature (2026). Market-implied ladders beat the public forecast stack by about 10% RMSE; one exchange, one variable, seven U.S. cities.
- `2604.24366v2` - The Anatomy of a Decentralized Prediction Market: Microstructure Evidence from the Polymarket Order Book (2026). Eight stylized facts plus the trade-direction measurement result; limited to 52 days of one venue's feed plus one cross-sectional snapshot.
- `2606.31675v1` - Settlement Manipulation in Prediction Markets (2026). Model and on-chain evidence of settlement manipulation in ultra-short asset-price contracts, with the horizon remedy; one contract, one platform, two months.
- `2605.10400v2` - Resolution-Aware Perpetual Futures on Binary Prediction Markets (2026). Terminal-equity and boundary-funding results plus a negative fixed-path replay; one week, sports-heavy, no equilibrium.
- `2605.00493v2` - ForesightFlow: An Information Leakage Score Framework for Prediction Markets (2026). ILS definition, scope conditions and the deadline-resolution typology; pilot evidence is negative and the full evaluation is pending.
- `1910.08858v2` - Beating the House: Identifying Inefficiencies in Sports Betting Markets (2019). Vig mechanics and a positive-ROI betting model across five sports; hyperparameter-searched on a sample with years excluded by design.
- `1701.01427v1` - Rational Decision-Making Under Uncertainty: Observed Betting Patterns on a Biased Coin (2017). 61-subject experiment on a known 60/40 edge; a lab setting with a small sample and a payout cap.
- `1201.6655v1` - Learning Performance of Prediction Markets with Kelly Bettors (2012). Aggregation, regret and fractional-Kelly theory plus simulations; no market data.
