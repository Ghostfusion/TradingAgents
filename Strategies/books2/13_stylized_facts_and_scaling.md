# Stylized facts, tails and scaling: the heavy tails and the scaling exponents are functionals of the conditioning variable and the estimator, so a lone published exponent is not a measurement of the asset

Date: 2026-10-07. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from a local harvest of arXiv financial-economics records, drawn from a category of 93 matching records spanning 1998-2026, of which 35 carry a journal reference. It is written for the research and analysis layer of this repository - the offline calculators under `tradingagents/strategies/`, the LangChain tools that expose them, the score engines, and the measurement panel - and its purpose is to say which distributional and scaling statistic is worth computing, which one is an artifact of the estimator or the window, and which measurement the repository should stop publishing. The slice runs from the 150-year multi-asset test of the classical stylized facts, through the microstructure reconciliation of predictable order flow with a random-walk price, to the long-memory and multifractality tests that decide whether a measured exponent says anything about the process at all.

## What this covers

The arc runs from which regularities survive a century and a half, through how fragile the measurement of those regularities is, to what mechanism makes the market look random anyway. Every one of the three strands reduces to the same conclusion: the tail and the exponent are not intrinsic properties of the asset. `1310.4538v2` shows that once returns are ordered by an observable stress level, 83 percent of randomly ordered equity windows look non-normal against 17 percent of stress-ordered windows, so most of the fat tail is a mixing artifact. `1601.00903v1` shows that conventional long-memory tests over-reject under multifractality, and with a joint test eleven of twelve currency pairs have no return long memory at all. `1409.3738v2` shows that over the full range a log-normal or stretched exponential beats the power law, which wins only on a truncated tail and then not significantly.

The single most consequential finding for this repository is that its own measurement panel already confirms the sceptical reading on the one exponent it publishes: the `hurst` component of the mean-reversion category has a rank information coefficient of -0.008 and a deflated Sharpe of -1.5 over 25 cross-sections (`docs/scores/MEASUREMENT_FINDINGS.md`), and the repository's own technical score card labels it "WEAK (ic_ir 0.56), unpaired" (`tradingagents/strategies/technical_score.py:264`). The literature here explains why that is the expected outcome, not a plumbing bug: a standalone rescaled-range Hurst number is an estimator-sensitive statistic, not an exploitable memory. What the repository lacks, and what this brief routes back to it, is the pair of readings that would turn the exponent into evidence - a multifractality companion to the fractional-integration parameter, and a conditional distribution read that separates a true heavy tail from a stress-mixture.

## The short answer

1. Predictable order flow and a random-walk price coexist only if price impact is sufficiently concave: with order-sign autocorrelation decaying as a power law of exponent gamma in (0,1), an impact exponent delta at most 1/2 is the necessary and sufficient condition for price diffusion (`2608.00988v1`, p.12).
2. The same minimal model, once an exponential inter-metaorder resting time is added and nothing else, reproduces the inverse-cubic law with tail exponent beta equal to alpha divided by delta, and volatility clustering with a correlation exponent in (0,1) (`2608.00988v1`, pp.14-15).
3. The long memory of order flow is micro-founded and quantified: typically a quarter of traders are order splitters but they issue about 80 percent of market orders, and the macroscopic exponent equals the microscopic metaorder-length exponent minus one (`2301.13505v2`, pp.3-4).
4. The prefactor of that autocorrelation recovers the number of splitting traders but systematically underestimates it, so the estimate is a lower bound (`2301.13505v2`, p.4).
5. Conventional long-memory tests over-reject in the presence of multifractality; a joint test finds no return long memory in eleven of twelve currency pairs, only multifractality (`1601.00903v1`, pp.8, 10).
6. Fat tails are largely a conditioning artifact: 83 percent of randomly ordered equity windows look non-normal at the 95 percent level against 17 percent of stress-ordered windows, and for bonds 44 percent against 22 percent (`1310.4538v2`, p.6).
7. Tail and kurtosis estimates are properties of the window, not the asset: excess kurtosis is 7.49 for the DAX 1959-2018, 19.95 for the Dow 1896-2018 and 32.50 for the S&P 1789-2018, a spread of more than a factor of four (`1912.02684v1`, p.5).
8. Across roughly 150 years and a dozen markets, seven of eleven Cont facts are supported; the return-autocorrelation fact holds for traditional markets but not for cryptocurrencies (`2504.08611v1`, pp.3, 6), and the absolute-return autocorrelation exponent is 0.38 monthly, 0.45 daily, 0.33 minute and 0.28 for minute crypto (`2504.08611v1`, p.7).
9. Long memory is regime-dependent: after the Lehman bankruptcy European corporate-bond Hurst exponents rise (Italy 0.5153 to 0.6468) while equity Hurst exponents fall below one half (Austria 0.5546 to 0.4956) (`1605.06700v1`, pp.8-9).
10. Cryptocurrencies are several stochastic processes, not one: the generalized Hurst exponent spans 0.36 to 0.73 across 84 coins, with large coins behaving like fractional Brownian motion and small coins strongly multifractal (`2003.09720v2`, pp.5-7).
11. A power-law label often loses to a rival over the full range: for Russian interbank network measures the log-normal wins for assets, capital, loan size and exposures and the stretched exponential for degrees, while the truncated power law wins only the tail (about 20 percent of the data) and rarely significantly (`1409.3738v2`, pp.1, 10).
12. A published power-law claim can be an exponential: the frequency of recession durations across seventeen economies fits an exponential with RMS error 4.67 against 11.86 for the power law, and the power law only wins once the one-year recessions (61 percent of the data) are dropped (`cond-mat/0311585v2`, pp.2-3).

## 1. Which stylized facts survive a century and a half

`2504.08611v1` tests the eleven Cont facts on roughly 150 years of monthly data from 1871, about a decade of daily futures and rates, minute futures from 2012 to 2022, and minute Bitcoin and Ethereum from 2017 to 2022 (p.5). It supports seven of the eleven outright, and reports mixed evidence for the absence of log-return autocorrelation and for the leverage effect (p.3). The fact that fails cleanly is the first one: log-return autocorrelation falls inside the 95 percent band within roughly 20 minutes for traditional markets but not for cryptocurrencies, which retain slower decay (p.6). The power-law decay of the absolute-return autocorrelation is fitted at exponents 0.38 for monthly, 0.45 for daily and 0.33 for minute traditional data, against 0.28 for minute crypto (p.7). Aggregational Gaussianity holds under the tests used, but the authors themselves flag that convergence toward normality at longer horizons may reflect observing fewer tail events rather than a property of the process (p.3), which is exactly the caution a research layer should keep.

`1912.02684v1` supplies the counterweight that matters more for a calculator library. Recomputing the same statistics on DAX, Dow Jones and S&P data, it shows the numbers move with the horizon as much as with the asset: excess kurtosis of 7.49, 19.95 and 32.50 on the three long histories (p.5), five-percent Hill estimates of 2.86, 2.62 and 2.38 (p.5), and absolute-return autocorrelation at lag ten of 0.206, 0.322 and 0.335 over a common 2008-2018 window (p.6). The vendor comparison is reassuring - differences of order 1e-2 between Stooq and Yahoo for the same series (p.5) - so the dispersion is horizon and index, not data vendor. The paper also notes at least thirty stylized facts have been documented (p.4) and frames agent-based models by three universal building blocks: agent design, a price-adjustment mechanism, and an environment that couples agents beyond the price (pp.9-11). That taxonomy is a reminder that matching an open-ended fact set is a weak validation criterion.

## 2. Fat tails are a mixture, not a property of the increment

`1310.4538v2` proposes that drift and volatility are functions of an observable market-stress level, proxied by an implied-volatility index, so returns are locally normal but a time series samples many different normal distributions (p.3). On S&P 500 and ten-year bond data from 1988 to 2012 (p.4), a Shapiro-Wilk test flags 83 percent of randomly ordered equity windows as non-normal at the 95 percent level, against 17 percent when the same returns are ordered by stress; for bonds the numbers are 44 percent and 22 percent, with chronological (partial stress) ordering in between at 28 percent and 26 percent (p.6). Ordering by stress also stabilises the parameter estimates that volatility clustering makes look unstable: sample standard deviations rise smoothly with stress instead of wandering (pp.4-5).

The payoff is in the tail, and it is large. The stress-conditional model assigns probability 8e-4 to a one-day decline of at least 9 percent, roughly once in five years, where a naive normal Value-at-Risk gives 2e-14; for the October 1987 fall of 22.9 percent the two estimates are 9e-8 and 4e-82, a gap that moves the event from virtually impossible to extraordinarily unlikely (p.9). The paper also shows the stock-bond correlation is not constant: it is negative when the stock market is stressed but turns positive when stock stress is low even if bond stress is high, so the diversification benefit is conditional (pp.11-12). It proposes replacing the Sharpe ratio with the probability of losing more than the expected accumulated return over a fixed period, reported at 0.0059 for stocks and 0.0002 for bonds (p.14). That is a conditional-distribution argument, and it is the one this repository most needs: `statistical.normality` is currently unconditional.

## 3. Power law, or something that fits the whole range

`1409.3738v2` beats the reflex of labelling every heavy tail a power law. It fits five candidate distributions to twelve network measures of the Russian interbank market from August 1998 to October 2004 (p.1). Every distribution is heavy-tailed and the fat tail averages about 20 percent of the observations (p.10). A truncated power law is the best tail fit in most months and its exponent is consistently near two, ranging from about 1.49 to 2.49 across measures (p.10) - but it is rarely significantly better than the log-normal or the stretched exponential, and over the full range the log-normal wins for assets, capital, loan size and exposures while the stretched exponential wins for the degree distributions (pp.1, 10). The conclusion is robust to weekly, monthly, quarterly and yearly aggregation (p.11). Average clustering is 0.198 and the average shortest path is about 3, both disrupted by the two crises in the sample (p.6).

The same discipline appears in `cond-mat/0311585v2`, a reanalysis of a published result. The frequency of recession durations across seventeen economies from 1870 to 1994 fits an exponential with RMS error 4.67 far better than a power law with RMS error 11.86 (p.2), and the power law becomes the better description only once the one-year recessions - 61 percent of the data - are dropped (p.3). A power-law claim that depends on deleting most of the sample is a statement about where the tail was cut, not about the process. For a repository whose `book_risk.extreme_quantile_var` already fits a Generalized Pareto tail, the missing piece is the rival: nothing in the tree compares a power-law tail against a log-normal or a stretched exponential before the tail is reported.

## 4. Long memory is fragile, and the estimator decides the answer

`1601.00903v1` is the paper that should govern every Hurst number this repository publishes. It shows, by Monte Carlo, that conventional tests of long memory over-reject the null of no long memory when multifractality is present (pp.3, 8), and it builds a joint test that estimates the long-memory and multifractality parameters together. Applied to 5,000 daily log returns for twelve currency pairs against the US dollar from 1993 to 2012 (p.3), the long-memory test fails to reject H equal to one half for all twelve, while the multifractality test rejects the unifractal null for eleven of the twelve, the exception being the GB pound (p.10). Eleven of the twelve return series are, in other words, best described by compounding an i.i.d.-normal series with a multifractal time-deformation process, with no long memory in returns at all.

That is the single most important correction this brief offers a repository that already publishes a fractional-integration parameter. `long_memory.memory_parameter` computes the GPH and local-Whittle memory parameter `d` (via `long_memory.py`), and `mean_reversion.hurst_exponent` computes a rescaled-range Hurst; neither has a multifractality companion. The paper's own machinery - the generalized Hurst exponent `h(q)` of the partition function and the multifractality parameter lambda, with the test statistics calibrated by simulation - is directly portable, and the share of the repository's own documentation that already flags the `hurst` component as weak is evidence that the naive read is not earning its place.

## 5. Hurst is a moving target, and crypto is many targets

`1605.06700v1` measures rolling Hurst exponents for corporate-bond and equity indices in six EU countries from 1999 to 2015, with a 500-point sliding window (p.6). Corporate-bond returns are not thin-tailed: excess kurtosis reaches 551.7 for Belgium, 162.5 for Finland and 120.7 for Italy (p.7). Over the whole period the mean bond Hurst exponent is 0.5573 for Italy, 0.5462 for Austria and 0.4652 for Luxembourg (p.8), generally above one half. Splitting at the Lehman bankruptcy, bond memory increases sharply - Italy from 0.5153 to 0.6468, France from 0.5342 to 0.5991 - while equity memory falls below one half everywhere, Austria from 0.5546 to 0.4956 (p.9). The authors attribute this to a flight to quality and liquidity that concentrates trading in fewer instruments during the crisis. A statistic that flips its interpretation across a regime boundary cannot be a fixed property of the instrument.

`1708.04532v1` finds the same time-variation in Bitcoin. Daily Bitcoin returns from 2011 to 2017 have a standard deviation of 6.24 percent against 0.57 percent for the euro and the pound, with negative skew and excess kurtosis of 25.57 (p.8). Detrended fluctuation analysis with a sliding 500-point window shows the Hurst exponent persistently above 0.5 until about 2014 and then stabilising near 0.5, while the euro and pound stay inside 0.45-0.55 throughout (pp.8-9); the authors cannot tie the change to any fundamental driver. At intraday sampling the kurtosis remains extreme and falls only slowly, from 107.5 at five-hour sampling to 53.0 at twelve-hour sampling (pp.10-11), and the Hurst profile is essentially the same at every sampling interval (p.11). A crypto calibration can therefore go wrong on a multi-year timescale rather than decaying gracefully.

`2003.09720v2` generalises this to 84 cryptocurrencies over 790 daily observations from January 2018 to March 2020 (p.5). The market is not one process. The generalized Hurst exponent at order one has mean 0.5150 and spans 0.3564 to 0.7333, and at order two has mean 0.4471 (p.5); the function `qH(q)` is nonlinear for coins in the third and fourth volume quartiles, so Brownian, fractional-Brownian and Levy models are inadequate there, while first-quartile coins follow roughly unifractal processes consistent with fractional Brownian motion (p.7). Shuffling the series reduces the multifractality measures but does not remove them, and kurtosis is associated with the residual level, strongly for the smallest coins (pp.7-9). One model does not fit all; the repository has no crypto strategy module and reads crypto price only through a dataflow, so this is a calibration rule rather than a new engine.

## 6. Predictable order flow, unpredictable price

`2301.13505v2` gives the microscopic measurement that grounds the whole paradox. Using nine years of Tokyo Stock Exchange data with account identifiers, from January 2012 to December 2020 (p.2), it clusters traders into order-splitting and random types and finds that typically a quarter of all traders are splitters but that they issue about 80 percent of all market orders (p.3). The metaorder-length complementary CDF obeys a power law whose exponent is typically between one and two - about 1.62 for Toyota in 2020 - and the measured macroscopic sign-autocorrelation exponent matches the prediction gamma equals alpha minus one across markets (pp.3-4). The prefactor carries information too: an estimator built from it recovers the number of splitting traders, though only as a lower bound (p.4). The authors argue the pair of microscopic parameters measures a latent illiquidity that spread, depth and average impact do not capture.

`2608.00988v1` supplies the reconciliation, and it is the cleanest result in the slice. If price impact has a linear permanent component, price dynamics under long-range correlated order flow are superdiffusive, contradicting the empirically observed Brownian motion; the paper proves that an impact exponent delta at most 1/2 is the necessary and sufficient condition for diffusion for any order-sign exponent gamma in (0,1) (p.12), and that the return autocorrelation then has an integrable exponent theta equal to alpha minus 2 delta plus 1 (p.13). Adding only an exponential inter-metaorder resting time yields the inverse-cubic law with beta equal to alpha divided by delta and a numerically observed volatility clustering with exponent in (0,1) (pp.14-15). The square-root impact law is what suppresses the impact of large metaorders and keeps the market random. The practical reading for a research layer is negative and useful: order-flow predictability is not, by itself, return predictability, and any signal that converts an order-flow long-memory estimate into a return forecast is assuming away the concavity that the data supports.

## 7. Sizing under power-law dynamics, and what the repository already gets right

`2609.22612v1` is the one paper in the slice that lands directly on book-level sizing. For an asset whose expected excess return and variance follow power laws in system age, the continuous-time log-optimal Kelly fraction scales as age raised to the power q minus p and is independent of age exactly when the two decay exponents match (p.4). For a power-law price baseline P of the form A t^alpha and variance decaying as t to the minus 2 gamma, the fraction scales as alpha over sigma-squared-zero times t to the 2 gamma minus 1, so exact temporal invariance requires gamma equal one half (p.4). Using daily Bitcoin prices over 1 to 12 year rolling windows, the fitted volatility exponent is 0.4576 at one year and 0.5240 at four years, with an arithmetic mean of 0.53 and a cross-window standard deviation of about 0.03 over the four-to-nine-year range (pp.5-6); the implied four-year Kelly fraction is about 0.74 (p.7). The estimates come from overlapping windows and the paper is explicit that the spread is a model-sensitivity measure, not a confidence interval. The finding that matters for `risk_sizing` is that a fixed fraction is age-invariant only under a specific exponent match, which is a testable condition rather than a free assumption.

Against all of this, the repository already runs three of the right machines, and the brief should say so rather than invent work. `book_risk.var_coverage_test` implements the Kupiec and Christoffersen joint conditional-coverage tests with closed-form chi-square survival functions, which is exactly the discipline a VaR needs. `book_risk.extreme_quantile_var` fits a Generalized Pareto tail for an extreme-quantile VaR and Expected Shortfall, so EVT is present. `book_risk.drawdown_envelope` computes four drawdown expectations and rescales the depth measures by T to the power H minus one half when a Hurst estimate exists, falling back to the square-root-of-time convention and labelling the record `hurst: "assumed"` when it does not - which is the corrected scaling the drawdown literature requires. The gaps the slice exposes are narrower: no multifractality companion to the memory parameter, no conditional normality, no distribution horse-race, and a rescaled-range Hurst where a detrended-fluctuation read is the literature's preferred estimator.

## Caveats

The read set is mixed in kind and several members cannot support a trading conclusion. `2608.00988v1` and `2301.13505v2` rest on one exchange, the Tokyo Stock Exchange, and the first is a proof of diffusion rather than an out-of-sample test; the volatility-clustering result there is numerical, not proved. `1310.4538v2` covers only US equity and Treasury data from 1988 to 2012 and depends on an implied-volatility stress proxy that does not exist for every asset class. `1409.3738v2` studies a bilateral interbank exposure network, an institutional structure rather than a tradable instrument, and `cond-mat/0311585v2` reanalyses a single small frequency table for macro recession durations. `1605.06700v1` and `2003.09720v2` cover six EU indices and 84 pre-2020 daily crypto series respectively. `2609.22612v1` is an in-sample calibration on one asset with no out-of-sample test, and its own author states that the cross-window spread from overlapping observations is a sensitivity measure rather than a confidence interval. `2504.08611v1` and `1912.02684v1` are partly reviews, so their value is the dispersion of the statistics, not any single number. No paper here models transaction costs, and none of the tail measurements is a tradable signal on its own; a conditional distribution and a joint long-memory test are statements about the data-generating process, and the step from either to a position is not supported by this slice.

## Papers read in depth

- `2504.08611v1` - International Financial Markets Through 150 Years: Evaluating Stylized Facts (2025). Tests eleven classical facts over 150 years and a dozen markets; supports seven of eleven and finds crypto breaks the return-autocorrelation fact. Its own caveat is that aggregational Gaussianity may be a sampling artifact.
- `1912.02684v1` - Stylized Facts and Agent-Based Modeling (2019). Recomputes kurtosis, Hill and autocorrelation for DAX, Dow and S&P and shows extreme horizon and index sensitivity, plus data-vendor agreement to 1e-2. A review, so its value is the dispersion, not any single number.
- `1310.4538v2` - The Origin of Fat Tails (2013). Attributes fat tails to stress-dependent parameters and quantifies the tail-probability gap against a normal; US equity and bond data only, and the conditioning depends on an implied-volatility proxy.
- `2608.00988v1` - Exactly solvable model for the diffusive price-dynamics paradox under long-range correlated market-order flow (2026). Proves square-root impact sufficient for diffusion and reproduces the inverse-cubic law and volatility clustering; a model result with no out-of-sample test and a single exchange's empirical backing.
- `2301.13505v2` - Can we infer microscopic financial information from the long memory in market-order flow? (2023). First quantitative validation of the Lillo-Mike-Farmer prediction gamma equals alpha minus one on nine years of TSE account-level data; single exchange, and the splitter-count estimator is only a lower bound.
- `1601.00903v1` - Long memory and multifractality: A joint test (2016). Shows conventional long-memory tests over-reject under multifractality, then finds no return long memory in eleven of twelve currency pairs; the joint-test machinery is the reusable part, and it is Monte-Carlo calibrated.
- `1409.3738v2` - Beyond the Power Law: Uncovering Stylized Facts in Interbank Networks (2014). A distribution horse race on the Russian interbank network; log-normal and stretched exponential beat the power law over the full range. An institutional network, not an instrument.
- `cond-mat/0311585v2` - The duration of recessions follows an exponential not a power law (2003). Refits a published power law and shows an exponential wins until most of the sample is discarded. Macro, from a single small frequency table.
- `1605.06700v1` - The impact of the financial crisis on the long-range memory of European corporate bond and stock markets (2016). Rolling Hurst exponents show bond memory rising and equity memory falling after 2008; six EU indices only, and the effect is attributed to a flight to quality.
- `1708.04532v1` - Some stylized facts of the Bitcoin market (2017). Rolling Hurst exponents for Bitcoin, the euro and the pound show Bitcoin's persistence fading to about 0.5 by 2014; a short and dated sample, and the authors cannot explain the change.
- `2003.09720v2` - One model is not enough: heterogeneity in cryptocurrencies' multifractal profiles (2020). Across 84 coins, large ones look fractional-Brownian and small ones multifractal, with kurtosis associated with the residual; pre-2020 daily data only, and the sample is by volume.
- `2609.22612v1` - Asymptotic Invariance of Kelly Allocation Under Power-Law Asset Dynamics: Evidence from Bitcoin (2026). Derives the age-invariance condition gamma equal to one half and estimates 0.53 +/- 0.03 for Bitcoin; in-sample, one asset, and the scaling hypothesis behind it is explicitly a set of assumptions.
